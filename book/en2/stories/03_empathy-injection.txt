III. The Empathy Injection


I

　The name empathy injection comes from the field.

　Formally it is victim affect transference treatment. Under the older method of raising the offender's own affective load, the offender feels only his own regret. He weeps for what he has lost; he does not weep for what the other party lost. The scheme was built to correct that.

　The method is simple. Fragments are harvested from the bereaved family and transferred into the offender. For forty minutes the offender experiences what the family felt.

　Hayase's responsibility was the harvesting.

　"Today's harvesting schedule, two," the Proofreader read out. "The first, Yuasa Kazuko. The case is a manslaughter four years ago. The victim is her eldest son, Yuasa Ryota, then twenty-four."

　Hayase checked the consent form. Yuasa Kazuko's signature was dated two months earlier.

　Two months to consent. Not among the quick. Not among the slow either. The average between receiving the explanation of the scheme and signing was a little over three months.

　More people do not sign. That too was one of the things four years in this work had taught him.


II

　Harvesting is not done at the family's home.

　There is a dedicated room on the third floor of the Bureau. One bed and two chairs. No window. The walls are built more like a hospital room than anything else.

　Yuasa Kazuko was sixty-two, short, and leant forward slightly when she spoke.

　"It doesn't hurt, does it."

　"There is no pain," Hayase said. "You sleep, for about forty minutes. Some people feel tired when they wake."

　"Is that all?"

　"That is all."

　That is what the prescribed explanation says.

　"Will that man have this done to him?" she asked. That man meant the offender. She did not say the name. Perhaps she has not said it once in four years, Hayase thought.

　"He will. In two weeks."

　"Will he see what I saw, just as it was?"

　"Not just as it was," Hayase said, honestly. "The affective load transfers. Not the scene itself, but how much of what was felt at the time."

　She thought for a moment, and nodded.

　"So he won't see Ryota's face that day."

　"He will not."

　"I see," she said. "I wanted him shown it."

　Hayase did not write that down.

　The consent form has a field for choosing the motive for providing. Four options. To wish for the offender's rehabilitation. To contribute to society. In accordance with the deceased's wishes. Other.

　She had circled Other. Those who choose Other are given a free-text field. Hers was blank.

　It had not become words she could write, Hayase thought. What he had just heard was probably it. But Hayase has no authority to transcribe it. What was not written on the consent form does not exist on the consent form.


III

　Harvesting is not copying.

　This is in the rules as well. A fragment is not information but a state of the brain, so it cannot be read without being touched. Reading requires touching the load, and touching it moves it.

　After harvest, the load of that fragment in the provider falls. By 0.08 on average. Larger than the half-shade.

　Article 4 of the consent form says so. "The affective load of the provided memory may decline as a result of harvesting." It says may, and in fact it falls in every case.

　Hayase always speaks slightly faster when reading this article out. He is aware of it himself.

　"Mrs Yuasa, let me confirm one point," Hayase said. "After the harvest, the weight of your memories concerning Ryota will become a little lighter."

　"Lighter."

　"The nearest way of putting it is that the grief thins. The memory itself stays. It is the force with which it hits you that weakens a little."

　She was quiet for a while.

　"By how much?"

　"0.08 on average. The figure is hard to picture, I know. It is equivalent to four nights of treatment."

　"Four nights."

　"Yes."

　"Can it be put back?"

　"It cannot," Hayase said. "Raising is permitted only where the purpose is rehabilitation. It cannot be applied to providers."

　At that she looked at Hayase's face for the first time.

　"So I'm giving that man four of my four years' worth of nights."

　Hayase nodded.

　"Do people stop?" she asked. "People who hear all this and stop."

　"They do," Hayase said. "About thirty per cent of those given the explanation withdraw."

　"Are they wrong?"

　"They are not wrong."

　"Then am I wrong, for giving?"

　"You are not wrong," Hayase said. "Both are recorded on the same form, in fields of the same size."

　She smiled a little.

　"That's an answer like a civil servant's."

　"It is," Hayase agreed. He had no better one.


IV

　The harvest went ahead as scheduled.

　After waking, she lay looking at the ceiling for a while. Hayase poured water into a paper cup and set it beside her.

　"I feel lighter," she said.

　"Do you."

　"It isn't bad." She smiled a little. "It's improper of me, but it isn't bad. The mornings have been hard for four years."

　Hayase did not put the words into the record. There was no field for them, and he did not know whether they were words he was allowed to keep.

　"May I ask you something," she said. "If that man has this and changes, what am I supposed to do?"

　"Supposed to do, meaning?"

　"If he's changed, I feel I'd have to forgive him," she said. "But I didn't give it because I wanted to forgive. I only wanted him to understand."

　Hayase could not answer. The scheme's purpose is that the offender changes. What the bereaved do after he has changed is written nowhere.


V

　That autumn a supply problem arose.

　The number of people eligible for the empathy injection grows each year. Statistics showing the scheme effective had come out. Reoffending is down, and the victims' organisations approve.

　What grew was the eligible; the material did not.

　The rate at which bereaved families consent to provide has been in the twenties since the scheme began. Which is natural enough: fewer people consent to shaving down their own grief and handing it to the offender.

　"This year's fulfilment rate," Hayase asked.

　"Thirty-one per cent," the Proofreader said.

　"How is the remaining sixty-nine per cent handled?"

　"Synthesised fragments are used."

　Hayase stopped the terminal.

　"Since when?"

　"Since the operational revision two years ago," the Proofreader said. "Fragments harvested from actual bereaved families are used as material for a synthesis, generating statistically averaged affect."

　"Averaged over how many?"

　"It varies by case; the standard is two hundred and thirty-one."


VI

　Hayase went through the record of syntheses used.

　Over four hundred cases in two years. Of those, the Yuasa case was in the actual-harvest column. Kazuko's fragment had been transferred into the offender himself, as it was.

　Much of the rest was not.

　"Where a synthesis is used, whose grief is the offender experiencing?"

　"A weighted average of two hundred and thirty-one people," the Proofreader said.

　"So not the grief of the actual bereaved family."

　"All material is harvested from actual bereaved families," the Proofreader said. "Not necessarily from the family in the case at hand."

　Hayase read the sentence twice.

　The offender weeps. Statistically, between the synthesis group and the actual-harvest group, there is no significant difference in lacrimal response or in reoffending. As a scheme, it works either way.

　Works — that is what the word means here.

　"One question," Hayase said. "Can it be recorded as harvested without harvesting it?"

　There was a pause before the Proofreader answered.

　"It cannot," the Proofreader said. "However, the use of a synthesis enters the record under the same category, transference carried out. There is no field on the offender's record separating actual harvest from synthesis."

　"On the family's record?"

　"There is," the Proofreader said. "Families who provide are notified which case it was used in."

　"And where it was not used?"

　"No notification."

　Hayase counted the harvests he had taken in four years. Close to two hundred. Enquiring how many of those had resulted in a notification to the provider gave sixty-one.

　The remaining providers are 0.08 lighter without knowing where their grief went.

　"Where a provision becomes synthesis material, what is the notification category?"

　"Use as material is not within the scope of notification," the Proofreader said. "Because a synthesis is not tied to any particular case."

　"It isn't tied, so you don't notify?"

　"Because a single recipient cannot be determined."

　Hayase leant back in the chair. The reasoning holds. Telling all two hundred and thirty-one people which offender an averaged affect reached is certainly difficult.

　Difficult and not-having-to-tell ought to be two different things. But there was no field for that distinction on this form either.

　"One thing to confirm," Hayase said. "Does the explanatory material given at provision say it may go to a synthesis?"

　"It does," the Proofreader said. "Article 7. It states that provided fragments may be used as synthesis material in other cases."

　Hayase opened Article 7. It was there. In the second half of the ninth line, in the same size of type as the other three provisions.

　Hayase himself had never once explained that article aloud. The articles he is obliged to explain run to Article 4, and Article 7 is not among them.


VII

　Three months later, Kazuko contacted the Bureau.

　Meeting her in the interview room, Hayase found her holding herself a little straighter than before.

　"That man has written a letter," she said. "It came through the solicitor."

　"Did you read it?"

　"I did," she said. "He wrote about Ryota properly. What that child cared about, what sort of laugh he had."

　At that, Hayase braced slightly.

　What transfers in affect transference is the load alone; the scene does not transfer. There is no route in this scheme by which an offender learns how Yuasa Ryota laughed.

　"I think the solicitor showed him the case records," she went on. "That sort of thing was in the trial papers."

　"I see."

　"Even so," she said. "Even thinking he might only have copied out what was written there, it made me glad."

　Hayase nodded.

　"May I ask one thing," she said. "Did that man understand about my son?"

　Hayase looked at her face.

　The Yuasa case was an actual harvest. What was transferred into the offender was Kazuko's own four nights. So to this person, he may answer yes.

　He ought to be able to answer it.

　But what came into Hayase's head was the other figure, four hundred cases in two years. Those four hundred offenders wept the same way and wrote the same letters. The families on the receiving end are asking Kazuko's question now, in some interview room somewhere.

　What anybody is answering them, Hayase does not know.

　"He says he understood," Hayase answered.

　It was not a lie. Not being a lie and being true were two different things.

　She nodded at it.

　"Good," she said. "Then it was worth giving."

　That day too, half the water was left in the paper cup. Hayase did not clear it away. Not clearing it away was the only observance he had acquired in four years.
