# English Edition — Translation Glossary and Style Guide

Volume I of the series, translated for a separate KDP listing.

This document is the authority on terminology. Settle a term here before using it
in a story. Twelve linked stories share one invented vocabulary; if the same
Japanese term comes out three different ways across the book, the world stops
holding together. Where the Japanese text repeats a phrase on purpose, the
English must repeat it too, word for word.

Notes on the harder ones.

**胎夢 → Foundations.** The Japanese word is the classical one for a dream
that foretells a child. English has nothing for it that is not either clinical
or twee. The story's own word is 土台, foundation — "the foundation, not the
character itself" — and it carries the theme, so the title takes it.

**夢の犯罪 → Bodily Harm.** The charge in the story is 傷害. "Bodily Harm" is
the legal term and holds the story's irony, which is that no body is harmed.

**消えた現実 → The Register.** A literal "The Vanished Reality" oversells the
ending. The story turns on one line restored to a public register, and the flat
bureaucratic noun sits with "The Bequest" and "The Sentence" in Volume I.

**ＡＩは夢を見るか → Does the Machine Dream?** The pivotal line is Jinbo's
question to the Proofreader, so the title has to echo it. "AI" is avoided in
the English because the book never uses the term in its own voice; the machine
is the Proofreader throughout.

Notes on the harder ones.

**夢裁判 → The Sentence.** "Dream Trial" is flat and slightly comic in English.
"The Sentence" carries both the judicial meaning and the sense of something
imposed for a fixed term, which is what the story is about: a load raised to
〇・八一 and left there.

**涙の代筆 → Grief by Proxy.** The Japanese is "ghostwriting tears". English has
no equivalent that is not either precious or absurd. "Grief by Proxy" keeps the
bureaucratic coldness, which matters more than the image.

**夢の遺言 → The Bequest.** Shorter than "The Dream Bequest" and lets the title
sit beside "Inheritance" without the two reading as a pair of near-duplicates.

### Two lines that vary by section

These two are repeated with the verb or noun swapped for the section's own work.
The frame stays word for word; only the slot changes.

**The cornered technician's query.** Frame: "Can it be recorded as ___ without
___ it?" The Editing Room and Scheduling sections use the base form, "reduced /
reducing". Later sections put their own verb in and say so explicitly, so the
reader sees it is the same question.

| Section | English |
|---|---|
| Editing Room, Scheduling | "Can it be recorded as reduced without reducing it?" |
| Bequest | "Can it be recorded as made by the person without his having made it." |
| Translation | "Can it be recorded as translated without translating it." |
| Consent Dream | "Can it be recorded as reconciled without reconciling it?" |
| Correctional facility | "Can it be recorded as raised without raising it?" |
| Long Sleep wing | "Can it be recorded as consented to without her having consented?" |
| Statistics Bureau | "Can it be recorded as measured without measuring it?" |

**The field for the person himself.** Frame: "The field for the person himself
does not contain ___." The Bequest story establishes the cumulative form, "contains
neither averages nor interpretations"; each later section adds its own absence and
the narration notes that the technician has heard the construction before.

| Section | Missing from the field |
|---|---|
| Bequest | averages, then interpretations |
| Translation | intent, then confidence |
| Consent Dream | interpretations, then the will to meet halfway |

## 4. Numbers

The Japanese uses 漢数字 throughout because the book is set vertically. English
is set horizontally and takes the opposite convention.

- Affective loads: decimals. 〇・〇五 → 0.05. Always two decimal places unless
  the Japanese gives three (〇・〇〇四 → 0.004).
- Counts and ages: words up to ten, numerals above. "seven nights", "13 years old",
  "231 people", "2,407 cases".
- Section numbers: Roman numerals, centred, matching the Japanese 一二三.

## 5. Style

The Japanese prose is dry on purpose. Carry that over rather than smoothing it.

1. **Short sentences. Do not join them with semicolons.** The Japanese ends
   sentences where English would be tempted to run on. Let it end.
2. **Report actions, not feelings.** The text does not say someone is crying;
   it says the technician pours water into a paper cup. Keep that discipline.
   Never add an emotional gloss the Japanese withholds.
3. **Bureaucratic register for institutional speech.** Forms, clauses, sub-clauses,
   the passive voice. This is where the book's satire lives.
4. **The Proofreader's voice is flat.** No contractions. No hedging. It states,
   or it says there is no record. Its pauses are described, never dramatised.
5. **Keep the paragraph breaks.** The Japanese isolates single sentences as
   paragraphs for weight. English typography handles this fine.
6. **British spelling** throughout (levelling, synchronisation, recognised).
   Chosen for consistency with "half-shade" and the institutional register.
7. **Do not explain.** Where the Japanese leaves a thing unstated, leave it.
   The temptation in translation is to clarify; clarifying is the one thing
   this book must not do.

## 6. What must not appear

The Hoshi Shinichi Award entry, 『境界線、未編集』, stays unpublished and
Japanese-only. The English edition is a translation of the twelve book stories
only. It must not contain:

- any translation of the entry
- the entry's proper nouns (三崎 / Misaki, 深瀬 / Fukase)
- the entry's distinctive lines, including the two reworded for the Japanese
  edition (see `book/01_世界観設定.md`)

Translating from the already-corrected Japanese book text keeps this true by
construction. Do not translate from an earlier draft.
