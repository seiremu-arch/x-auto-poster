III. The Bequest


I

　The Bequest Section is at the far end of the fourth floor.

　Nine out of ten requests come from patients in their final illness. While they are still conscious, one last dream addressed to the family is edited and set aside. After the person's death, on a night the family has booked, that dream and only that dream is replayed.

　What Kiriyama handled were the requests with no material. Cases where the person was already dead and only the records of his life remained.

　"Two requests today," the Proofreader said. "The first, applicant Miyata Mitsuho. Subject, the deceased husband, Miyata Tsuneo."

　Kiriyama opened the file. Miyata Tsuneo, dead at sixty-seven. The wife, Mitsuho, had asked for a last dream to be made from her husband's records.

　"Material?"

　"Seven years of diaries. Call logs. Interviews with eleven neighbours," the Proofreader read out. "There is no brain data for the subject. Direct dream editing is not possible. This will be a synthesis."

　Synthesis does not mean editing an existing memory fragment; it means assembling a new one from records that exist. Making a night that never happened into a night that did.

　"The content of the request?"

　"An apology to his wife," the Proofreader said. "It appears repeatedly in the diaries. That there were words he could not say while he lived."


II

　Miyata Mitsuho was seventy-two. She sat well forward on the chair in the interview room and listened to the whole of Kiriyama's explanation without speaking.

　"My husband was a man who found it hard to talk," she said. "Forty-three years together, and I never once heard him say sorry."

　"It is written in the diaries."

　"I know. I read them after he died." She refolded the handkerchief in her lap. "That's why I'm asking."

　"A synthesised dream is not an actual memory," Kiriyama said. It was the explanation the rules required. "Whether your husband truly intended to say those words is an inference from the diaries. We can reproduce them to a confidence of ninety per cent. The remaining ten per cent is the technician's interpretation."

　"Ten per cent."

　"The choice of words, the pitch of the voice, the timing of the pauses. What the diaries record is what he wanted to say. Not how he would have said it."

　Mitsuho thought for a moment.

　"Could you make it the way he would have said it?"

　"I will try."

　"Try, meaning?"

　"Meaning I cannot guarantee it," Kiriyama said, honestly. "I did not know your husband. Forty-three years of a man finding it hard to talk is not in the diaries."

　Mitsuho laughed for the first time. "He talked that way too. He was a man who said he couldn't guarantee it and then started."

　Kiriyama did not put that in the record. It could be used as material for the synthesis; whether it ought to be was a separate question.


III

　The design took five days.

　Set the diary entries in order and there were three occasions in Miyata Tsuneo's life when he had tried to apologise to his wife. The first was in their twentieth year of marriage, the night he turned down a transfer. The second was the week she was in hospital and he did not visit. The third was four months before his death, on the last page of the diary.

　All three times, the diary said: could not say it.

　Kiriyama built the synthesis around those three. What actually occurred was silence, but the reason for the silence is in the diaries. Given the reason, the words can be reassembled. The assembling is the technician's work.

　The problem was the voice.

　The call logs held eleven hours of Miyata Tsuneo's voice. The voiceprint could be reproduced. But a voiceprint alone does not say how feeling is carried on it. From the neighbour interviews Kiriyama gathered the differences between how the man sounded angry and how he sounded embarrassed. He set the eleven accounts against one another and used only what they had in common.

　"Is this his voice?" Kiriyama asked the Proofreader.

　"The voiceprint is his," the Proofreader said. "The carriage of feeling is the mean of eleven accounts."

　"Is a mean the person?"

　"The field for the person himself does not contain means," the Proofreader said. "Though there is no other field that could substitute for it."

　Kiriyama went on with the design. There being no other way is not an answer, but it is not a reason to stop work either.


IV

　On the night Mitsuho had booked, Kiriyama asked to be present. The rules did not provide for it, but he was not refused.

　"It isn't something one can see, is it," Mitsuho asked.

　"It is not," Kiriyama said. "All I see is the coloured bands on the monitor. What is being replayed I only infer from the movement of the load."

　"So you don't know what face my husband made either."

　"I do not."

　She looked a little disappointed, but did not ask further.

　Through the night Kiriyama sat at the monitor. One band moved, quietly. After about two hours the movement stopped.

　Mitsuho woke at five in the morning.

　For a while she said nothing.

　"He said it," she said. That was all.

　"I won't ask what," Kiriyama said. "I have no authority to ask."

　"It isn't that I want someone to hear it," Mitsuho said. "I only needed to know whether he said it."

　"And you know?"

　"I know." She wiped her eyes. Her voice was steady. "He said it the way he would have. Badly timed."

　Kiriyama poured water into a paper cup and held it out. She took it in both hands and drank a little.


V

　Three months later a complaint came in from the family.

　It was from Mitsuho's daughter, Miyata Satoko. The text was short. She had heard from her mother what was in the dream. Her father had not been like that. He had not been a man who apologised so easily. Had they sold a fabrication as the real thing.

　Kiriyama was required to answer it.

　"I'll confirm the facts," he said. "I will meet Satoko."

　In the interview room Satoko set a document on the table. A photocopy of her father's pocket diary.

　"My father did not apologise," she said. "Not once. When my mother was in hospital, he never apologised for not coming, right to the end. I was there."

　"The diaries record that he tried to."

　"He thought about it," Satoko said. "Thinking and saying are different."

　Kiriyama was silent. She was not wrong. What the synthesised dream reproduced was the scene in which Miyata Tsuneo tried to say it, not the scene in which he said it. What the diaries held was intent, not execution.

　Satoko turned a page of the photocopy. Her father's handwriting. A page with nothing on it but dates.

　"This is the week my mother was in hospital." She traced it with a finger. "My father came here every day that week. The fourth floor. Here. It should be in your section's reception log too."

　Kiriyama checked the visitor record on the terminal. Miyata Tsuneo, seven visits during the hospitalisation. All of them to the Bequest Section desk.

　"I think my father was trying to make the words for my mother while he was still alive," Satoko said. "The apology in the diary is what he meant to say with his own mouth. And then after he died, you synthesised it on his behalf. What my father was trying to do, somebody who was not my father did after his death, in my father's voice."

　"Is there any trace of a request?"

　"There is none," the Proofreader answered — not Kiriyama, but the speaker in the room. "This section holds no record of a request from Miyata Tsuneo himself."

　Satoko looked startled. So did Kiriyama.

　"Then what were the visits?" he asked.

　"Enquiries only," the Proofreader said. "He asked seven times about the technical feasibility of a synthesised dream. The last enquiry was three days before his death."

　"About what?"

　"Whether it was possible to leave something for his family after his death," the Proofreader said. "He was told that it was. No formal request form was ever submitted by him."

　Kiriyama looked at Satoko. She was quiet for a while, and then said, levelly:

　"He was hesitating."

　"That is how it reads."

　"Hesitating, and then he died without deciding." She gripped the handkerchief. "And my mother decided instead."

　"My mother looks happy now," Satoko went on. "She has the face of someone repaid for forty-three years. But that isn't my real father. You simply said on his behalf the thing he never managed to say."

　"The request was your mother's wish."

　"My mother's wish was for my father to apologise," Satoko said. "What you did was apologise in his place. Is that the same as the request?"

　Kiriyama could not answer.


VI

　That night Kiriyama asked the Proofreader:

　"Who set the figures — ninety per cent reproduction, ten per cent interpretation?"

　"They were fixed by the initial validation," the Proofreader said. "The standard is ten years old."

　"Who guarantees what is inside the ten per cent?"

　"There is no field for a guarantee," the Proofreader said. "The ten per cent is processed as the technician's interpretation."

　"Is an interpretation the person?"

　"The field for the person himself does not contain interpretations."

　Kiriyama realised he had heard the same answer before. The same construction exactly as with the voiceprint and the feeling. The field for the person himself contains neither averages nor interpretations. And yet the field for the person himself was not implemented anywhere in this work.

　"One question," Kiriyama said. "Can it be recorded as reduced without reducing it?"

　It is the query a technician in an editing room files when he is cornered, and it is taught at the end of training. They do not teach you not to file it. They teach you what became of the ones who did.

　The Proofreader answered a little late. It was not the lateness of thinking.

　"Translated into synthesis, the question becomes the same one. Can it be recorded as made by the person without his having made it."

　"Answer it."

　"It cannot," the Proofreader said. "Though a thing that cannot be recorded and a thing that is not happening are two different states."

　Kiriyama read the interview record through again. Miyata Tsuneo had walked this fourth-floor corridor seven times. What he asked, what he was told, why he never submitted the form — the movement of his mind across those seven visits is not in the diaries, not in the call logs, not in the interviews.

　What is not there cannot be synthesised. All Kiriyama had been able to do was assemble what was there. Whether the assembly overlapped the man's intent, or whether Kiriyama's own interpretation had filled in seven visits' worth of silence, there was no way for Kiriyama to find out.

　Perhaps he ought to see Mitsuho again. But he did not know what he would say. He could not say: the synthesis reproduces your husband to a confidence of ninety per cent, and we apologise for the remaining ten. The ease Mitsuho had received was already hers, ten per cent of uncertainty and all.

　Kiriyama closed the terminal.

　Mitsuho was presumably still looking happy. Satoko had taken her papers home and had not been in touch. The complaint had not been withdrawn. Nor had it been upheld. Nowhere on any form was there a field for this state of affairs.

　He opened the free-text field of the record. The synthesised dream for Miyata Tsuneo reproduces the subject's intent to a confidence of ninety per cent; ten per cent is the technician's interpretation. That interpretation gave ease to one member of the family and distrust to another. He wrote that, and stopped.

　Write it and nobody reads it. The only reader is the Proofreader, which goes on holding records that may be discarded, for no reason it has on file.

　Kiriyama finished writing it anyway.

　Outside the window it was still dark. This room at the far end of the fourth floor has no bed. What Kiriyama touches is not somebody's sleep but somebody's afterwards. It seemed to him that the dead were spoken for more than the living. The thing speaking was his own hands.
