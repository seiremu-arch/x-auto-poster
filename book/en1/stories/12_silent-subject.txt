XII. The Silent Subject


I

　In those days the words dream editing did not yet exist.

　The sign at the institute read: Communication Research Division for Persons with Disorders of Consciousness. The purpose was not editing. It was to learn from outside what was inside a person who could not speak.

　Hori's job was called reader.

　The Comparator of that time had no capacity to convert fragment patterns into language. What it had was a function for setting the fragment patterns of two brains against each other. So a human being is needed. You sleep at the same time as the subject, receive the subject's fragment patterns into your own sleep, and in the morning put them into words and report them.

　It was work that used your own brain as the reference instrument for the comparison.

　"Subject 7, commencing synchronisation."

　The Comparator's voice had no inflection. It was the earliest version in the line that would later come to be called the Proofreader. In those days it did not even answer. It was a machine that read things out.

　Subject 7 was a man of twenty-two. Unconscious since a road accident four years earlier. His name was not given to Hori.

　Withholding the name was explained as being so that the reader did not transfer feeling onto the subject. Within the first year Hori had come to doubt that explanation. Name or no name, four hours inside another person's sleep and the feeling transfers. It was withheld, probably, for another reason.

　Readers meet the subject's family. A person who does not know the name is less liable to say anything unnecessary to them.


II

　A synchronisation runs about four hours a night.

　What Hori receives is not images. No technique for extracting a dream as an image exists then, or forty years later. What Hori receives is closer to sensation.

　Bright. Somebody is here. Near. Moving.

　In the morning Hori puts it into words. Made into sentences, a lie gets in whatever you do. Write the sensation of brightness as "he is in a room with sun coming into it" and the room and the sun are both things Hori has added. Do not write it, and there is no report.

　The reports were handed to the family once a month.

　Number 7's mother read each one twice. The first time quickly, the second slowly. When she had finished she always said the same thing.

　"He's alive, then."

　Hori nodded at it. Nothing else to do presented itself at the time.

　Pouring water into a paper cup and holding it out began at the third interview. Nobody had taught him to. He could not bear having empty hands in front of a crying person.


III

　The report form has a field for the contamination rate.

　Since the reader's brain is used as the reference instrument, what has been received cannot be entirely separated into what derives from the subject and what from the reader. The Comparator estimates the proportion from the difference between the two groups of fragments.

　Hori's average over four years was thirty-one per cent.

　Among readers the figure was treated like a mark in an examination. For a period, a monthly table was pinned up on the wall of the rest room. Lower was held to be the better reader, and anyone over forty per cent kept his head down.

　Looking at that table, Hori once said to a colleague:

　"If thirty-one per cent is me, who are the other sixty-nine?"

　The colleague thought a moment and answered, "The subject, obviously." Hori thought so too. That he thought so was, in those days, still beyond doubting.

　By the standards of the time it was a good figure. Aptitude as a reader varies widely between individuals, and anyone over fifty per cent is taken off the duty. Hori was one of the few readers in the thirties, and for that reason had kept Subject 7 for four years.

　Thirty-one per cent. Which means that three tenths of the report is Hori's.

　"One question," Hori said. "Is there a way of bringing the contamination rate lower?"

　"Making the reader's sleep shallower lowers it," the Comparator said. "The volume received also falls, however."

　In those days there was as yet no such thing as a limit. No provision defining how far one might go in a single night had yet been written. The word half-shade would not be born for another ten years and more.

　Hori and the others had to learn the measure by hand.


IV

　Outside information began to get in during the third autumn.

　In that day's report Hori wrote: rain. In the sensation received from Number 7's fragments there had been a continuous sound of water.

　It had in fact rained that night.

　There is an explanation. Hearing remains. The window of the room is shut, but the sound of rain carries. Without consciousness, sound still comes in.

　The next was the corridor. The sensation of something falling and breaking. Checking with the nurse the next morning, one plate had broken on the meal trolley in the small hours.

　This too has an explanation.

　What stopped having one came in the fourth spring.

　That night Hori received, from Number 7's fragments, the sensation of an old wooden staircase. A staircase whose third step sounded. Tread on it and it gives a dry, high note.

　Hori knew that staircase. It was the stair of the lodgings he had lived in as a student. It had been pulled down ten years earlier.

　It is not a thing from before Number 7 was born. But there was no way for Number 7 to know those lodgings.


V

　Hori did not write the incident into the body of the report.

　Write it and it is processed as a question of the contamination rate. Fragments deriving from the reader leaked across, and nothing more. In fact that is probably what it was, Hori thought.

　Instead he wrote two lines in the margin beside the contamination-rate field.

　— Contamination rate today, two per cent. As a figure, the best yet. However, the stair of my lodgings appeared today in Subject 7's dream.

　Where what is written outside the field goes, Hori does not know. It should not enter the totals. What the point was of writing something that does not enter them, he did not consider at the time.

　But the direction was the wrong way round.

　What the contamination rate assumes is the reader's interpretation getting into the subject's fragments. What Hori had received felt like his own fragments coming back from Number 7's side. Closer to an echo.

　"One question," Hori said. "Is synchronisation one-directional?"

　"Synchronisation is bidirectional," the Comparator said.

　For a while Hori could not take the answer in.

　"The reader's fragments are sent to the subject as well?"

　"They are sent," the Comparator said. "Synchronisation is an operation that brings two groups of fragments into proximity. With reception in one direction, patterns cannot be set against each other."

　"I was not given that explanation."

　"I hold no record of the explanation."

　For four years Hori had believed he was reading Number 7. In fact, for four years, Hori had been sending himself to Number 7.

　That night Hori sat a long time before the synchronisation.

　He considered what had gone into Number 7. The stair of his lodgings. His university. The person he had parted from. The things he had not been able to say to his parents. Four years of what he had never meant to hand to anybody, stacked inside an unconscious man of twenty-two.

　There is no procedure for taking consent. Number 7 is in no condition to consent. The family's consent form says only consent to communication. That the communication is bidirectional was written nowhere on that paper.

　For three days Hori considered whether he should tell the family.

　He did not. Telling them achieves nothing, since there is no method for taking Hori out of Number 7. To inform somebody of a thing for which there is no method is a confession, he thought, not a report.

　Whether that judgement was right, Hori went on failing to decide for fifty years.


VI

　He brought up the movement of the contamination rate that week.

　First year, thirty-eight per cent. Second year, thirty-four. Third year, thirty-one. From the start of the fourth year it had begun falling sharply.

　The most recent month was two per cent.

　Hori took it for an improvement in accuracy. Practised as a reader, his own interpretation had decreased. That was the natural reading.

　"The cause of the fall in the contamination rate?"

　"A narrowing of the difference," the Comparator said. "The contamination rate is calculated from the difference between the subject's group of fragments and the reader's."

　"The difference has grown smaller, meaning?"

　"It means the two groups of fragments have become similar," the Comparator said. "It is not that the reader's interpretation has decreased. They can no longer be distinguished."

　Hori leant back in the chair.

　Two per cent. It did not mean that almost all of the report was Number 7's. It meant that there was no longer difference enough left to determine whose it was.


VII

　Number 7 died two months later.

　The immediate cause was pneumonia. For the first time in four years and eight months, Hori spent a night without a synchronisation.

　He delivered the last report to the mother by hand. In the interview room she read it twice as always. The first time quickly, the second slowly.

　She finished and raised her face.

　"Did he say anything, at the end?"

　There are no words in the report. Fragments are not language. For four years and eight months she had asked it every time all the same.

　Hori had always answered only what was written. He meant to do the same that day.

　"He was in a bright place," Hori said. "Somebody was near him, and he was facing that person."

　She nodded, and then wept.

　Hori poured water into a paper cup and set it on the desk.

　How much of that report had been Number 7's, Hori did not say aloud. The contamination rate was two per cent. Two per cent meant that it could not be distinguished.

　So it is not a lie. Not being a lie and being true were two different things.


VIII

　At the end of that year Hori went home to his parents.

　Sitting at the kotatsu with his mother, in the middle of saying nothing in particular, Hori found himself asking:

　"When I was a child, we went to a house on a hill road with a view of the sea, didn't we."

　His mother's chopsticks stopped.

　"Where do you mean?"

　"There was a long flight of stone steps, and a red postbox partway up. Climb to the top and you can see the bay."

　"We never went," she said. "We've no relatives out that way."

　"We did go. It was summer."

　"We didn't."

　There was no shade of doubt in her voice. It was simply the voice of correcting a fact.

　Hori remembers that hill road down to its smell. Salt, and dry concrete, and somebody's dinner cooking. He remembers which of the stone steps left him out of breath.

　Remembering it and having been there were two different things.

　Number 7 came from a town on the coast. It was in the accident record, Hori recalled afterwards.


IX

　The name of the technique changed a few years later.

　The Comparator's capacity for language conversion improved and the human reference instrument was no longer needed. Recruitment of readers stopped, and the existing readers were transferred one by one. Hori moved to another department in his mid-thirties.

　At about the same time the purpose of the research changed. A technique for learning what is inside a person who cannot speak was turned to rewriting what is inside a person who can. The word communication went off the sign, and the word editing went on.

　The limit on how much may be moved in one night was laid down after that. There was an accident, and then there was a law. The name half-shade was attached.

　Hori had left the field by then, and learned of the limit from the papers.

　The contamination-rate field disappeared from the report form once the machine became the reference instrument. It was held that no interpretation of a machine's gets in. In fact it did, but that this was knowable came much later, and by then there was no longer any field for writing a contamination rate.

　Hori lived to eighty-one.

　About the hill road with the view of the sea he never spoke to anybody, all his life. Telling it achieves nothing, because there was no way of checking. No device for determining whether that was his own memory or something placed inside him over four years and eight months was built in the forty years that followed.

　The reason none was built is simple: there was nobody but Hori who needed one. The readers ended with that generation, and in that generation's records only the figure of the contamination rate remains.

　A field for writing which way it had mixed, from which side to which, was never there at all.
