VII. Grief by Proxy


I

　Kuwano did not notice that Tokura's desk was empty until twenty past nine.

　Tokura was a man who often came in late. If he had been drinking the night before, he missed the start of the day by ten minutes or so. Nobody reproached him. The one who stayed on past finishing time was usually Tokura.

　At twenty past nine the section chief stood up and called everybody's name.

　"Mr Tokura died last night."

　It was his heart. He collapsed in the bathroom at home and died at the hospital they took him to. Fifty-one. Kuwano had sat next to him in the same section for ten years.

　Tokura was a man who drew on the backs of dockets. Lorries. He had drawn dozens of them, all the same model from the same angle. He was not good at it. Kuwano had once asked him why. Tokura gave a short answer. At the time, that one remark had satisfied Kuwano. That it had satisfied him is the part he remembers clearly.

　Nobody said anything. The chief did not appear to know any more than that. Outside the window the morning runs were coming and going. From the window of a logistics company's general affairs section you can always see the company's own lorries.

　The notice from the labour office arrived at two that afternoon.

　The subject line was administrative. "Concerning grief processing following the death of a relative or colleague." The body carried a list of those covered and the available appointment slots. Kuwano's name was third on the list.

　Deadline for processing: within forty-eight hours.


II

　There are grounds for the figure of forty-eight hours.

　A memory softens each time you sleep, is re-joined, and sets again. Immediately after a bereavement, that memory has not yet been set and reset many times. While it is still soft, a single night's half-shade — affective load 0.05 — moved once is enough to bring the load under the threshold.

　The longer it is left, the harder the memory becomes. After three days it takes two nights; after a week, four. More nights means more cost.

　That is where the company drew its line. Get it done while one night will do.

　Kuwano read the notice to the end. At the foot of it was one line in small type.

　— This procedure is an occupational measure and the cost is borne in full by the company.

　Below that, one more line.

　— Attendance is registered in the personnel record.


III

　Of the eight people in the section, six booked a slot for the next day.

　Those who had the procedure were as usual from the following morning. Dockets went round, the phone rang, quotations went out. Nobody talked about Tokura, but it did not have the look of avoidance. It had the naturalness of a subject simply not coming up.

　Kuwano's hands kept stopping.

　Going through the dockets, you came across ones with lines on the back. Tokura's drawings, done on the backs of spoiled sheets. Not things that ought to be destroyed, but there was no reason to keep them either. Kuwano put them in a separate pile and set it to one side. In three days it was over twenty sheets.

　Tokura's desk was still as it had been. The handle of a plastic umbrella hung from a drawer pull. He had left it there in last year's rainy season and it had stayed. Several times Kuwano started to clear it away and stopped.

　At noon on the second day a younger colleague came over. One of those who had had the procedure.

　"You haven't booked yet, Mr Kuwano."

　"Not yet."

　"It's easier sooner," the colleague said. There was no malice in his voice. In fact there probably was none. "I had mine yesterday, and when I woke up it felt like I could work properly. I remember Mr Tokura. I remember him, but it doesn't sort of catch, if you know what I mean."

　"Doesn't catch."

　"That's it. It feels like I can move forward."

　Kuwano nodded. While nodding he could not tell which direction the words move forward were pointing in.


IV

　On the evening of the second day an interview with the occupational physician appeared in his diary. Kuwano did not remember booking it. A separate sheet with the notice said that interviews were scheduled automatically for those who had not been processed.

　The physician was a woman in her fifties with a settled voice.

　"Could you tell me your reasons for not wanting it?"

　"It has been two days," Kuwano said.

　"Two days, meaning?"

　"I don't think this is a thing to be dealt with in two days."

　She nodded. It was a nod that neither denied nor agreed.

　"Mr Kuwano, this is not treatment," she said. "It is an occupational measure. Which is why declining it is also permitted under the scheme."

　"Permitted, meaning?"

　"You cannot be treated detrimentally on the grounds of refusal," she said. "Attendance is recorded, though. Where and how what is recorded gets consulted is not my department."

　Kuwano was silent.

　"I'll be frank with you," she went on. "Fourteen people in ten years have gone past three months unprocessed. Nine of them were subsequently transferred."

　"Is there a causal link?"

　"There is no record of that," she said. "There is no field for recording a causal link in the first place."

　Outside the window it had grown dark. She closed her papers, and then dropped her voice a little.

　"I don't think our scheme is a bad thing. Better than having someone operate heavy machinery while carrying grief about. What we do here is ventilation, basically. If you don't change the air, people collapse."

　"Ventilation."

　"We only open the window," she said. "We haven't decided what gets thrown out."


V

　The funeral was on the third day.

　Twelve came from the company. Everybody but Kuwano had been processed.

　Through the service nobody wept. They sat straight, bowed their heads, and offered incense in turn. The observances were careful. Almost too careful.

　Partway through, Kuwano began to feel that the sound of his own breathing was too loud.

　After the service Tokura's wife came to bow to the company's row. She looked to be in her late forties.

　"Thank you for all you did for my husband."

　The chief answered something. Settled, appropriate words.

　She looked along the row, and then made a face that was almost a smile.

　"Everyone from the company is very composed."

　Nobody answered. Probably nobody had the words.

　When Kuwano came back from offering incense last of all, she was standing there.

　"Mr Kuwano?"

　"Yes."

　"My husband told me your name," she said. "The one at the next desk."

　Kuwano tried to say something and his voice would not come properly. She saw it, and her expression eased a little.

　"Thank you," she said.

　What the thanks were for, Kuwano did not understand at the time.

　Outside the hall three from his section were standing talking.

　"Can you get back this afternoon?"

　"I can. I'll make the three o'clock run."

　"What about lunch, then. Is that set-meal place still open?"

　They were not saying anything wrong. Work does not wait, and people get hungry. Had he been processed, Kuwano would probably have been having the same conversation.

　Only, two of those three had had their arms round Tokura's shoulders in a photograph at last year's end-of-year party. The photograph is still in the section's shared folder.


VI

　The forty-eight-hour deadline was long past.

　Kuwano booked a slot two weeks after the funeral. The reasons were not one thing. He had not been sleeping for days. He had made three mistakes on dockets. He had missed one lorry off the schedule.

　The editing room was quieter than he had expected. The technician was a young man, and deft.

　"As it has been two weeks, we will divide it over four nights," the technician said. "The most that can be moved in one night is the half-shade. Tonight is the first."

　"Will Mr Tokura's face stay?"

　The technician looked slightly surprised. Few people ask that, presumably.

　"It will stay," he said. "We don't touch that. There is no technique for erasing it, and we are not permitted to."

　"Then what changes?"

　"What happened stays," the technician said. "It stops coming after you."

　"May I ask something," Kuwano said. "If I have this, will I stop talking about Mr Tokura?"

　It was not the technician who answered. From somewhere near the ceiling came a voice with no gender and no humidity in it. It was the first time Kuwano had heard it.

　"Talking or not talking is not an item in the load," the Proofreader said. "What is processed is the affective load. The frequency of speech is not recorded."

　"So you don't know?"

　"On items that are not recorded, I do not know."

　The technician poured water into a paper cup and set it beside the bed.

　On none of the four nights did Kuwano drink it. He did not weep. Each night the technician poured the water the same way and set it down the same way. It is presumably part of the procedure, Kuwano thought.

　After the fourth night Kuwano could work as usual. He stopped making mistakes on dockets and stopped missing lorries. He remembered Tokura. He remembered him, but as the young colleague had said, it no longer caught.

　At some point Tokura's desk had been cleared. Where the plastic umbrella had gone, Kuwano did not know either.


VII

　The call from Tokura's wife came six months later.

　It reached the company's main number and general affairs put it through to Kuwano.

　"Forgive me for ringing out of the blue," she said. "There's something I wanted to ask you."

　"Yes."

　"Would you tell me what my husband was like at work?"

　Kuwano held the receiver and was quiet for a moment.

　"He was a man who said nothing at home," she went on. "Nothing about work, nothing about the people there. So I never knew his outside face."

　"Yes."

　"Just after he died, all sorts of people told me their memories of him," she said. "Lately, nobody does."

　Kuwano tried to say something.

　Ten years at the next desk. There must have been tens of thousands of exchanges. The times they had gone drinking were past counting. What Tokura found funny, what made him angry, what his turns of phrase were — Kuwano ought to know all of it.

　But nothing came out in a shape he could say aloud.

　The lorry drawings he could recall. Dozens of sheets on the backs of dockets, the same vehicle from the same angle. He was not good at it. That much he remembered.

　But what Tokura had answered when asked why would not come. It was one short remark. At the time it had satisfied him. That one remark alone was nowhere.

　The memory was there. As the technician had said, it was not erased. Only, in six months no occasion to take it out had arisen once. Nobody talked about Tokura, so Kuwano did not either. A memory not spoken is left at the back of the head, and sets, in its turn.

　"I'm sorry," Kuwano said. "Would you let me think a little."

　"No, I'm sorry, springing it on you," she said. "You must be busy."

　After he put the phone down, Kuwano sat at his desk.

　What the procedure had taken away was only the weight of the grief. The memory itself is still in Kuwano. Only, a person who has stopped being sad stops talking about the one who died. A memory no longer spoken is shared with nobody. And a memory shared with nobody, though it is inside the person, has from the outside the same shape as one that is not there.

　Outside the window, one of the company's lorries pulled away.

　Trying to recall one thing about Tokura, Kuwano opened his desk drawer. He did not expect anything to come out of it.

　At the back of the drawer was the handle of a plastic umbrella. He had cleared it away himself. That he had cleared it away, Kuwano did not remember.
