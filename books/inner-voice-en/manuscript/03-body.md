# Chapter 3: Telling them apart in the body

## The apartment that was perfect on paper

I still remember the third place I looked at, the year I moved.

On paper it was perfect. Seven minutes from the station, good light, rent inside my number. The first two had been obviously wrong, so the agent said "this one's good, isn't it," and I agreed with him.

Three steps down the hallway from the front door, my shoulders came up.

I couldn't tell you why. The walls were white. It didn't smell like anything. My shoulders just rose about an inch toward my ears and stayed there.

I said "that was a nice place" and went home. My head was in favor of it right to the end.

Three days later I signed for a different apartment. Twelve minutes from the station, worse light. My shoulders didn't come up in the hallway.

I still live there.

## You can borrow words. You can't borrow a body

In Chapter 1 I described the borrowed voice — somebody else's phrasing, still running in your head.

That voice has an awkward property: **the vocabulary can be borrowed.** Your mother's way of putting things, a manager's way of putting things, coming out as your own sentences. So if you're only watching the words, you lose the line between what's you and what's somebody else.

But you cannot borrow the height of your shoulders.

Not the depth of your breathing, not the tightness in your throat, not whatever happens across your chest. Those only report what is happening in this body, right now.

That's the reason to use the body. **The body answers before the words do.**

In terms of order, the body is the fastest thing there is. Which may sound like a contradiction, since Chapter 2 said fast voices aren't yours. But speed was about voices, and the body is not a voice. The body makes no claims. It loosens, or it tightens.

A thing that makes no claims cannot be borrowed.

## Loosens or tightens. That's all you watch

Watch two things. **Loosens** or **tightens.** Nothing else.

And watch four places, fixed in advance.

- **Chest** — opens, or narrows
- **Throat** — clear, or closed
- **Shoulders** — drop, or rise
- **Breath** — deeper, or shallower

If two of the four point the same direction, take that as the answer for the day. All four rarely agree.

You'll want to add more categories. Don't. The moment you're thinking *there's a fluttery thing in my stomach, which one is that*, you've left observing and started interpreting. And interpreting happens in words, which means one more voice in the room.

**Two options is the point. It leaves nothing to interpret.**

## The body answers "safe," not "right"

This is the most important part of the chapter.

The body will not tell you the right choice. The body answers one question: is this safe?

Which means that **something you're frightened of but want** will make the body tighten. A new place, a new person, work you haven't done before. As far as the body is concerned, all of that is unknown, and unknown is not safe.

Decide by the body alone and you will spend your life avoiding everything you haven't already done. That may be quiet. It isn't what this book is trying to hand you.

So what is it for?

**Use the body to find the places where it disagrees with your words.**

You say *this is the job I should take*, and your throat closes. You say *I want this to keep going*, and your shoulders come up. **The disagreement** is the information. Where it shows up, there's a good chance the borrowed voice or the *should* voice is mixed into the sentence.

For years I said *it's faster to do it myself than to ask someone.* Every time I said it, my shoulders came up.

The words hold together. Sometimes it genuinely is faster. But when a well-reasoned sentence and a tightened body keep arriving as a pair, there's something in there that hasn't made it into language yet. In my case it was *I don't want to be turned down.* It took me two years to admit that.

The disagreement doesn't hand you an answer. **It only tells you where to dig.**

Where there's no disagreement, you can let the sentence stand. If your body is tight and your words are tight too, then you're simply still frightened. Whether to move anyway isn't a body question — that's Chapter 6.

## First, find out what your ordinary looks like

There's a sequencing problem here.

Neither "loosens" nor "tightens" means anything **without something to compare to.** You can't say your shoulders came up if you don't know where your shoulders usually sit. Unlike the order in Chapter 2, the body needs a baseline.

So before Practice 3, spend thirty seconds.

1. Sit. Take three breaths without thinking about anything in particular
2. Check the four places — chest, throat, shoulders, breath — top to bottom, once each
3. Think *this is roughly where I am today*, and stop

No verdicts. Not good, not bad. Just note where you are. That's your baseline.

I skipped this at first. So when I thought *my shoulders came up*, I had no way of knowing whether they'd come up now or had been up since morning. **Some days your shoulders are up the whole day.**

With practice you stop needing the thirty seconds, because you start checking where your shoulders are a few times a day anyway. Once that happens, skip it.

## Practice 3: Try both options in your body

**Five minutes. Somewhere you can speak out loud.**

### Steps

1. Sit down. Get your back off the chair back and your feet flat on the floor
2. Turn the two options into **one short sentence each.** The form is "I'm going to do A" / "I'm going to do B"
3. **Say one of them out loud.** "I'm going to the Friday thing."
4. When you've finished saying it, **wait ten seconds.** In that time, check the four places in order
5. Write one line: "going — shoulders up / breath unchanged"
6. Stand up and walk around a little. Let the body reset
7. Repeat 3 to 5 with the other option
8. Put the two lines side by side. Look only at **which one loosened.**

**Saying it out loud** is the hinge of this practice. Said in your head, the sentence gets talked over before you finish it. Said aloud, you have to reach the end. Those few seconds are when the body's answer has room to show up.

Ten seconds is longer than you think. Any shorter and all you catch is the startle response, which tightens for anything.

### What the records looked like

Three from my notebook.

> **Go to the interview?**
> - Go → chest opened / shoulders still up
> - Don't go → nothing changes at all
>
> **Cancel Saturday?**
> - Go → throat closed
> - Cancel → breath got deeper
>
> **Tell people I'm writing this book?**
> - Tell them → shoulders up / but I wanted to say it again
> - Don't tell them → nothing happens

The third one shows the other marker in this practice: **whether you want to say it again.**

Fear, and the thing you're frightened of but moving toward, both tighten the body. What I use to separate them is this. If, after the tightening, you **want to say the sentence out loud one more time**, that's the one you're moving toward. If you never want to say it again, that's the one you're avoiding.

## Where people get stuck

**I don't feel anything.**

The most common one, and usually it means you're tired. On short sleep, right after a deadline, after a day of seeing many people, the signal doesn't come.

On those days, skip Practice 3. Sleeping is faster than using "I couldn't feel anything" as evidence against yourself. Try again in the morning and it often shows up easily.

If weeks go by with nothing, the body may just not be your instrument right now. Work with speed (Chapter 2) and with writing (Chapter 5) instead. **You do not have to use all seven practices.**

**Neither one loosens. Or both tighten.**

As information, that's close to *not yet.* Or the two options are badly framed.

If "do A" and "do B" both tighten, there is usually **a third option.** Not "go or don't go" but "go for the first hour." Not "keep going or stop" but "cut the frequency in half." When two options jam, build a third with a condition attached and run it again.

**There's nowhere I can say it out loud.**

Family in the next room, thin walls, wanting to do this at work. It comes up a lot.

In that case, **move your mouth without the sound.** Shape the words with your lips and tongue and no voice behind them. It works less well than speaking, but far better than saying it in your head — because a thought gets interrupted halfway, and a moving mouth has to reach the end of the sentence.

A bathroom works. A parked car works. The walk home alone works. I use the twelve minutes from the station most days.

**I start trusting the body too much.**

This one shows up after it starts working. Get it right a few times and you'll want to use your body as an oracle.

The body is not deciding anything. It's answering whether something is safe. Keep choosing whatever loosens and you will end up avoiding everything unfamiliar. **Use the body only to find where it disagrees with your words.** Come back to that as often as you need to.

## Do not read your symptoms through this chapter

One thing to say plainly.

**Please do not use this chapter to interpret physical symptoms.**

Tightness in your chest. A closing feeling in your throat that doesn't let up. Breathing that stays shallow. Not sleeping. A body that won't move in the morning. These can be the body reporting on itself, not a signal about a decision.

All I can do is write this much. **If you're unsure, see a doctor.** That is not the long way around. It matters more than finishing this book.

And if you feel worse after doing this practice, the practice is not for you. You're allowed to stop.

## At the end of this chapter

You can borrow words. You can't borrow a body.

The body doesn't know what's right — so use it only to find where it disagrees with what you're saying.

That's both criteria in place now: **speed** and **the body.** From here the book stops sharpening the criteria and starts building the conditions. Beginning with quiet.
