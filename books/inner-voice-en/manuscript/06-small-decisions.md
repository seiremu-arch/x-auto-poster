# Chapter 6: Deciding small

## My first fork in three years was a Friday night

There was a stretch where I believed I hadn't decided anything for three years.

I didn't change jobs. I didn't move. The thing I kept saying I'd start, I didn't start. People would ask what was new and I'd say "same as ever." Which was accurate.

Reading back my notebook from that period, there is a day where something clearly began to move.

It was a Friday night. That day, I turned down one invitation.

There's one line about it.

> Said no to drinks tonight. Didn't give a reason.

Not a dramatic event. There were similar invitations the following week and I went to some of them. I'm fairly sure I'd forgotten about it by the next day.

But when I count back, *not going to things I don't want to go to* started for me on that day. My first fork in three years looked like that.

## A fork becomes a fork afterwards

When we say decision, we picture a large one. Stay or leave. Stay together or don't. Buy it or don't.

Those days do exist. It's just rare, on one of those days, to know that you're standing at a fork in your life.

**A fork becomes a fork when you look back at it.** At the time, it almost always looks like a small thing that could go either way.

This connects to the nature of the quietest voice.

As Chapter 1 said, it is **short, and it says things once.** It doesn't explain, and it doesn't hand over a blueprint for your life. What it hands over is always one step's worth.

*Maybe that's enough.*
*I'm not going tonight.*
*I'm not asking him for advice again.*

That's the size of it. **The quietest voice never brings a whole large decision.** So if you're waiting for a large decision, it will look as though nothing ever arrives.

It isn't that nothing arrived. It's that it was too small to notice.

## Sort by reversibility, not by size

When it comes time to decide, I use one line only.

> Can this be undone?

Not big or small. How I handle it depends on whether it can be undone.

**Reversible decisions: try them without thinking.**

Say no once. Go once. Send one email. Stop for one week. For these, the thinking costs more than the doing. Compare the answer you reach after three days of thought with what you learn on day three of doing it; the second one is more accurate.

**Irreversible decisions: take the time.**

Sign the thing. Say the thing out loud to the person. Quit. For these, use the week from Chapter 2 and the body from Chapter 3. Don't hurry here.

And once you start checking, you find something. **Irreversible decisions are far rarer than they feel.**

I used to file *quitting* as irreversible. But between here and quitting there were many reversible stages. Look at listings. Talk to someone who left. Apply. Interview. Get an offer. Every one of those is still undoable. The only step that isn't is telling your current employer.

Big decisions, it turns out, are mostly **a chain of reversible small ones with a single irreversible step at the end.**

## When you can't tell whether it's reversible

The line exists but isn't always easy to locate. Three rough tests I use.

**One: has money moved?** A deposit paid, a cancellation fee triggered, a purchase made. Once money moves, it usually can't be undone. Which also means: until money moves, most things can.

**Two: have you told anyone?** This is the strongest test. Deciding in your head and saying it out loud are entirely different stages, because the moment you say it, other people's plans and feelings start to move.

So as a matter of order, **make "telling someone" the last step, always.** Put looking, meeting, and trying first. Tell people first and every option behind it closes.

**Three: would undoing it require an apology?** If yes, it's on the irreversible side. If "actually, never mind" covers it, it's reversible.

If none of the three apply, the decision is reversible. **You may try it without thinking.**

## Practice 6: The smallest possible step

**Five minutes. Not time for deciding — time for cutting it down.**

### Steps

1. Write one line: the thing you can't decide. "Whether to change jobs"
2. Underneath, write **the smallest action that would count as this having moved**
3. If that action **won't be finished within seventy-two hours, it's still too big.** Cut it again
4. Once it's cut, **write when you'll do it, as a date and time.** Not "this week" — "Thursday evening"
5. When you've done it, write one line. If you didn't do it, write that you didn't

The seventy-two hours has a reason. Past three days, the action stops being a plan and becomes a goal. Goals arrive with the *should* voice attached.

Don't skip step four either. A step with no date on it almost never happens. In my own records, steps with a time attached got done about three times as often.

### Examples of cutting

> **Whether to change jobs**
> → Look at job listings (too big — no boundary)
> → **Thursday evening, open one job site and read ten titles. Only the titles**

> **What to do about this relationship**
> → Talk it out (too big — needs the other person's time too)
> → **Today, reply one line to the message I haven't answered**

> **Whether to change how I use my mornings**
> → Start getting up early (too big — a habit doesn't finish in 72 hours)
> → **Tomorrow, set the alarm ten minutes earlier. One day only**

The trick is to **cut until it becomes a test.** *Decide* can't be cut. *Try* can be cut indefinitely.

Adding **just once, just one day** the way the third example does also works. One day is reversible. Reversible things don't need thinking.

### When no step comes, look for a step to stop

Some days no starting step suggests itself. When that happens, look the other way.

**A stopping step** is smaller than a starting step, and almost always reversible.

- Say no (once)
- Don't go (this week only)
- Stop looking (delete one app, for one week)
- Don't reply (let one non-urgent message sit a day)
- Don't say it (swallow the one sentence you always say, once)

My own first fork in three years was not a starting step. It was **saying no** — a stopping step.

Stopping steps have one more advantage. **Your body answers immediately afterward** (Chapter 3). Did saying no make you lighter or heavier? The answer comes back faster than it does with a starting step.

### What the records looked like

Three months of mine, copied out.

> Thu Read ten listings. Turns out there are barely any jobs in my field
> Sat (didn't do it. Writing that down)
> Tue Messaged a former colleague. Just catching up. She replied
> Thu Made a plan to meet. Next Wednesday
> Wed Met her. We barely talked about job hunting. But talking about my current
>     job made it clear what I actually dislike about it

By line five, the answer to the original question has moved. But note that I didn't *decide.* **What happened is that moving changed the question.**

The value of small movement isn't that answers appear. It's that **the question gets more accurate.** Three months of thinking inside your head leaves the question exactly the same shape.

## Is cutting turning into avoidance?

One thing to watch.

**Cutting things small also works as a tool for avoidance.**

*I'm still preparing. Let me gather more information first* — and you stay parked just before the one irreversible step. I did that for two years. I read five hundred job listings and applied to none of them.

Use the test from Chapter 2 here too.

**Avoidance is heavy the whole time you're stopped.** When you're moving a step at a time, the moving is light.

There's a clearer marker as well. **If you've repeated the same size of step three or more times, you aren't moving.** Reading ten listings twenty times doesn't advance a stage. Do it twice, and if nothing changed, go up one size.

## Where people get stuck

**It's so small it feels pointless.**

The point isn't the action. It's **the fact that you moved.**

There is no meaning in setting an alarm ten minutes earlier. But someone who believed they hadn't decided anything in three years decided one thing. The next one comes from there.

**I failed on the first step.**

You picked a reversible step, so undo it. You're back where you started.

Failing matters less than **having been able to test it inside a reversible range.** Do that with an irreversible decision and it's a different conversation entirely.

**I took one step and then stopped.**

Stopping is allowed. The next step can wait until it arrives.

If it's light while you're stopped, that's waiting. If it's heavy, you've crossed into avoidance. Ask the body (Chapter 3).

**The big decision genuinely won't divide.**

Inside things that won't divide, there is almost always a *look it up*, an *ask someone*, or a *say it out loud and see.* The reason those get missed is that **they don't look like deciding.**

The less a step looks like deciding, the more reversible it is. The more reversible it is, the sooner you can test it.

## At the end of this chapter

A fork in the road does not look like a big decision.

Sort by reversibility, not size. Reversible things get tried, not thought about.

Six of the seven are now in your hands. What's left is the one about **the days you hear nothing.** That day is coming.
