# Chapter 4: Making room

## The hand moves the moment five minutes open up

While the microwave counts down two minutes.

The light turning red. Fifteen floors in an elevator. The three minutes of brushing my teeth. Seven minutes waiting for someone.

In every one of those, my hand did the same thing. Into the pocket, screen up, thumb moving.

It wasn't a decision. It was over before I noticed. Sometimes the microwave beeps and I can't tell you what I was looking at.

The strange part is that I didn't experience any of that as *being bored.* If anything, I was busy. In two minutes I read three opinions from strangers, saw five ads, and remembered a message I hadn't answered.

**The time was open. I was not.**

## This is not about cutting information

Conversations like this usually turn into a digital detox. Put the phone away for a week, delete the apps, kill every notification.

I'm not going to recommend that in this book, for two reasons.

**One: it's expensive.** Work is on there. So is your family's schedule. Cutting it off takes preparation, and it takes explaining yourself to other people. Anything that needs preparation doesn't last. The rule in this book is to start cheap.

**Two: it doubles when you come back.** Think about the fourth day, after three days away. I read like I was catching up. And during those three days, a voice was running in the background the whole time: *what's happening out there right now.* That isn't quiet.

So this is not a conversation about volume. It's about **placement.**

What you need isn't less input. It's **a gap between one input and the next.**

## Quiet means no next input is coming

Let me be precise about what this book calls quiet.

**Quiet is not silence.**

You can get quiet inside the noise of a café. The voices and the dishes aren't asking anything of you. That's background, not input.

And you can lose quiet in a completely silent room. The phone face down on the desk lights up once. That's enough. No sound. But **the moment you think something else might be arriving, your seat is taken.**

So: quiet is **knowing that no next input is coming.**

Which connects back to Chapter 2. The quietest voice is slow. It arrives late, once, after everything else has gone around.

For a slow voice to land, the seat has to be empty. In a life where new input arrives every two minutes, the seat is always occupied. The voice didn't disappear. **There's nowhere for it to sit.**

## If you turn off one thing, turn off what arrives unannounced

You don't have to kill all your notifications. But if you pick one category to stop, it's clear which one.

**Stop the things that arrive unannounced.**

Something you go and look at (you open the app and read) and something that comes to you (a banner, a badge, a buzz) fill the seat differently, even when the content is identical. What you opened arrives in a time you chose. What comes to you interrupts the middle of your time.

The nasty part of an interruption is that **the anticipation takes the seat, not the content.** Which means it's working even when nothing has arrived.

What I actually did was not turn off all notifications, but **cut the apps allowed to notify me down to three.** Calls, my family's messages, one work channel. Everything else I read when I open it.

It's a one-time job and takes about five minutes. It might land before the seven-minute gap does.

## Where in the day does seven minutes live

The task is simple. Put **seven minutes with no incoming input** somewhere in your day.

Placement matters more than willpower. Three that survived for me:

- **Part of the walk home** — earbuds out for the last stretch. Walking gives you something to be doing, so the awkwardness of doing nothing is thinner
- **After a shower** — you're already empty-handed, so nothing new has to be built
- **In bed, before the light goes off** — the most effective and the hardest. Once you're sleepy, you stop caring

Some people would pick the morning. It didn't work for me: the *about to begin* voice is too loud in the morning, and seven minutes can't hold it.

One guideline for choosing. **Look for time you're already not using.** Time you newly carve out doesn't last. Find the time that was already open, and that you had been filling — and stop filling it.

## Practice 4: Seven minutes of doing nothing

**Seven minutes. No equipment.**

### What "doing nothing" means

Left abstract, this doesn't survive contact with a real week. So here's the line.

**Allowed**

- Walking. Sitting. Lying down
- Eyes open. Or closed
- Thinking. Remembering. Worrying
- Checking the time
- Stopping early

**Not allowed**

- **Adding new input** (looking at a screen, putting on music, reading, starting a conversation)

That's the only line. Not "empty your mind," not "focus on your breath." **You are allowed to think.** This practice is intact even if your head is loud the whole time.

The only thing being reduced is what comes in from outside.

### Steps

1. Put your phone **out of reach.** Another room is best. Face down on the desk isn't enough (it lights up, and the seat is gone)
2. Set a timer for seven minutes — then put that phone away too. A clock works if you have one
3. Sit, or walk
4. When seven minutes are up, stop
5. Write **one line** in your notebook. Not what you thought about. Just **loud, or quiet.**

Step five is deliberately not "what I thought about." Holding onto thoughts so you can record them is the same work as adding input. This isn't a journaling practice.

### Why seven

Five isn't enough, because **the first three minutes are usually loud.**

You'll see it the first time. The noisiest moment is right after the input stops. Everything left undone today, the message you haven't answered, the thing you said yesterday — all of it arrives at once. If you stop at five, you stop inside that noise, and the only thing you take away is *this isn't for me.*

Ten gives you an excuse. Ten is a length you can call impossible today. Seven is harder to argue with.

**The first three minutes being loud is part of the procedure, not a failure.** Knowing that is most of whether you keep going.

### What the records looked like

A one-line record tells you nothing on its own. It starts saying something when you lay three weeks of them side by side.

My first three weeks went like this.

> Wed loud / Sun loud
> Wed loud / Sun quiet near the end
> Wed quiet / Sun loud (work day)

Quiet showed up inside the seven minutes in week three. Before that, it was just seven loud minutes.

And I noticed something else. **The loud days had a pattern.** Days I saw a lot of people, and days with something to decide, were loud without exception. So what the record actually taught me wasn't how my quiet was progressing. It was **which kinds of days make me loud.**

That turns out to be more useful than it sounds. On a day I can predict will be loud, I don't make important calls that night. That alone cut down my mistakes.

## Why I asked for a paper notebook

In the prologue I asked you to get a paper notebook, and said the reason would come here.

A notes app **lives on the same screen as everything else.**

You open it to write and a notification slides down from the top. You close it and pass the home screen on the way out. That's enough. The seat is gone.

Paper doesn't have that. Open paper and it's still just paper.

This isn't about discipline; it's about placement. **Things kept in the same place get handled by the same reflex.** Don't keep the tool you want quiet in the loud place.

## Where people get stuck

**I can't stand being bored.**

You'll want to quit at three minutes. This happens to nearly everyone; it still happens to me on some days.

Quit if you need to. But before you do, **check the time once.** It has usually been about two minutes. **Boredom wrecks your sense of duration.** Having seen that once, you can hold out a little longer next time.

**I fall asleep.**

Then sleep. If you fell asleep, it wasn't that quiet was unnecessary — it was that sleep was. There is no version of reflection that beats being tired.

If you keep falling asleep, fixing your bedtime will get you further than this practice will.

**It doesn't stick.**

You don't need to do it daily. **Twice a week is plenty.**

I failed three times with "every day," and it stuck when I switched to "Wednesday and Sunday." Fixing the days removes the guilt on the days you don't. Guilt adds a voice, so don't install any.

**I'm never alone at home.**

Seven minutes alone is hard with other people around. So put it outside: the walk home, a round trip to the corner store, the car in the parking lot before you go in.

**The place doesn't have to be silent.** Remember: quiet isn't silence, it's no next input. Even where people are talking, if none of it is aimed at you, the seat stays empty.

**I made it quiet and nothing came.**

If you were expecting *empty the seat and a voice arrives*, this is where it breaks.

Honestly: days when something arrives inside seven minutes are not common for me. Maybe one or two in ten. The other eight I just sit there for seven minutes.

I keep doing it because the one time that lands usually comes **after those eight.** The number of times the seat was empty seems to be what matters.

Whole stretches with nothing arriving get their own chapter — Chapter 7. I think that one is the most realistic chapter in the book.

## At the end of this chapter

Quiet wasn't cutting information. It was putting a gap between one input and the next.

Seven minutes, twice a week. The first three are loud.

As the seat opens up, slow voices start landing. In the next chapter we put **a question** on that seat. The shape of the question changes which voice answers.
