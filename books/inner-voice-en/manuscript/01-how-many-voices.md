# Chapter 1: How many voices are talking

## One sentence, and then more

I was at dinner with a friend on a Friday, complaining about work a little.

She laughed and said: "You should just quit."

I laughed too and let it go. It was the kind of thing people say. She doesn't know the details of my job, and she didn't mean it as advice.

The problem started the next morning.

Saturday, making coffee, the sentence came back. In the afternoon, folding laundry, it came back again. By the evening I was calculating what rent would look like without a paycheck. By the night I was imagining three years from now if I stayed, and feeling worse.

By Sunday I had spent an entire day on it. I hadn't spoken to anyone, and there was still an argument going in my head. *You should quit. That's irresponsible. Everyone feels like this. Stop whining. But it is wearing you down.*

One sentence had turned into several people overnight.

This happens to most of us, I think. And we have a name for this state: *I'm struggling with a decision.*

Look closer, though, and it isn't quite that. **You are being made to sit through an argument.**

## It's loud because you think it's one person

We bundle everything in our heads together as "my thoughts."

Bundled, it looks like this: I have been contradicting myself since yesterday. I said I want out, then I said I'm being irresponsible, then I said stop whining. So I must be weak and indecisive.

That's a misreading. The inconsistency isn't weakness. **It looks that way because you're listening to several voices as though they were one person's.**

Have you ever read a transcript of a meeting where the speaker labels were stripped out? It's unreadable. The same person appears to switch positions every three lines, a rambling monologue that contradicts itself. Add the names back and it becomes an ordinary meeting.

That is what's happening in your head.

So the first thing to do is not to decide which voice is real. It is to **count how many there are.**

## The four voices

I sort the talking into four. The categories aren't the point; they're handles.

### The anxious voice

It arrives first. It's fast. And it repeats.

The easiest tell is that it **speaks in the future tense.** *What if. What happens when. Suppose that.* It isn't talking about what's happening; it's talking about what hasn't happened.

It also brings no new information. It says the same thing with the wording changed. What I was listening to on Saturday evening, calculating rent, was this voice. I spent three hours on it, and the arithmetic never once changed.

You don't need to get rid of the anxious voice. It has a job — lookout — and some days it earns its keep. But a lookout shouldn't get a vote.

### The *should* voice

It uses the vocabulary of correctness. *Normally. Properly. Reasonable people. At your age. Everyone else.*

Its signature is that it **arrives at conclusions that benefit nobody.** It doesn't move toward you having an easier time, or toward anyone around you having an easier time. It just points at the correct shape.

Its other feature is that it's hard to argue with. Say something back and you feel like the bad guy. So don't argue with this one. Just count it.

### The borrowed voice

Somebody else's phrasing, intact.

This is the easiest one to spot, because **it has an accent.** Write the lines down and some of them are clearly not yours — the word choices, the rhythm, where the sentence lands. Your mother's phrasing. A manager's. An ex's. A book you read a decade ago.

The important thing, when you find one, is not to blame the person. In most cases they've long since stopped saying it, and don't remember saying it at all. What's left is only the recording.

*This isn't my position, it's the way he used to say things* — just knowing that halves the weight of the line.

### The quietest voice

Whatever's left.

It has four features. **It's slow. It's short. It says it once. And it speaks in the present tense.**

Slow means late in the order. The anxious voice talks, the *should* voice talks, the borrowed voice talks, and then in the gap after all of that has gone around once, this one shows up.

Short: usually one line. It won't explain itself. *Maybe that's enough. I'm not going to call him again. I'll send the email tomorrow.* Then it stops.

And it doesn't repeat. That's the sharpest difference from the anxious voice. It won't argue you into it. Miss it, and it isn't coming back today.

You don't have to think of this as the voice of your soul. I think of it as simply **your quietest judgment.** It can't be heard in a loud room, but it hasn't gone anywhere.

## Counting is what separates them

Here is the criterion for this chapter.

> Before deciding which voice is real, count how many are in the room.

Trying to pick the right one doesn't work, because the part of you doing the picking hasn't been identified yet either.

Counting, though, you can do right now. And counting separates them on its own. A voice with a label on it gets a little smaller. Anxiety you've recognized as anxiety is still anxiety, but it has stopped being *your conclusion.*

That's the first step of what this book means by **telling apart.**

## Practice 1: Write the voices down

**Ten minutes. Paper and a pen.**

### Steps

1. Get a sheet of paper and a pen. Put your phone in another room, or face down and out of reach
2. At the top of the page, write **one line** about what's on your mind. "Whether to quit." "That reply." Short is fine
3. Underneath, write the sentences that show up, in the order they show up, exactly as they arrive. Don't tidy them. Contradictions are fine. **Aim for ten lines**
4. When your hand stops, stop. Don't squeeze more out
5. Mark the left edge of each line: **A** (anxious) / **S** (*should*) / **B** (borrowed) / **?** (don't know)
6. Copy only the **?** lines onto the back of the page, or a separate one

### What one of mine looked like

Here is the page I wrote two days after that dinner, unedited. It reads badly. That's correct.

> **Whether to quit**
>
> - **A** what happens to rent next year
> - **S** three years in is too soon to leave
> - **B** people will laugh at you
> - **A** there's no guarantee the next thing works out
> - **S** everyone else puts up with it
> - **A** but I'll be saying this again next year
> - **B** you're too old to be starting over
> - **?** Monday mornings are the worst part
> - **A** what do I tell my family
> - **?** I can't picture myself here in five years

Four anxious, two *should*, two borrowed. Two lines left as **?**.

What's interesting is that neither **?** line is an answer. "Monday mornings are the worst part" and "I can't picture myself here in five years" don't conclude anything. They just state something.

The quietest voice often looks like this. It shows up as an observation, not a verdict. It doesn't say *you should quit*; it says *Monday mornings are the worst part.* Which means that if you read the page looking for answers, you'll walk past it.

### Three things to look at afterward

- How many were in the room
- Which voice showed up the most
- How many lines ended up as **?**

You don't have to answer anything. Look at the counts and stop.

At this stage the quietest voice is still mixed in with the **?** pile. Pulling it out is the job of Chapters 2 and 3, so leave it mixed today.

## Where people get stuck

**All of it looks like the anxious voice.**

Common — especially when you're tired, and just before a deadline. That's fine. Keep it as a record: *eight of ten lines were anxious today.* Do the same exercise on another day and the ratio changes. The change is the information.

**There isn't a single quiet line.**

Zero in the **?** column on the first try is unremarkable. My first several attempts were like that. The quietest voice doesn't come out in a loud room. Chapter 4 works on the room, so until then, counting is enough.

Please don't file this as failure. Finding out that nothing came is the result of that session.

**I can't tell which of the four a line belongs to.**

Then don't decide. Mark it **?**. The **?** column isn't "quietest voice"; it's "not sorted yet," and it can be as large as it likes.

Trying to classify precisely turns the classifying into a new thing to worry about. That's just one more voice.

**Writing makes it louder.**

Sometimes you feel worse partway through, because writing puts in front of you what you had been arranging not to look at.

Stop there. Close the page and use another day. This is not the kind of practice that works better if you push through it.

And if that keeps happening, choose talking to someone over the practice. A friend, or a professional. Paper doesn't ask follow-up questions. People do.

## At the end of this chapter

There is more than one voice talking in your head.

Count them, and they start separating on their own.

In the next chapter we'll line the separated voices up **by speed.** Whatever arrived first is usually not yours.
