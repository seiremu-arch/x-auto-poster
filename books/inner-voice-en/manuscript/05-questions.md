# Chapter 5: Writing to listen

## I wrote "what should I do" and sat there for ten minutes

I opened the notebook and wrote one line at the top of the page.

*What should I do.*

My pen didn't move for ten minutes.

It wasn't that nothing was happening in those ten minutes. It was loud, if anything. *What am I even upset about. I shouldn't be spending time on this. This is a waste of an evening.* None of which was the kind of thing you write in a notebook.

I closed the page and went to make coffee.

After a few rounds of that, I decided for a while that writing wasn't for me.

What wasn't working was not the writing. It was **the question.**

## A big question only reaches the big voices

*What should I do* is hard to answer for two separate reasons.

**One: it's too big.** Any answer counts, and no answer is enough. A question with no boundary gets answered first by whatever can talk indefinitely — which is to say, by whatever most wants to talk.

**Two: it asks about the future.**

This is the hinge of the chapter. Go back to Chapter 1, to the four voices. The anxious voice **speaks in the future tense.** *What if. Suppose. What happens when.*

The quietest voice spoke in the **present tense.** *Monday mornings are the worst part. I'm not going to call him again.*

So when you ask a question about the future, the voice best equipped to answer is the anxious one. Write *what should I do* and only the anxious voice puts its hand up. Of course it does.

> A big question only reaches the big voices.

Change the shape of the question and a different voice answers. That was all it was.

## Split the question into three

What I use now is a split into three.

1. **What happened** — past tense. Facts only
2. **What's still catching** — present tense. What's left right now
3. **What could I do tomorrow** — small. Only what's within reach

The order does work.

**In the first, write only facts.** No adjectives. Not *I got spoken to horribly* but *in the meeting, I was told a number in my deck was wrong. Six other people were there.* Put an evaluation in and the evaluation summons the *should* voice.

Facts-only is harder than it sounds. My first attempts were two-thirds impression. I had to start by circling the adjectives and crossing them out.

**In the second, ask in the present tense.** Not *what upset me* but *what is still catching right now.*

Asked in the past tense, you get an account of the event. Asked in the present tense, you get what's still here. Those are often different things. The meeting is over, and what's left turns out to be *in front of six people*, not the number.

**In the third, make it small.** Not *what am I going to do about this* but *what could I do tomorrow.*

The word *tomorrow* does the work. Nothing about the direction of your life fits inside tomorrow. Only specific, small things fit.

## Don't use "why"

There is no *why* anywhere in those three. That's deliberate.

**"Why" has too much chemistry with the *should* voice.**

Write *why did I say that* and what comes back isn't a reason. It's either self-blame or an excuse. *Because I was careless. Because he was out of line.* Both are standing in the arena of correctness — and correctness is the *should* voice's home ground. Write there, and it talks longer.

So I replaced every *why* with **what, where, or since when.**

- Why do I feel heavy about this → **What** makes it feel heavy
- Why do I dislike him → **Where** exactly does he catch on me
- Why can't I keep it up → **Since when** did it stop

Swap the word and what comes back is an observation instead of a verdict. As Chapter 1 said, the quietest voice tends to arrive as an observation. Ask for observations and observations come.

## Practice 5: The three-part question

**Fifteen minutes. Five for each part.**

### Steps

1. Open the notebook and divide the page in three. Top, middle, bottom is fine
2. **Top: "What happened."** Five minutes, facts only. No adjectives. Times, headcounts, the words that were actually said
3. When you're done, **circle the adjectives and evaluations and cross them out.** What you cross out gets used in part two
4. **Middle: "What's still catching."** Five minutes, present tense. If a sentence ends in "it was," rewrite it as "it's still"
5. **Bottom: "What could I do tomorrow."** Five minutes. Only things that fit in tomorrow's schedule
6. If you can't write anything in the third part, **write that too.** "Nothing comes to mind for tomorrow."

Dividing the time in three is there to stop you spending it all in part one. Left alone, I spend fourteen minutes there. Writing facts is the safest and least productive place to be.

### Practising part one

Here's how hard facts-only is, using my own page.

The first thing I wrote was this.

> Terrible meeting. He humiliated me in front of everyone. He always talks like that.

Of three lines, the facts are *there was a meeting* and *he said something.* Everything else is my verdict.

Circling and crossing out:

> ~~Terrible~~ meeting. He ~~humiliated me~~ ~~in front of everyone~~. He ~~always~~ ~~talks like that~~.

Then rewrite what survives as fact.

> In Tuesday's meeting, he said a number in my deck was wrong. Seven people were there. I said "I'll check." The meeting ended on time.

The words I crossed out — *terrible*, *humiliated*, *always* — don't get thrown away. **They go straight into part two.** What you delete in part one is the raw material for part two.

*Always* is an especially useful marker. **When you write "always" about something that happened once, there's usually a borrowed voice or an old memory mixed in.** In fact, that was the first time he'd ever said it to me.

### What the records looked like

The same event, written with a big question and written in three parts.

> **The day I wrote "what should I do"**
>
> What should I do. Should I stay in this job. I don't think I'm suited to it. But leaving
> probably ends up the same. Maybe it's the same everywhere. No point thinking about it.

> **The day I wrote it in three parts**
>
> **What happened** — In Tuesday's meeting, he said a number in my deck was wrong.
> Seven people there. I said "I'll check." The meeting ended on time.
>
> **What's still catching** — The wrong number doesn't matter to me now. What's left is
> how small my voice was when I said "I'll check."
>
> **What could I do tomorrow** — Email him the corrected number. Three lines is enough.

The first produced nothing in ten minutes. The second **had one thing decided** by the time I finished writing.

And look at what surfaced in part two. What was catching wasn't the error. It was the size of my own voice. **A big question would never have gotten that out of me.**

### The three-minute version

For days when fifteen minutes isn't there.

1. One line of fact (1 minute)
2. Write "what's still here is" and finish the sentence (1 minute)
3. Write "tomorrow I'll" and finish the sentence (1 minute)

Just the bones of the three parts. It works less well, but **it beats making a day with nothing in it.**

I do this one before bed more often than not. Sloppy three times a week grows a notebook faster than perfect once a month does.

## Where people get stuck

**I have nothing to write.**

If part one won't fill, don't write that day. Facts not coming usually means nothing much happened. That's a good day.

**Only negative things come out.**

That's what part two is for. You asked what's catching, so what's catching arrives.

But **don't stop at part two.** Close the notebook before part three and all you've done is dig something up. On short days, skip part two and do one and three. I do that on tired days.

**I'm afraid to read back what I wrote.**

Don't read it back.

I mean that. This practice exists to get things out of your head, not to build a document. Deciding in advance that you'll never reread it makes you more honest on the page.

The only things worth rereading are **the first line from Chapter 2's twenty-four hour rule** and **Chapter 4's one-line record.** Those two are short and contain no verdicts, so they don't sting.

**I look up and I've only written about other people.**

Early on, half my notebook was other people. What he said, what she must be thinking, why he is the way he is.

I thought I was writing about myself. But **every sentence had somebody else as its subject.**

There's one fix. **Put yourself back as the subject and write it again.**

- "He always talks down to me" → "I go silent when he talks to me that way"
- "She doesn't take me seriously" → "My voice gets smaller around her"

Rewritten, the thing changes category. You can't move someone else's personality. You can test your own behavior tomorrow. Part three usually appears right after you've switched the subject back.

**Part three never comes.**

Not being able to name something for tomorrow usually doesn't mean the problem is too big. It means **part two isn't finished.**

On those days, split part two again. *What's still here* → *which of these is the smallest?* Take the small one and part three sometimes appears.

If it still doesn't, write that it didn't and close the page. Part three is also the doorway to the next chapter, where deciding gets its turn.

**Do I have to do this daily?**

No. It takes fifteen minutes, so I do it once a week, on a fixed day.

You'll want to write on the day something happens — but **write it that day and part one won't be facts.** While there's still heat in it, the adjectives don't stop. Two days later goes better.

## At the end of this chapter

A big question only reaches the big voices.

Split it in three — facts, what's still here, what fits in tomorrow. Don't use *why*.

The small thing that shows up in part three is the material for the next chapter. **A fork in the road does not look like a big decision.**
