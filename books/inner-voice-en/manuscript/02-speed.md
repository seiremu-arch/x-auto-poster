# Chapter 2: Telling them apart by speed

## "Sure, I'm in" — and then, three days later

A group text came in on a Tuesday afternoon. A name I hadn't seen in six months.

"We're getting together Friday, finally. You coming?"

I replied in three seconds. "Sure, I'm in!" With the exclamation mark.

Thursday night, with Friday getting closer, I felt heavy about it. It wasn't that I didn't want to go. I knew I'd enjoy it once I was there. But who, exactly, had decided in those three seconds?

In three seconds I had thought nothing at all. The reply still went out wearing the face of my own intention.

## Speed is not conviction

From the outside, a fast answer looks like confidence.

*She's decisive* is almost always meant as praise. Being able to decide quickly is taken as evidence that you know what you stand for.

But after writing down a few dozen of my own replies, what I noticed was the opposite. **The faster the answer, the less likely it was mine.**

It's fast because I've been down that road many times. *It's rude to say no. If you're invited, you go. Reply quickly.* None of those were produced by thinking. They are well-worn paths, and I slid along one.

Fast replies have also been rewarded for a long time. People who answer quickly are called reliable; people who take time are called indecisive. For a stretch of my life I thought my quick replies were one of my few useful traits.

A rewarded behavior gets repeated. A repeated behavior gets faster. A behavior that's fast enough stops showing you that no thinking happened. That's how a three-second reply is built.

Reflexes are fast, and there's a reason for that. It's the same design as the anxious voice in Chapter 1: a system for protecting the body. A car comes, you step back before deciding to. That speed saves lives.

The problem is that almost nothing asking for a response today is a car. It's messages, and questions from a manager, and something a family member says. And the reply comes out at the same speed anyway.

So here's how I hold it now.

> Speed is not evidence of conviction. It's evidence of familiarity.

## Line them up in the order they arrived

In Chapter 1 we took volume off the table, because choosing by loudness lets anxiety win every time.

If it's off the table, something has to replace it. In this chapter, it's **order.**

Order has a convenient property: **it doesn't need anything to compare against.** Whether something is loud or quiet only means something next to other voices. But *this one came first, that one came after* is observable on its own.

Laying out my own records, the order came out roughly like this.

1. **The anxious voice** — within three seconds. Essentially reflex
2. **The *should* voice** — seconds to minutes. Arrives with "normally" and "properly" attached
3. **The borrowed voice** — minutes to hours. Shows up after somebody's face comes to mind
4. **The quietest voice** — overnight. Sometimes days

Don't trust the timings. They vary by person, and by how tired you are that day. What's usable is **the order.**

Why does the quietest voice arrive last? I think it's simply **a road you haven't worn in.**

To come fast, a thought has to have been used many times. *It's rude to say no* is a road I've traveled thousands of times. *What do I actually want here* is a road most adults have barely walked since school. An unused road takes longer to get down. I don't think it's more complicated than that.

Which leaves one thing to do. **Wait.**

## Practice 2: The twenty-four hour rule

**Ten seconds now, three minutes tomorrow.**

### Steps

1. When someone asks you for an answer, before answering, ask **when they need it by**
2. Don't answer on the spot. **Say you'll come back, with a date attached** (wording below)
3. Write one line in your notebook: **the answer you have right now.** The three-second one is the one to write
4. Twenty-four hours later, open that page. **Before you read the first line**, write today's answer underneath
5. Look at the two together. If they match, it wasn't a reflex. If they don't, note that and close the page

Step four is the one to be strict about. If you read first, you'll write something that agrees with what's already there.

### How to say you'll come back

- "Thanks — let me check my week and get back to you tomorrow."
- "I can't give you a straight answer right now. I'll message you by tomorrow evening."
- "I'd like to think about it. Can I tell you by Thursday?"

**Always attach the date.** "Let me think about it" lands as a soft no. A date turns it from a refusal into a hold.

### Three things to look at, twenty-four hours later

- Is today's answer the same as the first line?
- If it changed, was it the conclusion that changed, or the reason?
- How many times did you think about it during those twenty-four hours?

The third one does the work. **Whatever you thought about repeatedly is usually held by the anxious voice.** If an answer showed up the next day without your having revisited it once, that's the slow voice.

### What the records looked like

Three, copied from my notebook. Top line is the three-second answer; the line under it is the next day.

> **Go to the Friday thing?**
> - Sure, I'm in!
> - Go for the first hour. Say up front that I'm leaving early
>
> **Take on mentoring the new hire?**
> - No — I'm already underwater
> - Yes. But ask to make it once a month
>
> **Pick a weekend to visit my parents**
> - Sometime next month
> - (nothing came, twenty-four hours later either)

The first changed only its conditions. The second flipped. The third produced nothing.

The interesting part is that **what changed was the conditions more often than the conclusion.** Not whether to go, but how to go. Not whether to do it, but at what frequency.

I kept about thirty of these records, and the conclusion flipped in five. The rest either gained a condition or didn't move.

Which means this practice isn't for changing your answers. It's for **being able to put conditions on them.** A three-second reply never has conditions attached.

## When twenty-four hours isn't enough

Big decisions need more. Changing jobs, moving, ending something, spending a serious amount of money. One night won't get you to the last voice.

For those I stretch it to a week. But I made one rule about stretching.

**Decide, in advance, the day you write the first line and the day you write the answer.**

"I'll think about it for a while" means the thinking period never ends. And for the whole of that period, the anxious voice and the *should* voice keep talking. That isn't waiting; it's just spending longer in a loud room.

Set the day, and you get to stop carrying it. A thing you've scheduled for Friday can be put down on Tuesday. **The useful part of waiting turns out to be the not-thinking.**

If a week goes by and nothing comes, change the conditions instead of waiting more. Being unable to decide is usually not missing information — it's having only two options. Invent a third and an answer sometimes appears immediately.

## Where people get stuck

**Delaying feels unfair to the other person.**

Sometimes it is. Don't use this on the person trying to book a table tonight, or on a colleague up against a deadline in an hour.

Here's the test. **If waiting twenty-four hours would actually cause someone a problem, it isn't a waitable decision.** If it wouldn't, it usually is. And once you start checking, you find that genuinely unwaitable decisions are rarer than they felt. Most of the time, the person in a hurry is not the other person; it's your own restlessness.

**Twenty-four hours pass and nothing comes.**

That's a result too.

The quietest voice has nothing to say about things that don't matter to you. Nothing arriving isn't a failure — it's the information that either option is fine. Keep your first answer. You've just found one decision you're allowed to make casually.

**Someone is pushing for an answer right now.**

"I need to know today." Sometimes that's true, so ask about the deadline first.

But if you ask and still get pressure with no explanation, **that's information by itself.** Some people would rather not give you time to think.

One line I keep for those moments: *"If I have to decide this second, the answer I'll give is no."* That isn't a negotiating tactic. It's accurate. What comes out when you're rushed is the anxious voice, and the anxious voice defends.

**Slow does not mean right.**

This is the easiest mistake to make. Waiting does not make an answer correct. Some days you're just avoiding it.

One way to tell them apart: **avoidance is heavy the whole time you're not deciding.** Waiting for a slow voice is light while you wait.

But you can't make that call in your head. The moment you start wondering *is this avoidance?*, you've added another voice. This one is faster to ask the body. That's the next chapter.

**I already answered too fast.**

You can take it back. "I replied too quickly yesterday —" will recover most situations. A decision you can reverse is better reversed than lived with. More on that in Chapter 6.

## At the end of this chapter

Speed was evidence of familiarity, not conviction.

Line them up in order, and the quietest voice is at the back.

In the next chapter we'll look at **the lightness and the heaviness** of waiting, in the body.
