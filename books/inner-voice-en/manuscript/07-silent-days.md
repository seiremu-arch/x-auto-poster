# Chapter 7: The days you hear nothing

## In week three, nothing came

The first two weeks went well.

Write it down and the voices separated. Wait twenty-four hours and a different answer showed up. My body answered honestly, and a few of the seven-minute gaps turned into quiet ones. I thought I had the hang of it.

Monday of week three, I opened the notebook and nothing came.

Tuesday was the same. By Wednesday, doing the practice at all had become a chore. By Thursday I reread a line I'd written two weeks earlier and thought, *how did I write something like that.*

Friday I closed the notebook, and I didn't open it again for two months.

I think this happens to nearly everyone who reads this book. **It does not take the shape of "keep going and it gets quieter."** As far as I know, it doesn't.

## The voice didn't go. You got faster

Two months later I opened the notebook, and the first thing I wrote was this.

> Why did I stop being able to hear anything

The person who told you in Chapter 5 not to use *why* wrote *why* on the first line. What came back was, predictably, self-blame. *Because you don't follow through. Because you weren't serious.*

So I rewrote it.

> Since when did I stop being able to hear anything

That one had an answer. The Monday of week three was the first day of a week in which a large project had started.

Which means this. **The voice didn't disappear. I had gotten faster.**

Chapter 2 said that speed is evidence of familiarity. When you get busy, you travel only the worn roads. And for as long as you're on the fast roads, a slow voice can't get there in time.

The awkward part is that **you cannot notice from the inside that you've sped up.** When you're fast, the speed feels normal. There's nothing in you, right now, to compare it to.

So keep the markers outside yourself. I use these four.

- I'm answering before the other person has finished
- My eyes are sliding down the page (I've just read this paragraph and can't recall it)
- I'm walking fast. I'm passing people
- My hand goes into my pocket at a red light (Chapter 4)

If two of those are showing, I'm almost certainly fast. Worrying on such a day about not hearing your quietest voice is like trying to catch a small sound while running.

## Search, and the searching fills the room

The moment you notice you can't hear anything, the thing you most want to do is **look for it.**

Write it out again. Add another practice. Try to remember how you were doing it in week two. Work out what went wrong.

I'll repeat what the prologue said. **If you go looking, what turns up matches what you were hoping for.** And the looking itself makes noise. In a room where you can't hear anything, pacing around being anxious about not hearing anything makes the room louder.

So the thing to do on a day you hear nothing isn't to search.

**Stop searching, and go back to the place you come back to.**

There's only one such place. **Now.**

You can't go back to the past (that becomes regret). You can't go back to the future (that becomes anxiety). The only place available is where you are. And where you are is not in your head — it's where your body is.

## Practice 7: Three lines to come back

**Thirty seconds. You don't have to write anything.**

This is the last practice, and probably the one you'll use most. Over the last year I've used it more than the other six put together.

### Steps

1. Name one thing **you can see** right now. "White wall."
2. Name one thing **you can hear** right now. "The fridge."
3. Name one thing **your body is touching** right now. "The back of this chair."

That's it. It takes under thirty seconds.

**Don't close your eyes.** If you can't say it out loud, say it inside your mouth. Three lines in a notebook is fine if one's there, but it isn't needed.

You can do it walking, in a meeting, standing in a line. I use it on trains more than anywhere else.

### Why these three

Because all three are in the **present tense**, and none of them require a verdict.

Chapter 5 was about the tense of a question. Ask about the future and the anxious voice answers; ask about now and you're in the same tense as the quietest voice. These three lines are the smallest present-tense question there is.

And "white wall" has no good or bad in it. With no verdict to make, there's no gap for the *should* voice to come in through. **A practice you cannot possibly score yourself on** — that's what these three lines are worth.

Of the seven, it's the only one that works while you're exhausted. Which is why, in the stretches when the other six are impossible, this is the one that's left.

### Deciding in advance where you come back

These three lines work anywhere. And a thing that works anywhere **gets done nowhere.**

So tie it to one place. Decide: *when I get here, three lines.*

Mine is standing at the doors on the way home, on the side that opens. When I'm standing there, the three lines start before I think about it. Once it's fixed, you no longer have to remember. Anything that needs remembering is the first thing to fall off a busy day.

Some options to tie it to:

- Just after taking your shoes off at the door
- The moment you step out of the building at work
- Standing in the kitchen waiting for water to boil
- In bed, before the light goes off

**The faster you're going, the more the tie matters** — because on fast days, the decision to do a practice never surfaces. Build it so it needs no decision.

### What I wrote the day I came back after two months

Let me put down what happened the day I opened the notebook again, in case you're wondering what to write.

The first line I wrote was this.

> Didn't open this for two months.

That's all. No reason, no reflection. I knew that trying to write reasons would start the blaming, so I wrote the fact and stopped there (the same method as part one in Chapter 5).

The second line was that day's three.

> Fluorescent light / extractor fan / the corner of the desk

That was enough to come back.

If you're opening this book again after months away, do the same. **Coming back doesn't require an account of where you've been.** One line of fact, and today's three lines. From there, it's continuous again.

### When I actually use it

I don't keep records of this one; it isn't the kind of practice that needs them. But I did log when I used it, for a while.

> Mon Train. When it stopped for an incident
> Tue After a meeting, before sitting back down
> Wed (didn't use it)
> Thu In the kitchen, after speaking sharply to my kid
> Fri Before bed. Couldn't sleep

Laying them out, I noticed I was **not using it to hear anything.** Every one of them was to stop a version of myself that had sped up.

I think that's right. These three lines aren't a tool for summoning a voice. They're **a tool for slowing yourself down.** Whether anything arrives after that can be left to the day.

## Where people get stuck

**There are months where I hear nothing at all.**

I went two months, and I've had two more gaps about that long since.

Don't try to fix those stretches. **Treat them like a season.** Blaming the soil because nothing is flowering in winter doesn't get you anywhere.

Do one thing, though. **Leave the road open for when you come back.** Don't throw the notebook away. Do the three lines when you remember them. That's enough to keep the place there.

**I can't bring myself to open the book.**

That's fine. This book is sometimes doing its job while it's closed.

What I want to hand over isn't the content of the chapters. It's **the habit of wondering how many are talking.** The habit stays after the book shuts.

**The practices have turned into things I have to do.**

This is a bad sign, so let me name it.

When a practice gets taken over by the *should* voice, this book starts working backwards. *I haven't done my seven minutes today. I skipped the three-part question.* And now it's a device for adding one more voice.

If that happens, **stop all of it.** Keep only Practice 7. Thirty seconds, no verdict. I only know of one person who came back that way, but I do know of one.

**I came back and there's nothing here.**

More often than not, where you come back to is quiet and nothing arrives. As Chapter 4 said, for me it's one or two times in ten.

Nothing arriving is not a failure. **Having come back is the result.** Whether a voice shows up is not under your management.

## This part is not about the practices

One last thing, plainly.

If your mood doesn't come back for weeks. If you can't sleep, night after night. If your body won't move in the morning. If you can't stop crying. If everything stays behind a thin film no matter what you do.

**Then it isn't the practices in this book you need — it's a professional.**

Maybe you can't hear anything, and it isn't because you're fast. Something that returns when you slow down, and something that needs rest and treatment, look similar from the outside and are not the same. I'm not qualified to tell them apart.

If there's a day when you close this book and go to a doctor, I think that's the day this book worked best.

## At the end of this chapter

The voice didn't go. You got faster — and you can't notice that from the inside.

So on the days you hear nothing, don't search. Three lines, and you're back in the present.

That's all seven. What's left is to look once more at what this book was trying to hand you, and stop.
