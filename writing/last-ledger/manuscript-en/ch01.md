# Chapter One: The Baton

The wind changes character after two o'clock.

In the morning it is only cold, but past noon it begins to carry the smell of dry earth. It comes down off Akagi. Shuji Kamiya shifted the traffic baton from his right hand to his left and stood at the entrance of the fourth car park of the shopping centre. The heat pad stuck inside his coat had stopped working three hours ago.

It was Saturday afternoon and the cars did not let up.

"I'm sorry, we're full here. Please go around to the multi-storey on the south side."

A white minivan lowered its window, the man in the driver's seat clicked his tongue, and turned away. Kamiya bowed. Bowing was part of the job, so he did not take offence. That it did not offend him still surprised him occasionally.

A hundred and twenty cars an hour. Ninety minutes' average stay. Three floors full, so there were at least two thousand people inside the building. Put the average spend at three thousand two hundred yen and—

He did not intend to count. He counted anyway. Thirty years in the lending department. When he saw a number, the accounts behind it stood up by themselves. The money was gone. Only the habit had stayed.

A little before three a single kei car dropped out of the queue. A woman of about eighty was driving. She tried to get into an empty bay, cut the wheel three times, and came out crooked all three times. A car waiting behind her sounded its horn.

Kamiya lowered the baton and walked over to the driver's window.

"Full lock to the right. Now straight back, slowly. You've got room. You've still got room."

As he spoke he held up a palm to the car behind. On the fourth attempt the car went in. The woman lowered her window and bowed several times. Kamiya bowed too. The job of a car park attendant is not to park the car; it is to make the car behind wait. That much he had learned in a year.

At the three o'clock break he went back to the staff room behind the service corridor. He bought a hundred-and-thirty-yen coffee from the machine and sat down on a folding chair. Matsumoto, on the same shift, put his phone face down. Sixty-two, and he had started here last year.

"Kamiya-san, you were at a bank, weren't you."

"That was a long time ago."

"This new tax-free investment scheme. Is it too late for someone my age? I've still got the younger one at university."

Kamiya drank a mouthful of coffee. The can was hot and it stung his fingertips where he had taken the glove off.

"The same amount, on the same day, every month. That's all."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Matsumoto said, "Is that really how it works," and laughed. Kamiya laughed too.

That was the one thing he had not been able to do.

He clocked off at five and drove home in his kei car. A builder's house on the edge of Takasaki, twenty-six years old. The loan had finished last summer. When it finished, Kayoko had cooked red rice.

Opening the postbox beside the gate had become the habit of the past year. Once in the morning, once when he got home. He looked twice.

Last February a securities company envelope had reached Kayoko first and she had said, "What's this." *Company paperwork*, he had answered, and that had been the end of it. It had been the end of it, but after that Kamiya had started getting to the postbox before his wife. The annual transaction statement usually arrives at the end of January. This year it had not come yet. That it had not come yet had been caught in his throat for two weeks.

In April 2025, Kamiya had sent twenty-eight million yen to a firm called Arclight Capital. Generative AI, they said, trading automatically in markets around the world, twenty-four hours a day. The account manager's Japanese was good and once a month a PDF report arrived. The numbers always went up a little. In October he applied to withdraw part of it and was told a tax procedure was required. In November they asked for a further two million four hundred thousand to cover the cost of that procedure. Kamiya did not pay it. Because he did not pay it, the account was frozen.

It had been most of his retirement money.

Kayoko believed it was in a fixed-term deposit.

A brown parcel was sticking out of the mouth of the postbox.

It was not a securities envelope. Kamiya took a breath before pulling it free. It was wrapped in two layers of newspaper and tied crosswise with paper cord. It was a very old way of tying a parcel. Not a courier label — a post office slip, stuck on one side.

The sender's box was blank.

Only the address was written, in fountain pen, in a hand that rose to the right. Takasaki City, Gunma — the block, the number, *Mr Shuji Kamiya*. The house number was correct.

The postmark said February 10. Ugano Post Office.

Ugano.

Thirty years, and he had not said the name of the place out loud.

The branch building had stood two streets in from the station. Two storeys, with tall narrow windows, and in winter the dust blew in off the mulberry field behind it so the glass was always clouded white. His desk had been third from the right along the counter.

He remembered. He remembered it in detail.

He remembered, and yet since leaving he had never once driven past it. It would be more accurate to say that he had chosen roads that did not go past it.

Kamiya stood at the gate for a while holding the parcel. The wind came in at the back of his neck.

"What's that?"

Kayoko put her head out of the kitchen while he was taking his shoes off in the entrance. Kamiya kept the parcel under one arm and put the shoehorn back.

"Company paperwork. Year-end."

"A car park has paperwork?"

"Safety management."

Kayoko said, "Hm," and went back to her pot. A man gets good at lying in a year, Kamiya thought. And there is no one he can tell that he has got good at it.

Mai rang partway through dinner. Kayoko put her on speaker, so Kamiya could hear. Five minutes about the grandson running a temperature, and then Mai's voice dropped a register.

"So. About the house."

They had been to see a second-hand flat, she said. Eight minutes from the station, fifteen years old, forty-two million. Put eight million down and the monthly repayment would be the same as their rent now.

"What do you think, Dad?"

"Fifteen years old — look at the history of the repair reserve fund. There'll be an increase scheduled."

"Spoken like a banker."

"Former."

After the call ended, Kayoko stacked the rice bowls and said:

"Eight million. We couldn't manage that, could we."

"…"

"When does that deposit mature again?"

"Year after next."

"I see."

Kayoko went to the sink. Watching her back, Kamiya set down his chopsticks. A single shred of spring onion was stuck to the rim of the miso soup bowl, and he looked at it for a while.

After eleven he went up to the six-mat room on the first floor. It was halfway between a store room and a study, with the box of tax returns, the textbooks his daughter had left behind, and a set of golf clubs he had never used.

He cut the paper cord with scissors.

Two layers of newspaper. When he opened them out it was a local paper from June 2019. If you only want to wrap something, that day's paper will do. Someone had kept this one for nearly seven years.

What came out was a hand-bound notebook.

About twenty-four centimetres tall. The cover was dark blue with the colour worn off the four corners. There were two kinds of binding thread. The original had broken and been restrung twice. When he touched it, the paper seemed to cling to his fingers.

Kamiya opened it on his knees.

In the upper right of the first spread, a single character had been written in ink.

　　土

Below it, several lines. Not Japanese. Angular letters, running from right to left. Kamiya could not read them. He could not read them, but he could see that whoever wrote them had not been in a hurry. The characters were even.

From the next line it became Japanese. The hand had changed.

　　Showa 16, twelfth month　the port closed　I had put the goods in three places so two remained

Below that, another hand again. Pencil this time.

　　Showa 23　the black market　they went in the order of those who had staked on a single article　I sold rice　I am alive

Fountain pen. Blue-black ink.

　　Showa 48　the paper vanished　I sold everything on the shelves　only those with three things to sell could sell

Ballpoint.

　　Heisei 3　I held only land　I held nothing but land

The Heisei 3 line ended there. Something had been started on the next line and scratched out.

Kamiya turned the page.

In the upper right of the next spread there was a different character.

　　種

Below it too, hands from different periods stood one under another. Turning further: 木, 蜜, 鍵, 手 — tree, honey, key, hand. There were seven characters in all. On none of the pages was there a single amount of money. Only what had been lost in what year, in one line or two. There was nothing to say who had written it either.

The quality of the paper changed from page to page. Some of it was coarse *washi*, some of it the rough ruled stock of the post-war years, and toward the back it looked as though sheets had been cut from a commercial notebook and stitched in. Every few decades somebody had added what was missing. Added, and closed it again.

*A ledger,* Kamiya thought.

A ledger is a thing you write amounts in. And yet there was no other word for it.

There was no debit and no credit. There was no balance. And still, every single line was about money.

He went back to the 土 page.

Below the Heisei 3 line was writing in blue ballpoint that looked as though it had not long dried. It was newer than any of the other hands, and it was the only line in the book written in the Western calendar.

　　February 10, 2026　I divided it in three　there is one more　I could not divide it

February 10. The same day as the postmark.

Kamiya read the line three times. Reading it three times did not add anything to it.

He turned on. He came to a spread with the single character 水 — water — and nothing else.

Below it was white. The next page, and the one after that, were white to the end. No one had written anything there.

Kamiya closed the ledger, and then opened it again. He laid the pad of his finger on the line in blue ballpoint.

*—there is one more. I could not divide it.*

Downstairs, Kayoko turned over in her sleep. Outside, the wind rattled the shutter once.
