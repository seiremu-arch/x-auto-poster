# The Last Ledger: An Estate in Three Parts

The Last Ledger, Book 1

Kazu A. Suzuki

# Prologue

March 1941. Kobe.

The man sat at the second-floor window of a boarding house partway up the Kitano slope.

The harbour was visible from the window. Since morning there had been the sound of cargo being worked. He had been listening to that sound for five months. He had arrived the previous October. The ship left next week. The destination was supposed to be Shanghai. It had changed three times.

On the desk lay a single hand-bound notebook.

He had bought it in this town. Japanese paper was thin and the ink showed through. He had taught himself a way of writing that did not show through. Lay the pen flat. Take the strength out of the hand.

He opened the first page and wrote one character in the upper right corner.

土. It was read *tsuchi*. In the language of this country it meant the ground, the keeper of the boarding house had told him.

There was a word of similar sound in his own language. The name of the material a man was made from. Having written it, he was a little disappointed that the two shapes were not alike.

Below it he wrote a few lines.

What he wrote was not an amount of money. It was what he had lost, and what had been left afterward.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The first time he left, the man did not sell the house.

To sell it was to let people know he was running. Once they knew, he could not go. So he locked it and left. The key was still sewn into the lining of his coat. There was no longer a door it opened.

The second time he left, the man carried cash. The cash he carried was halved at the border. It shrank each time he had a paper stamped.

The third time he left, the man divided it.

He gave one share to his sister to carry, carried one himself, and left the third with a person he had never met. His sister's share vanished on the way. His own was halved at the port. Only the share he had left behind arrived, three years later, from an address he did not know.

Why it arrived, he still did not understand.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The man finished writing and put down the pen.

On six more pages he wrote one character each. 種. 木. 蜜. 鍵. 手. 水. Seed. Tree. Honey. Key. Hand. Water.

All of them he had learned in these five months. There was a reason for the order. It was a reason no one but the man who wrote them would understand.

When he had finished, he looked for a while at the space below the last character.

There was nothing he could write there yet.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The day before he sailed, the man went down the slope to the pawnshop.

He had made use of the place many times over the five months. The owner was about sixty and spoke broken German. The man never asked where he had learned it.

He brought out an overcoat, a watch, and his mother's ring. Bringing the three on separate days had been his practice for five months. Offer everything at once and the price falls.

The owner looked at the three and wrote a figure on a slip of paper. The man nodded.

Then he took the notebook from inside his coat and set it on the counter.

"This won't fetch you anything."

The owner said it in Japanese. The man did not understand the words. He did not understand them, but he understood what they meant.

The man shook his head and pushed the notebook across the counter toward the owner.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The ship sailed on the Thursday of the following week.

No one knows how far that man got.

The pawnshop owner put the notebook on a shelf at the back of the shop and left it there.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

From the autumn of that year, the owner began dividing his stock into three and keeping it in three places.

He could never explain why. A dealer down the street asked him about it and laughed that it was nothing but extra work. He did not say that it was because he remembered the foreigner bringing in an overcoat, a watch and a ring on separate days. Saying it would not have got him anywhere.

In December the port closed.

The owner took the notebook down from the shelf and opened it for the first time. Below the few lines of foreign writing he wrote one line in his own hand. What he wrote was not an amount. It was what he had lost that month, and what was left. Only that.

Four years later, two of the three places burned.

One remained.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The next time he opened the notebook was the winter of 1948.

The owner added one more line. Below it there was still a good deal of blank paper.

When he had finished he closed the cover and rested his hand on it for a while.

Only then did he understand what the foreigner had been doing.

It was not a notebook for writing amounts in.


# Chapter One: The Baton

The wind changes character after two o'clock.

In the morning it is only cold, but past noon it begins to carry the smell of dry earth. It comes down off Akagi. Shuji Kamiya shifted the traffic baton from his right hand to his left and stood at the entrance of the fourth car park of the shopping centre. The heat pad stuck inside his coat had stopped working three hours ago.

It was Saturday afternoon and the cars did not let up.

"I'm sorry, we're full here. Please go around to the multi-storey on the south side."

A white minivan lowered its window, the man in the driver's seat clicked his tongue, and turned away. Kamiya bowed. Bowing was part of the job, so he did not take offence. That it did not offend him still surprised him occasionally.

A hundred and twenty cars an hour. Ninety minutes' average stay. Three floors full, so there were at least two thousand people inside the building. Put the average spend at three thousand two hundred yen and—

He did not intend to count. He counted anyway. Thirty years in the lending department. When he saw a number, the accounts behind it stood up by themselves. The money was gone. Only the habit had stayed.

A little before three a single kei car dropped out of the queue. A woman of about eighty was driving. She tried to get into an empty bay, cut the wheel three times, and came out crooked all three times. A car waiting behind her sounded its horn.

Kamiya lowered the baton and walked over to the driver's window.

"Full lock to the right. Now straight back, slowly. You've got room. You've still got room."

As he spoke he held up a palm to the car behind. On the fourth attempt the car went in. The woman lowered her window and bowed several times. Kamiya bowed too. The job of a car park attendant is not to park the car; it is to make the car behind wait. That much he had learned in a year.

At the three o'clock break he went back to the staff room behind the service corridor. He bought a hundred-and-thirty-yen coffee from the machine and sat down on a folding chair. Matsumoto, on the same shift, put his phone face down. Sixty-two, and he had started here last year.

"Kamiya-san, you were at a bank, weren't you."

"That was a long time ago."

"This new tax-free investment scheme. Is it too late for someone my age? I've still got the younger one at university."

Kamiya drank a mouthful of coffee. The can was hot and it stung his fingertips where he had taken the glove off.

"The same amount, on the same day, every month. That's all."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Matsumoto said, "Is that really how it works," and laughed. Kamiya laughed too.

That was the one thing he had not been able to do.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He clocked off at five and drove home in his kei car. A builder's house on the edge of Takasaki, twenty-six years old. The loan had finished last summer. When it finished, Kayoko had cooked red rice.

Opening the postbox beside the gate had become the habit of the past year. Once in the morning, once when he got home. He looked twice.

Last February a securities company envelope had reached Kayoko first and she had said, "What's this." *Company paperwork*, he had answered, and that had been the end of it. It had been the end of it, but after that Kamiya had started getting to the postbox before his wife. The annual transaction statement usually arrives at the end of January. This year it had not come yet. That it had not come yet had been caught in his throat for two weeks.

In April 2025, Kamiya had sent twenty-eight million yen to a firm called Arclight Capital. Generative AI, they said, trading automatically in markets around the world, twenty-four hours a day. The account manager's Japanese was good and once a month a PDF report arrived. The numbers always went up a little. In October he applied to withdraw part of it and was told a tax procedure was required. In November they asked for a further two million four hundred thousand to cover the cost of that procedure. Kamiya did not pay it. Because he did not pay it, the account was frozen.

It had been most of his retirement money.

Kayoko believed it was in a fixed-term deposit.

A brown parcel was sticking out of the mouth of the postbox.

It was not a securities envelope. Kamiya took a breath before pulling it free. It was wrapped in two layers of newspaper and tied crosswise with paper cord. It was a very old way of tying a parcel. Not a courier label — a post office slip, stuck on one side.

The sender's box was blank.

Only the address was written, in fountain pen, in a hand that rose to the right. Takasaki City, Gunma — the block, the number, *Mr Shuji Kamiya*. The house number was correct.

The postmark said February 10. Ugano Post Office.

Ugano.

Thirty years, and he had not said the name of the place out loud.

The branch building had stood two streets in from the station. Two storeys, with tall narrow windows, and in winter the dust blew in off the mulberry field behind it so the glass was always clouded white. His desk had been third from the right along the counter.

He remembered. He remembered it in detail.

He remembered, and yet since leaving he had never once driven past it. It would be more accurate to say that he had chosen roads that did not go past it.

Kamiya stood at the gate for a while holding the parcel. The wind came in at the back of his neck.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"What's that?"

Kayoko put her head out of the kitchen while he was taking his shoes off in the entrance. Kamiya kept the parcel under one arm and put the shoehorn back.

"Company paperwork. Year-end."

"A car park has paperwork?"

"Safety management."

Kayoko said, "Hm," and went back to her pot. A man gets good at lying in a year, Kamiya thought. And there is no one he can tell that he has got good at it.

Mai rang partway through dinner. Kayoko put her on speaker, so Kamiya could hear. Five minutes about the grandson running a temperature, and then Mai's voice dropped a register.

"So. About the house."

They had been to see a second-hand flat, she said. Eight minutes from the station, fifteen years old, forty-two million. Put eight million down and the monthly repayment would be the same as their rent now.

"What do you think, Dad?"

"Fifteen years old — look at the history of the repair reserve fund. There'll be an increase scheduled."

"Spoken like a banker."

"Former."

After the call ended, Kayoko stacked the rice bowls and said:

"Eight million. We couldn't manage that, could we."

"…"

"When does that deposit mature again?"

"Year after next."

"I see."

Kayoko went to the sink. Watching her back, Kamiya set down his chopsticks. A single shred of spring onion was stuck to the rim of the miso soup bowl, and he looked at it for a while.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

After eleven he went up to the six-mat room on the first floor. It was halfway between a store room and a study, with the box of tax returns, the textbooks his daughter had left behind, and a set of golf clubs he had never used.

He cut the paper cord with scissors.

Two layers of newspaper. When he opened them out it was a local paper from June 2019. If you only want to wrap something, that day's paper will do. Someone had kept this one for nearly seven years.

What came out was a hand-bound notebook.

About twenty-four centimetres tall. The cover was dark blue with the colour worn off the four corners. There were two kinds of binding thread. The original had broken and been restrung twice. When he touched it, the paper seemed to cling to his fingers.

Kamiya opened it on his knees.

In the upper right of the first spread, a single character had been written in ink.

　　土

Below it, several lines. Not Japanese. Angular letters, running from right to left. Kamiya could not read them. He could not read them, but he could see that whoever wrote them had not been in a hurry. The characters were even.

From the next line it became Japanese. The hand had changed.

　　Showa 16, twelfth month　the port closed　I had put the goods in three places so two remained

Below that, another hand again. Pencil this time.

　　Showa 23　the black market　they went in the order of those who had staked on a single article　I sold rice　I am alive

Fountain pen. Blue-black ink.

　　Showa 48　the paper vanished　I sold everything on the shelves　only those with three things to sell could sell

Ballpoint.

　　Heisei 3　I held only land　I held nothing but land

The Heisei 3 line ended there. Something had been started on the next line and scratched out.

Kamiya turned the page.

In the upper right of the next spread there was a different character.

　　種

Below it too, hands from different periods stood one under another. Turning further: 木, 蜜, 鍵, 手 — tree, honey, key, hand. There were seven characters in all. On none of the pages was there a single amount of money. Only what had been lost in what year, in one line or two. There was nothing to say who had written it either.

The quality of the paper changed from page to page. Some of it was coarse *washi*, some of it the rough ruled stock of the post-war years, and toward the back it looked as though sheets had been cut from a commercial notebook and stitched in. Every few decades somebody had added what was missing. Added, and closed it again.

*A ledger,* Kamiya thought.

A ledger is a thing you write amounts in. And yet there was no other word for it.

There was no debit and no credit. There was no balance. And still, every single line was about money.

He went back to the 土 page.

Below the Heisei 3 line was writing in blue ballpoint that looked as though it had not long dried. It was newer than any of the other hands, and it was the only line in the book written in the Western calendar.

　　February 10, 2026　I divided it in three　there is one more　I could not divide it

February 10. The same day as the postmark.

Kamiya read the line three times. Reading it three times did not add anything to it.

He turned on. He came to a spread with the single character 水 — water — and nothing else.

Below it was white. The next page, and the one after that, were white to the end. No one had written anything there.

Kamiya closed the ledger, and then opened it again. He laid the pad of his finger on the line in blue ballpoint.

*—there is one more. I could not divide it.*

Downstairs, Kayoko turned over in her sleep. Outside, the wind rattled the shutter once.


# Chapter Two: The Postmark

He noticed the white envelope the next morning.

Sunday, February 15. Kamiya got up at six and went up to the six-mat room on the first floor. The ledger was still on the desk where he had left it the night before.

He was folding up the newspaper it had been wrapped in when the envelope fell out.

A long white office envelope. It had been hidden in the thickness of the notebook and he had missed it. Nothing written on the front. Nothing on the back. It was sealed.

He cut the end with scissors.

Three sheets of writing paper inside. Ordinary ruled paper, the kind sold anywhere.

The hand was the same as the address. Fountain pen, rising to the right.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

　　Mr Shuji Kamiya

　　Forgive me for writing so suddenly.
　　It has been thirty years. I do not know whether you remember me.

　　If this has reached you, I am already dead.

　　If I died of illness, treat this letter as an old man fussing, and throw it away.

　　If I died falling down the stairs, it was not an accident.

　　What I have enclosed is something I received from another person seven years ago. I decided last autumn who to pass it on to. I will not write my reasons. If I wrote them, I think you would stop reading.

　　A lawyer named Tachibana will be in touch.
　　Whether you take it on is yours to decide. But before you answer that you will not, come to Ugano once.

　　For thirty years I have said nothing from my side.
　　You said nothing either. I think that was the right way.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　Soichiro Tono

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The third sheet was blank.

Kamiya went back to the first sheet and read it again.

Soichiro Tono.

The name came at once. A name he had not spoken in thirty years came up from nothing more than seeing it written. There seemed to be a place set aside for it somewhere in his head.

The man who had been president of Tono Precision.

What came after that, he did not let himself think about. He had been doing that for thirty years.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The smell of miso soup came up from downstairs.

Kamiya put the letter back in the envelope and wrapped it with the ledger in a cloth. Then he went down to fetch yesterday's paper.

"You're up early."

Kayoko said it from the kitchen.

"I woke up."

"On a Sunday."

Kamiya turned the pages. Nothing on the prefectural page that fitted. The paper from two days ago had already been thrown out.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The light on the answering machine was on.

Almost everything that came to the landline was a sales call. Kamiya listened to it about once a month. He pressed the button. Two messages.

The first was a roof inspection company.

The second was from just after four o'clock the previous afternoon.

　　*—This is Tachibana, of the Ugano Central Law Office. I am calling Mr Shuji Kamiya in connection with Mr Soichiro Tono. I would be grateful if you could return my call.*

She gave a number and rang off. The voice was level. It was the speech of someone who does not state her business first.

Kamiya wrote the number down. Having written it down, he did not call.

At four o'clock yesterday afternoon, this person had telephoned him. The ledger had gone into the postbox about two hours after that.

The order was wrong. The lawyer had come before the letter.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

After breakfast, Kamiya went to the municipal library.

There is a newspaper section on the first floor with bound local editions and a database terminal. He only had to be shown how to use the terminal once. A man who has spent thirty years looking things up has an instinct for a search.

*Ugano City. Fall. Death.*

On the prefectural page of the February 14 edition there was a single-column item.

　　At about 8.40 a.m. on the 13th, at a private house in —— , Ugano City, a company
　　director aged 78 who lived at the address was found collapsed at the foot of the
　　stairs by his eldest daughter. The man was already dead. According to Ugano
　　police, the only injury was to the head and there was no sign of disturbance inside
　　the house. Police believe he fell accidentally.

No name. In an accident, a local paper will sometimes withhold the name at the family's request.

Kamiya looked at the date of the article. Found on the 13th.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

At the terminal, Kamiya took out the envelope.

He looked at the postmark.

　　UGANO　26.2.10　16–18

It had crossed the counter at Ugano Post Office on February 10, between four and six in the afternoon.

The body had been found on the morning of the 13th. Allowing for how long he had lain there, he had died on the 11th or the 12th.

Posted on the 10th.

This man had died in exactly the way he described, the day after he sent the letter, or the day after that.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya took the letter out again and read the one line.

　　*If I died falling down the stairs, it was not an accident.*

To read a piece of writing is to read what the person writing it had in mind. He had done that with credit memos.

This man had set down two ways of dying, side by side. Illness, and the stairs.

When a man of seventy-eight imagines his own death, he thinks of illness first. What comes after is the car, or the bath, or the brain, or the heart. The stairs are not in that list.

Only a man who has a particular staircase in front of him every day puts the stairs second.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was not quite ten when he left the library.

In the car, Kamiya took out his phone and held it for a while.

Calling the police was an option. *I have a letter*, he could say.

But what would he say after that. A man I dealt with once, thirty years ago, sent me a letter the day before he died, and he got his own manner of death right. That was all of it. The letter said nothing about who, and nothing about why.

He did not call.

Instead he went home and said to Kayoko:

"I'm going out today."

"Where?"

"Ugano."

Kayoko looked at him with the cooking chopsticks still in her hand.

"Ugano. That's where you used to be."

"That's right."

"What for?"

Kamiya had never once said the name of that place in this house. That he had never said it meant that his wife knew it too.

"To pay my respects."

Having said it, he realised he did not know where he was supposed to go.

He got into the car and called the number on the memo. A woman answered on the third ring.

"Ugano Central Law Office."

"My name is Kamiya. You called me yesterday."

Mitsuko Tachibana gave her name, and then left a short pause.

"I had begun to think you would not call."

"What is this about?"

"I would rather tell you in person."

The funeral service had finished that morning, she said. The family were at Soichiro's house. She gave him the address. It was in the centre of Ugano.

"Would today be all right?"

"Today would be better, I think."

It was not a lie. He had only chosen a way of putting it that was not a lie.


# Chapter Three: The Eleventh

The road into Ugano was the same as it had been thirty years ago.

Off the national route onto the prefectural road, over the river, north through the fields. The width of the road and the angle of the bends were exactly as he remembered. What had changed was that the signboards that used to stand out in the paddies were all gone, and that the agricultural co-op building was new.

Coming into the area in front of the station, Kamiya slowed down.

On the corner two streets in there was a three-storey building. The ground floor was an ATM lobby and the bank's name was on the glass. Joshu Akagi Bank, Ugano Branch.

He drove past.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Soichiro Tono's house was toward the south of the town centre.

A new single-storey house. It looked as though it had been built about twenty years ago. The black and white mourning curtain had already been taken down from the gate. Four cars were parked outside, one of them a light truck with *Tono Construction* on the side.

When he slid open the front door, the smell of incense came out.

Several people in black were going back and forth along the hallway. Kamiya gave his name and said, "I was at the Ugano branch thirty years ago."

"You're from the bank?"

The woman who came out was about forty. Still in mourning dress, with an apron over it.

"I'm Chizuru. Tono's eldest daughter."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He was shown into the tatami room.

The altar was already half cleared: the plain wood memorial tablet, the photograph, and the chrysanthemums were left. The Soichiro Tono in the photograph had a gentler face than Kamiya had expected. The face he had seen once, thirty years ago, had had more edges to it.

He lit incense.

When he turned around there were three people in the room.

One was a man in his mid-forties. His mourning tie was slightly crooked. He sat cross-legged on a cushion and put his phone face down.

One was a woman of about thirty. The mourning dress fitted her. She sat without letting her knees go slack, counting the visitors' teacups.

The last was a man past seventy, not in mourning dress but in a navy work jacket. He was watching the incense smoke.

Chizuru introduced them in turn.

"My brother, Ryosuke."

Ryosuke said, "Hello," from where he sat, then stood up, then sat down again. He seemed unable to decide which it should have been. He shifted his knees twice.

"Sagara-san, from the company."

"Misaki Sagara."

Kamiya's hand stopped short of his teacup.

Sagara.

Thirty years ago, that name had been in the preparer's box on a trial balance, stamped with a small seal chipped at one corner. It was the name of the man who had stood behind Kamiya the whole time he was counting the racks in the warehouse.

*A common enough surname*, he thought. There would be any number of them in Gunma, and in Saitama.

He thought it, and asked nothing.

Having given her name, Misaki took one cup away and carried it toward the kitchen. Nobody had asked her to.

"My father's younger brother, Katsumi."

Katsumi stood up and bowed. A sunburnt face with deep lines at the corners of the eyes. His hands were folded in front of him. The knuckles were thick.

"Thank you for coming all this way."

He was the only one of them looking straight at Kamiya. The other three met his eyes and went back to their hands at once.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Out in the hallway a woman who seemed to be a neighbour said, "Are there another three cushions anywhere?"

"In the cupboard. Bottom right."

Chizuru answered without turning around.

Ryosuke asked the room in general, "Which way's the toilet again?"

"Out of the hall, far end on the left."

Chizuru answered, again without turning.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Chizuru poured tea.

"How did you know my father?"

Kamiya chose his words.

"We dealt with each other once, thirty years ago. On business."

"You were with the bank."

"Yes. I've been retired some time."

Chizuru said, "I see," and did not ask anything further. *A person who doesn't ask,* Kamiya thought. Not out of indifference. She knew what asking would cost the person she asked.

"Where did it happen? Your father."

That was the question he had come to ask.

"At the old house," Chizuru said. "There's an old place up north of here, out in the hamlet. My mother's tablet was in the family altar room there."

"Was he living at the old house?"

"No. He lived here. The old house is empty."

Chizuru went on setting out the cups.

"My father always went to the old house on the eleventh."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya's hand stopped on the way to his cup.

"The eleventh — meaning?"

"My mother's monthly memorial day. She died on June 11, 2019. Every month after that, on the eleventh."

"Every month?"

"Every month. Rain or snow, he went. So that day as well."

Chizuru's hands did not stop. She set five cups out on the tray and squared up their positions.

"I understand he was found on the 13th."

"Yes. I found him."

"From the 11th to the 13th—"

"I called on the 12th. He didn't pick up. That happened sometimes. His hearing had gone."

Chizuru picked up the tray and stood.

"I went the next morning."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Misaki came back from washing the cup she had taken away. She knew where the kitchen was as well.

Ryosuke's eyes followed her back.

"Sagara-san."

"Yes."

"You're from the company, right."

"Yes."

"Should you be sitting in the family seats?"

The sound in the room cut out for a moment.

Misaki said, "I'm sorry," got off the cushion and sat down again on the bare tatami. That was all.

Chizuru started to say something and stopped. Katsumi said, low, "Ryosuke."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Ryosuke turned his phone over and said:

"Kamiya-san, was it?"

"Yes."

"Did the old man talk about money? Thirty years ago."

"It was a lending matter."

"Huh. How much?"

Misaki set down a cup with a little more sound than necessary. Ryosuke shut his mouth.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Just after four there were voices at the entrance.

A woman with a black document case came in. She looked about fifty-five, and she was not in mourning dress but in a dark navy suit. Chizuru went out to meet her, exchanged two or three words, and then looked back into the room.

"Tachibana."

The woman gave her name and sat down in front of Kamiya.

"I telephoned you."

"You did."

"I had begun to think you would not come."

Mitsuko Tachibana spoke without taking anything out of her case.

"I am holding Mr Soichiro Tono's notarial will. As the family are all here, I will now go through its contents."

The air in the room changed slightly. Ryosuke straightened where he sat.

"Before that, one point for Mr Kamiya."

Tachibana turned to him.

"You have been named as executor of the will."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Nobody said anything.

Ryosuke said, "What?" Chizuru stood holding the tray, not moving. Misaki was looking at her own knees. Only Katsumi was looking straight at Kamiya's face.

"I—"

Kamiya said it. His voice came out lower than he expected.

"I have not seen Mr Tono in thirty years."

"I am aware of that."

Tachibana opened the mouth of her case.

"Mr Tono said the same thing."


# Chapter Four: The Three Baskets

Tachibana laid the papers out on the low table.

Four copies of the notarial will. Four copies of a single sheet summarising the estate. And one white envelope, unsealed.

"First, a word about the form."

She handed round the copies of the will.

"This is a notarial will. A notary drew it up with two witnesses present. The testator stated his wishes aloud, the notary took them down, read them back, and both signed and sealed. It is a very difficult form to overturn."

Ryosuke turned a page.

"What's the date on it?"

"February 10, 2026."

Tachibana said it and moved on to the next page.

Nobody wrote the date down. Kamiya did not write it down either.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"The estate is divided into three."

Tachibana pointed at the single sheet.

　　One　Land　　the old factory site (9,800 m²), woodland, three empty houses
　　Two　Business　Tono Precision Co., Ltd., all shares
　　Three　Cash　deposits and listed securities

"The testator's own view was that each of the three came to roughly one hundred million yen."

"Three hundred million."

Ryosuke said it. His voice went up slightly.

"Land a hundred, company a hundred, cash a hundred. Three hundred million, that's what you're saying."

"That was his estimate."

"His estimate? So it isn't?"

"The valuations have still to be fixed. That is what an executor is for."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Next, who takes what."

Tachibana set the white envelope in the centre of the table.

"The testator did not decide the allocation. What he decided was the method."

"The method."

"Yes. I will read Article Three."

She opened the copy.

　　Article 3. The testator does not name in advance the recipient of each of the three
　　properties set out above. The eldest son, Ryosuke Tono, the eldest daughter, Chizuru
　　Tono, and Misaki Sagara shall each write down one only of the three, seal it, and
　　submit it to the executor.

The room went quiet.

"Where two or more name the same property, that property shall pass to none of them, but shall be donated to a public interest foundation. That is the second half of Article Three."

"…What is that supposed to mean."

Ryosuke looked up from the page.

"If we write the same thing, it disappears?"

"It disappears."

"Is a will even allowed to do that?"

"It is valid."

Tachibana was unmoved.

"As long as the testator's intention is clear and it does not offend public policy, he may divide as he likes."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

From the corner of the room Katsumi said, "That's my brother, all right."

Chizuru had the copy on her knees and a pen in her hand. At some point she had started writing in the margin.

Misaki had looked up once, when her own name was read out, and after that kept her eyes down.

"Article Four."

Tachibana went on.

　　The cash shall not be delivered in a lump sum, but shall be paid out at the rate of
　　eight million yen a year over ten years.

Ryosuke put the copy down on the table.

"Not in a lump sum?"

"Not in a lump sum."

"There's a hundred million, or whatever it is, and I get eight million a year?"

"Yes."

Ryosuke looked at the ceiling, and then laughed. There was something damp in the laugh.

"He didn't trust me."

Nobody contradicted him.

"Article Five."

She turned the page.

　　For ten years following the testator's death, the three properties may not be sold
　　between the recipients, merged, disposed of to a third party, or pledged as security.
　　For that period the properties shall be placed in trust, with a trust bank as trustee.
　　Should any one person acquire two or more of them, that person's beneficial interest
　　shall be extinguished.

"Ten years, and you can't move it," Ryosuke said.

"Correct."

"Can't sell it?"

"You cannot sell it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Finally, the statement of wishes."

Tachibana opened the copy at the very back.

"This part carries no legal force. It is a space for the testator to set down how he felt. However — the testator asked, strongly, that it be placed on the first page."

She turned the copy back to the front.

Before the schedule of property. Before the appointment of the executor. Before the three baskets began, there was the line.

　　To anyone who staked it all on one thing, leave not a single yen.

Tachibana stopped reading there.

There was no more. The statement of wishes was that one line.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The incense smoke went straight up.

Chizuru's pen had stopped.

Katsumi sat with his hands folded on his knees, looking at the tatami.

Misaki picked up the cover sheet for the first time and followed the line with her eyes. Then she put it quietly back.

Ryosuke spoke first.

"Who is that about?"

Tachibana closed the copy.

"I only pass on what is written."

"No, but — that's me, isn't it."

He put a hand on the table.

"He's written me down as a gambler. *Staked it all on one thing.* That's what that means, isn't it."

"Ryosuke," Chizuru said. "Not today."

"Yes, today. It's written here."

He tapped the page with his finger. Then, all at once, the strength went out of him and he sat back down on the cushion.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

For a while nobody said anything.

Eventually Ryosuke asked, without looking at Tachibana:

"Can I ask one thing."

"Of course."

"Does this prove the old man was in his right mind?"

Tachibana left a small pause.

"A notary verified his identity. There were two witnesses. It would be difficult to argue that he lacked testamentary capacity."

"That's not what I mean."

Ryosuke kept his eyes on the tatami.

"I'm asking whether a man who writes something like this is in his right mind."


# Chapter Five: 1996

It was past seven in the evening when he left the Tono house.

Tachibana said there was no hurry over whether he would take on the executorship. She also said that inheritance has deadlines. Renunciation, three months. Inheritance tax, ten months. Nothing could begin until an executor was in place.

"Give me a week."

"That will be fine."

Tachibana handed him a card and went.

Kamiya sat in the car for a while without starting the engine.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the way back he drove through the station district.

He stopped the car on the corner, two streets in. Joshu Akagi Bank, Ugano Branch. It was not quite nine at night and only the ATM lobby was lit.

The building had been replaced. Thirty years ago it had been two storeys, with tall narrow windows. There had been a mulberry field behind it, and in winter the dust off the field left the glass clouded white.

His desk had been third from the right along the counter.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the spring of 1996, Kamiya was thirty-seven.

His third year in the lending department. The Ugano branch carried about a hundred and fifty borrowers, and some thirty of them were the small factories clustered south of the station. Subcontractors for car parts, farm machinery, construction plant.

Tono Precision had been the second largest of them.

Five years had gone by since the bubble ended. The companies in this town were still holding on. Holding on, but the way they held on had been going quietly wrong.

The first thing he noticed was the inventory.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Tono Precision's monthly trial balance always came in on time.

For a company that size, that alone was unusual. The figures were careful and the accounts were broken down in detail. Kamiya had thought it was a good company.

In the April balance, inventory was up ten per cent on the previous month.

Sales were flat. Purchases were flat. And still the stock rose. There are any number of explanations. A year-end rush on materials. Forward procurement ahead of a production run. A customer slow to take delivery.

Kamiya knew it was none of them.

Pile up inventory and your cost of sales falls. When cost falls, profit appears. On paper, and only on paper.

In the May balance, the inventory had risen again.

Kamiya went to look at it.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The stock was not in the factory warehouse.

The man who walked him round was the senior managing director. A jacket over his work clothes, about fifty. He stood behind Kamiya the whole time he was counting the racks.

When he had finished counting and turned round, the man was looking at the floor.

"It's short."

When Kamiya said it, the man said, "Yes."

He made no excuse. He did not say why it was short. He only said yes.

Kamiya went back to the branch and wrote the credit memo. It took him three days. On the evening of the third day he put it up to the branch manager.

That same month, head office decided to pull the line.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Tono Precision did not die.

Another regional bank came in, tightened the terms, and carried it. He heard later that the president had covered the bad receivables from the construction company out of his own assets.

In June, the senior managing director died.

Kamiya learned it at the branch morning meeting. The deputy manager said, "Mr Sagara, senior managing director at Tono Precision, has passed away," and nothing else. He did not give the cause. By not giving it, he told everyone.

Kamiya spent that day looking at figures.

He had done the right thing. He had found falsified accounts and reported them. A banker who does not report is not a banker. If he had let those numbers through, the hole would have grown and more people would have gone under. Thirty years on, he had not changed his mind about that.

He had not changed his mind, and still he remembered the exact angle of that man's face when he said yes.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In September the transfer came through.

A branch in Maebashi. Neither promotion nor demotion, an ordinary move. Kamiya handed over his files and left Ugano.

After that he was transferred four more times, spent two years at head office, and retired from a branch in Takasaki.

For thirty years he did not come back to Ugano.

On the expressway or the national route, he chose roads that did not pass through. He never said the name of the place to his wife or his daughter.

Nobody blamed him. Nobody had any reason to. So for thirty years Kamiya had never had to say anything to himself either.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was not quite ten when he got back to the house in Takasaki.

Kayoko was already asleep. Kamiya went up to the six-mat room and undid the cloth.

He took out the ledger and the envelope.

He read the letter again.

　　*For thirty years I have said nothing from my side.*
　　*You said nothing either. I think that was the right way.*

*For thirty years I have said nothing from my side.*

He understood what that line meant now.

Soichiro Tono had known. He had known who stopped the credit. He had known the name, and for thirty years he had said nothing.

*I think that was the right way*, he had written.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya let the letter rest on his knee.

The trial balance had been prepared by the senior managing director. But a managing director does not think of it on his own. That man would not have broken his own careful figures on his own judgement. The man who said yes in the warehouse had made them because he was told to, and to the end he never gave the name of the man who told him.

For thirty years Kamiya had kept himself from thinking about it.

He had been able to not think about it because Soichiro Tono had said nothing.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

*—If this has reached you, I am already dead.*

In the envelope had been a ledger. A notebook with not one amount of money written in it.

This man was not asking him to protect his money. He was calling in a man who had failed to protect his own.

He had named the banker who cut off his company thirty years ago to stand watch over his money after his death.

If it had been spite, it would have been easier.

Kamiya sat for a while without turning off the light.

He had not been looking for a reason to take it on. He already knew that there was not one reason to refuse.


# Chapter Six: The Stakes

On the morning of February 16, Kamiya was in the passenger seat of Maeda Realty's little van.

Maeda was about sixty and talked a great deal while he drove. Third generation in Ugano, he said. When the executor said he wanted to see the properties in order to draw up the schedule of the estate, Maeda had brought the car round without hesitating. It was not kindness; he had worked out that the sale would come his way eventually. Kamiya had spent thirty years watching that kind of kindness.

"Kamiya-san, what figure are you going to put on it?"

"I haven't seen it yet."

"Before you do, let me say this. Since last spring this town has been running a strange temperature."

They turned north off the national route onto the old road. Shuttered shops ran along both sides. A barber, a seal-engraver, a ladies' outfitter. One shop in three had a *TO LET* notice in the window, and the notices had faded in the sun.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The old Tono Precision site was at the northern edge of the town.

Nine thousand eight hundred square metres. Bare ground. The buildings had been demolished in 2015 and nothing had been put up since. Dead winter grass came up to the knee and lay over in one direction when the wind came.

"Two thousand nine hundred and sixty-four *tsubo*," Maeda said, without looking at his notebook. "Now, until March last year that was eight thousand yen a *tsubo*. Nobody buys it. No company is going to build a factory here any more. It's too big for housing."

"And now?"

"Thirty thousand a *tsubo*."

Kamiya laid the figures out in his head. Two thousand nine hundred and sixty-four times thirty thousand. Eighty-eight million, nine hundred thousand.

"Nearly four times. What happened?"

"Data centre."

Maeda pointed north of the site. Three hundred metres away stood two transmission pylons.

"The North Kanto Data Centre Project. The city's been courting it since last spring. It's those pylons that do it. This is the only land around here you can run extra-high voltage into. So the land men started coming."

"The land men."

"Brokers. Out of Tokyo. Going round every landowner in the district."

The wind came at them head-on. Kamiya turned up his collar. Bare ground looks larger than you expect. There is nothing on it to measure by.

"Has the project actually been decided?"

"It has not," Maeda said easily. "If it had, you couldn't buy it at thirty thousand."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The woodland was on a slope five minutes' drive from the site.

"That isn't a mountain," Maeda said, without getting out of the car. "It's a tilted patch of scrub. The boundaries have never been established. It's never been surveyed, so the area is whatever the register says."

"What's it worth?"

"Put down three million. If anyone will have it."

There were three empty houses. One in the town centre, two on the old road. All of them Soichiro Tono had taken twenty or thirty years ago as security against somebody's debt and then simply kept.

Maeda stopped the car outside one of the pair on the old road.

Part of the tiling had come away; a length of guttering hung loose. Newspaper had been pasted over the glass of the front door from the inside. Only the nandina in the garden was red.

"On this one the building is a minus."

"Demolition."

"One and a half million a house. Four and a half for the three. The land, for all three together, is around ten million."

Kamiya wrote it in his notebook. Factory site eighty-nine million. Woodland three million. Empty houses ten million. One hundred and two million in all.

As he wrote it, he noticed that more than eight-tenths of that figure was riding on a rumour.

Maeda produced a key and opened one of the houses on the old road. The door was badly hung and stopped twice on the way.

It was dark inside. A kitchen to the left of the earth-floored entry, a six-mat room to the right. The tatami had been lifted but the cupboard doors were as they had been.

"How long has this been empty?"

"Twelve years."

Maeda stayed on the earth floor, looking in without stepping up.

"It belonged to a man named Seki. He ran a fuel business in the town. Kerosene and propane. Strong in winter, weak in summer, that trade."

"When was it taken as security?"

"2013. Seki borrowed five million from Mr Tono and couldn't repay it. So it was settled with the house."

On the upper shelf of the cupboard a single old calendar was pinned up. It carried the name of a gas company. It had stopped at December.

"What became of Mr Seki?"

"He went to his son's. In Maebashi. He's still alive."

Maeda slid the door shut. It stopped twice on the way closed as well.

"The thing is, Mr Tono never once rented this house out after he took it. Never sold it. He's been paying property tax on it for twelve years."

"Why?"

"Couldn't tell you." Maeda turned the key. "I never asked."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Somebody was living next door to the empty house on the old road.

The nameplate said Okada, and the woman who came out was past seventy. She saw Maeda's face, said "Oh," and then looked at Kamiya.

"And you are, with the Tonos—"

"I've been asked to execute the will."

Okada said, "Ah," and wiped both hands on her apron.

"Poor Mr Tono. My husband hurt his back on the stairs only last year, so it wasn't someone else's business to us."

Kamiya gave her his card. Okada read it through her reading glasses and wiped her hands on the apron again.

"The old house — is that still as it was?"

"I understand it's being cleared out."

"I see. Chizuru-san used to come a lot."

The wind sang in the overhead wires. Okada looked at the sky and said, "Cold today."

"There was a car there the week before he died, too."

"At the old house?"

"Yes. The 9th, was it. In the evening, a young man's car. Not one you see around, so I remember it."

"A young man."

"Young — well, forty-something, I suppose. The son, perhaps? But the son's in Tokyo, isn't he."

Maeda said, beside him, "That'll be Ryosuke-san." Okada nodded vaguely.

"I only saw from a distance. It was just that voices got a bit loud at the door, and that's why I looked. He left again straight away."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Back in the car, Maeda spoke without starting the engine.

"Don't you need to see the old house?"

"I asked to."

He had telephoned Chizuru the day before. When he said that the contents of the old house also had to go into the schedule, there had been a short pause, and then this:

　　*—I'm clearing it out myself. Could it wait until it's tidy?*

"Well, she would," Maeda said, and started the engine. "That house is bad inside. Too much in it. After his wife died, Mr Tono became a man who never threw anything away."

"You've been in?"

"Once, at the end of last year. About the demolition."

Kamiya looked at him.

"I thought Tono Construction handled the demolition."

"We handle the land. What it fetches once it's cleared, that sort of talk. The building side will be Katsumi-san's."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the way back they passed the site once more.

Kamiya had him stop the car and got out alone.

He walked to the southern corner of the bare ground. Under the grass the earth was hard. Concrete footings seemed to be still there, just below the surface. A factory had stood here for thirty years. When Kamiya killed the credit memo in 1996 there had been some twenty people inside that building.

He got that far and stopped walking.

Near the boundary, wooden stakes had been driven in.

He could see four. Squared timber, cut to a point, with the heads painted red. The paint was new. It had been put in this winter at the earliest.

Kamiya crouched and looked at the side of one. In black marker there was something like a reference number, and a date.

　　T-4　2026.1.20

January 20. Three weeks before Soichiro Tono died.

The woodland has no established boundary, Maeda had said. Nor had Kamiya heard anything about the factory site having been surveyed.

He stood up and looked across the ground. There were two more of the same stakes at the northern end.

Somebody was measuring this land.

Back in the car he asked Maeda. Maeda was silent for a while, and then said, "Wouldn't know." It was not the way a man says he does not know.


# Chapter Seven: The Steward

The Tono Precision factory stands south of the town, one street in off the old road.

At nine on the morning of February 17, Kamiya stood at the gate. The lettering on the sign was new; it looked as though it had been repainted last year. Inside the gate stood three light trucks and a row of staff cars. Counting them, there were seventeen.

The sound of the presses carried out into the street.

The office was a single-storey extension in front of the factory. Inside it smelled of a kerosene heater. Four desks. At the one furthest back sat Misaki Sagara.

"We've been expecting you."

She stood and set two files on the table in the meeting corner. The accounts, and the share register. Kamiya had asked only for the accounts.

"I thought you would want the register as well."

"That helps."

When he opened it, every issued share stood in Soichiro Tono's name. Not one had moved since the company was formed in 1962.

"How many employees?"

"Thirty-one. Four are part-time, the rest are permanent."

"Turnover?"

"Two hundred and forty-two million last year. Operating profit eleven million."

"Margin?"

"Four and a half per cent. It's low."

She answered without looking at a sheet of paper. This woman does not only do the accounts, Kamiya thought.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

They showed him round the factory.

Precision sheet metal, and simple press tooling. Two turret punch presses, three brake presses. At the back was an inspection bench where two women were measuring parts with vernier callipers.

"What's the main line?"

"Interior components for construction plant, and housings for medical equipment."

The works manager, a man named Yamane, came over and bowed. Mid-fifties, with an old scar on the back of one hand. When Misaki said, "This is Mr Kamiya, the executor," Yamane said, "Ah."

"About the chairman—"

"I'm sorry for your loss."

"It still hasn't landed on us, to be honest. He was in here every morning."

Yamane pointed to the side of the factory entrance. A single folding chair stood against the wall.

"He'd sit there for a couple of hours. Never said anything. Now and again he'd correct the way somebody held a caliper, and go."

"How long did he keep coming onto the floor?"

"Until last month."

Yamane said it, and then laughed a little.

"At seventy-eight."

They went round to the inspection bench.

Two women were closing the callipers on a part and writing in a sheet, over and over. There was no wasted movement in it. When Kamiya came near, one of them bowed without stopping her hands.

"The chairman stood here?"

"He stood here," Yamane said. "Stood and watched. Never said a word. Two or three times a year he'd correct the angle."

"The angle."

"On the caliper. Hold it too shallow and you read a few hundredths under. Everybody goes shallow once they get used to it."

Yamane showed the angle with his own fingers.

"When you measure every day, bit by bit you start only thinking you're measuring. That's what he came in to correct."

It was the same with a credit memo, Kamiya thought. The man who wrote it is not lying. He has only, bit by bit, started thinking he is looking.

"Have you been here long, Mr Yamane?"

"Thirty-two years."

Thirty-two. From 1994.

"Then—"

"Yes."

Yamane looked at Kamiya's face. He said nothing more. By not saying it, he was saying that he knew.

"Those were hard times."

That was all he said, and he went off to straighten the stock shelves beside the bench.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Ryosuke came just before noon.

Black puffer jacket and trainers; he slid the office door open and said "Hello" in the same movement. The part-time clerk looked up and then looked at Misaki. Nobody stood.

"Heard you were here, Kamiya-san."

"I'm drawing up the schedule."

"What are the shares worth?"

That was the end of the greetings.

"I have an accountant preparing the valuation. Somewhere around ninety-eight million."

"Ninety-eight million."

Ryosuke put a hand on the corner of the table and looked round the office. Calendar, safety slogans, certificates. High on the wall hung an old photograph in a frame: men in work clothes lined up in front of the factory, in black and white.

"The shares go to the eldest son. That's how it works, normally."

Misaki did not answer. Kamiya answered.

"The will does not say that. One of the three, each, for three people."

"Right, so I write down the business."

"You are free to write what you like."

Ryosuke said, "There you are," and looked at Misaki for the first time.

"You'll take the cash, won't you, Sagara-san."

Misaki was squaring up the delivery notes in front of her. When she had squared them she looked up.

"What I write, I will not say until it is sealed."

"That's how it is, is it."

"Those are the rules. They're in the will."

Ryosuke laughed and went out of the office.

After the door slid shut, the part-time clerk asked Misaki, quietly, "Who was that?"

"The chairman's son."

"Oh."

From the way she said it, Kamiya understood. A woman who had worked in this factory for more than twenty years did not know the president's son by sight.

Misaki came out of the office carrying two cups. Kamiya took one and drank it standing with his back to the factory wall.

"How long have you been here, Sagara-san?"

"Seven years."

"And before that?"

"Accounts work, in Tokyo."

"What brought you here?"

"I saw the advertisement."

The answer came fast. Too fast, Kamiya thought.

"A bit of luck, then."

"Yes."

Misaki held her cup in both hands and looked into the factory. The presses sounded at an even interval.

"Do you think the chairman meant to give me the company?"

"The will is written in a way that can be read like that."

"Read like that. That is all it is."

She said:

"Whether I write down the business, I have not decided."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the afternoon he went through the personal papers at the back of the office.

Not the company's — Soichiro's own. Misaki brought two cardboard boxes out of the bottom of a cabinet. He had kept his papers half here and half at home.

There were seven passbooks. Two regional banks, a credit union, the post office, and three older books. Kamiya went down the printed lines, working from the newest.

To read a passbook is to read a person's life. Electricity, gas, property tax, the co-op, the newspaper, the direct debit for the barber. The grave maintenance fee twice a year. The hospital once a month.

Among them was a transfer on the twenty-fifth of every month.

　　　TRANSFER　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　80,000

Nothing in the description field. It ran back three years unbroken. Going as far as the oldest book, the amount was different. Until 2016 it was fifty thousand. Before that there was no book.

Kamiya asked Misaki whether the remittance slips had been kept.

"If you mean the copies of the transfer forms, they're in that brown envelope."

She opened her own desk drawer and took out a bundle held with a rubber band. Soichiro had entrusted even the records of his private remittances to this woman.

Kamiya took off the rubber band.

He looked at the top copy. Dated last month, January 25. Eighty thousand yen.

In the box for the recipient, a name was written.

　　SAGARA MISAKI


# Chapter Eight: The Shortfall

On February 18, Kamiya spent the morning going round the banks.

Tachibana had put together the papers proving his authority as executor. A certified copy of the notarial will, Soichiro's closed family register, Kamiya's own seal certificate. Every counter was courteous, and every counter took its time.

For thirty years he had sat on the other side. Seen from the customer's chair, the counter was much further away than he had thought.

By three in the afternoon he had the balance certificates.

　　Joshu Akagi Bank, Ugano Branch　　　　　　　21,400,000
　　Gunma Chuo Credit Union, Head Office　　　　　9,800,000
　　Japan Post Bank　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　3,100,000
　　Kita-Kanto Securities, Ugano (listed shares)　　37,700,000

Seventy-two million in all.

Kamiya laid the four sheets side by side on a coffee-shop table and added them twice. Both times it came out the same.

The Ugano branch of Joshu Akagi Bank stood two streets in from the station.

The building had changed. Where there had been two storeys, there was a new three-storey branch. The mulberry field had gone; it was a coin car park now.

He went in and took a ticket. On the lobby wall, portraits of past branch managers hung in a column. The fourth from the top was a face he knew. The deputy manager in those days — the man who had stamped Kamiya's credit memo. The dates under the photograph said 2009 to 2012.

It was forty minutes before his number was called.

At the securities company he was given the breakdown of the shareholdings. The thirty-seven million seven hundred thousand in listed shares was two electric utilities, a railway, a local food company and two of the big trading houses. Every one of them held for more than ten years. Five years had seen only four trades.

It was an account that did nothing but collect dividends. It did not grow dramatically; nor did it shrink. Kamiya had met many people with accounts like this. As a rule, they were careful people.

Which made it harder to understand.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He telephoned Tachibana's office.

"Ms Tachibana. What figure did you have in mind for the cash basket?"

"One hundred million."

She answered at once.

"On what basis?"

"A note Mr Tono wrote himself last October. He gave it to me when we were drafting. *Land one hundred million, business one hundred million, cash one hundred million.* He wanted the three split so that each came to about a hundred million."

"May I see the note?"

"You should already have it. I put it in with the papers for the schedule."

It was in his bag. A single sheet of B5 writing paper. Soichiro's hand.

　　Land　　　one hundred million
　　Business　one hundred million
　　Cash　　　one hundred million

Dated October 14, 2025.

"In fact it's seventy-two million."

"…A difference of twenty-eight million."

Tachibana's voice dropped a little. It was the first time Kamiya had heard her lower it.

"He may have sold shares. Though it is not an amount he would need to live on."

"I'll look into it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The next day he went back to the Ugano branch of Joshu Akagi Bank.

An executor may ask for disclosure of the deceased's transaction history. Kamiya had once been on the receiving end of such requests. He remembered the bank's internal rules on what to give and what to hold back. So he also knew what to ask for to get it fastest.

"Deposits and withdrawals, January to December 2025."

It came out in thirty minutes. Nine sheets of A4.

He read them on the sofa beside the counter.

January to July, nothing. Pension, utility bills, director's pay from the company.

In August there was a single line of a different order of magnitude.

　　2025-08-06　　　OVERSEAS REMITTANCE　　　　　28,000,000

Twenty-eight million yen.

Kamiya's finger stopped on that line.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It took another hour to get the copy of the remittance application. The branch manager came out and checked with someone above him. Thirty years ago, Kamiya had done the same thing.

The copy was on thin carbon paper.

The beneficiary was an account at a bank in Hong Kong. The account name was a company name in Roman letters ending in LIMITED. In the box for the purpose of the remittance it said only *Investment*.

The applicant's signature was in Soichiro Tono's hand. It did not shake.

"Did the counter ask him anything?"

The branch manager chose his words.

"It was a large overseas remittance, so we did confirm. He said himself that it was an investment."

"Did you stop him?"

"…We are not in a position to stop a customer."

Kamiya nodded. He had given the same answer more than once himself.

"Did he say anything about who introduced it?"

The manager left a small pause.

"He said it was an introduction through his daughter's family."

Before leaving the branch, Kamiya looked at the statement once more.

After the overseas remittance on August 6, nothing happened on the account in September or October. The pension went in, the utilities went out, and that was all. No shares sold. No sign of having consulted anyone.

From the month after he sent the twenty-eight million, Soichiro Tono's account had gone back to moving exactly as it had before.

It looked as if nothing had happened. It might be closer to say that he had decided nothing had happened.

On October 14 this man had written *Cash one hundred million* on a sheet of paper. Two months after sending twenty-eight million out of the country.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

When he came out, the sun was going down.

Kamiya sat in the car without starting the engine for some time. The carbon copy lay on his knee.

Twenty-eight million.

The same amount he had sent to Arclight Capital.

*Coincidence*, he thought. It's a round figure. A man of this generation putting in most of his retirement money ends up somewhere around there. Not twenty million, not thirty — twenty-eight, an awkward number, is what you get after you subtract what you mean to keep back. That's all it is.

That was all it was, and still his hands would not move.

Soichiro Tono had sent twenty-eight million last August.
Shuji Kamiya had sent twenty-eight million last April.

In the same year, two men had sent the same amount. One was seventy-eight and one was sixty-six. One had run a factory for thirty years and one had examined loans for thirty years.

Neither had been able to stop himself.

There was one more thing that stayed with him.

*An introduction through his daughter's family.*

That was what the manager had said. Chizuru's husband was in the business of brokering financial products. Kamiya had not met him. He had only seen the name, in the relatives column of the schedule. Makoto Kajiwara.

One phone call would settle it. He could ask Tachibana. He could get the records of who Soichiro's mobile had been in contact with around last August. An executor could do that.

Kamiya took out his phone, and put it back in his jacket.

An executor's job is to establish the estate and divide it as the will says. It is not to find out where it was lost. Soichiro had sent the twenty-eight million of his own free will. The papers said so.

He thought that, and knew at the same time that it was running away.

If he asked, there would be an answer. If there was an answer, he would have to tell Chizuru. *Your husband—*

Not yet.

How many cases he had put off with those words over thirty years, he could not remember. The things he put off usually came back later, doubled.

Kamiya put the carbon copy back in its envelope and the envelope in his bag. When he shut the bag, the clasp made a louder sound than he expected.


# Chapter Nine: The Sealed Paper

At two in the afternoon on February 19, five people gathered in the meeting room of the Ugano Central Law Office.

Mitsuko Tachibana, Ryosuke Tono, Chizuru Tono, Misaki Sagara, and Kamiya. Tachibana took the short end of the long table, with Ryosuke and Chizuru on one side and Misaki facing them. Kamiya sat beside Tachibana, his chair pulled back a little.

This was the second time he had seen the three of them in one room. The first had been the day the will was read, when nobody spoke.

"Today I will explain how the choice is to be made."

Tachibana handed out three copies. The statement of wishes, and Articles Three to Six of the will.

"You will each write down one of the three. Land, business, or cash. Seal it and give it to me. The deadline is the forty-ninth day after his death — the Buddhist memorial. March 31."

"One," Ryosuke said. "What if I write two?"

"It is void."

"What if we all talk it over and decide together?"

"You may. It is not forbidden."

Tachibana went on, level.

"However, if two or more people write the same property, it becomes no one's. It goes as a donation to the foundation."

The room went quiet.

"…What is that," Ryosuke said. "Write the same thing and it just vanishes?"

"It vanishes."

"If all three of us write land—"

"The land goes to the foundation. You would then have to agree between you on the other two, but the will makes no provision for that, so the agreement would not be simple."

Ryosuke put his copy on the table and rubbed his face with both hands.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Sagara-san."

Misaki looked up. It was the first time that day anyone had said her name.

"One point to confirm," Tachibana said. "You are not a statutory heir. What you receive under this will is a bequest."

"Yes."

"For inheritance tax, anyone who is not a parent, child or spouse pays a twenty per cent surcharge."

"Yes."

Misaki was answering with her eyes on the paper.

"Excuse me."

After a pause, Misaki spoke.

"Am I entitled to receive this at all?"

Before Tachibana could answer, Ryosuke snorted.

"Bit late for that."

"It isn't late." Misaki did not look at him. "I am not a Tono. I am sitting here because the chairman wrote my name. That is all."

"If it is entitlement you are asking about," Tachibana said, "so long as the will is valid, you have the right. You may decline it yourself, but what you decline passes to the two statutory heirs."

Misaki was quiet for a moment and then said, "I understand." She did not say she would decline.

Chizuru took notes throughout.

It was the way someone who does admin at a care home writes. The date at the top, numbered items, what was said written down short, exactly as said. No opinions of her own. From where Kamiya sat he could not see the words, but he could tell from where her pen stopped.

"Is there any point you writing all that down?"

Ryosuke said it. Chizuru answered without stopping her hand.

"I'll have to explain it later."

"To who?"

"The children. Uncle as well."

Ryosuke opened his mouth, then shut it again.

That was the first time that day Kamiya had seen the two of them speak to each other as brother and sister. They were four years apart, he had heard. When they were children, he thought — stating the obvious to himself — this one would have been the older brother, and that one the younger sister.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Tachibana read Article Four aloud.

The cash basket would not be paid in a lump sum. A trust bank would act as trustee and pay out eight million yen a year over ten years.

"I heard that last time," Ryosuke said. "I heard it, but is it really like that?"

"It is really like that."

"Seventy-two million there, and eight a year."

"Yes."

Ryosuke leaned back in his chair.

"Why would he do that? It means he thinks I'll blow it."

"I cannot tell you what the testator intended."

Something did not line up, Kamiya felt. What did not line up, he could not tell. At the bank, a few times a year, he had had the same feeling looking down a row of figures, and when he did, it was usually a premise one step further back that was wrong.

Tachibana went on to Article Five. For ten years, the three baskets could not be merged or sold between the recipients. Disposal and pledging as security were also prohibited. And — should any one person acquire two or more of them, that person's beneficial interest would be extinguished.

"How would anyone get two?" Chizuru asked. "It's one each."

"By buying one later, or receiving it as a gift. This closes that off."

Chizuru said "Yes" and wrote something in the margin of her copy. Kamiya could not see it from where he sat.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Last, Tachibana read the statement of wishes.

　　To anyone who staked it all on one thing, leave not a single yen.

Read aloud, the line was shorter than he expected. When she had finished nobody said anything. There was only the second hand of the clock on the wall.

"That," Ryosuke said. "Who's that about?"

Tachibana squared up her copies.

"You would have to ask the man who wrote it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

When the explanation was over, the three of them did not get up straight away.

Kamiya was thinking about how to read what was in front of him. The three did not look at each other. They did not look, but they were listening. Every one of them was calculating what the others would write.

The will was forcing the three of them to talk. If they did not talk, they risked writing the same thing and losing it to the foundation. If they talked, they gave away their hands. Soichiro had built that mechanism, and then died.

"The land—"

Chizuru said it. She said it to no one in particular.

"Who in this town would want land?"

Kamiya looked up.

Three days earlier, in Maeda Realty's van, he had heard the figure of thirty thousand a *tsubo*. Nearly four times last spring's. Brokers were going round the landowners, and there were red-headed stakes driven into the bare ground. No one in Ugano who owned property could be unaware of it.

"Chizuru-san," Kamiya said. "It's reached thirty thousand a *tsubo*."

"So I heard."

"That's over a hundred million."

"I heard."

Chizuru said it and reached for her teacup. Her hand stopped. The cup was empty.

"But that place won't sell, will it."

"Let me say one thing."

It was the first time Kamiya had spoken. The three turned to him.

"If the three of you write different things, all three baskets survive. No one loses anything. It only shrinks if two of you overlap."

"So he's telling us to talk it through," Ryosuke said.

"It can be read that way."

"If we talk it through, we have to say what we're going for."

"You do."

Ryosuke drummed on the table with his fingers.

"So what are we supposed to do?"

"I'm the executor. I won't tell you what to write."

"Useless."

"Yes."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Ryosuke laughed.

"She's right, though. Who'd take land you can't sell?"

He picked up his coat and stood.

"So nobody's going to write land."

Chizuru said nothing. Misaki said nothing either.

Tachibana said, "I will prepare the envelopes and give them to you," and laid three white envelopes on the table.

As they were leaving the room, Misaki came up beside Kamiya.

"Mr Kamiya."

"Yes."

"The rule that it goes to the foundation if two of us overlap — is that a threat?"

Kamiya thought for a moment.

"If it were a threat, he wouldn't have named the foundation."

"It's named."

"A foundation in Gunma that supports children's schooling. It exists. Mr Tono chose it himself, at the end of last year."

Misaki said, "I see," and put her arm into the sleeve of her coat.

"So he meant it."

"I think he meant it."

The three envelopes were identical. There was nothing written on the front or on the back.


# Chapter Ten: The Winning Side

Kamiya caught up with Ryosuke just outside the office.

"Ryosuke-san. Could you spare me a little time?"

"I'm heading back to Tokyo."

"I'll come with you."

Ryosuke stopped and looked at him.

"What for?"

"To confirm any advance on the inheritance. If your father gave you anything during his lifetime, it has to be reflected in the schedule."

"He didn't give me anything."

"If he gave you nothing, I write that he gave you nothing. That's part of the job too."

Ryosuke was silent for a while, and then said, "Suit yourself."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Takasaki to Tokyo is fifty minutes.

Ryosuke sat by the window with his phone held upright, moving his thumb. On the screen, thin green and red bars stood in a row. Candlesticks. Now and then his thumb stopped, and then moved again.

"What are you looking at?"

"Bitcoin."

"Is it up?"

"It's down."

Ryosuke turned the screen face down and looked out of the window. The Kanto plain went by, flat.

"Did the old man say anything? About me."

"I hadn't seen him in thirty years."

"Right. So you said."

Ryosuke laughed. The way he laughed was a little like the face in his father's photograph.

"I got five million in 2018."

Kamiya took out his notebook.

"When I went independent. I said I needed it to set up the company, and he gave it to me. I didn't sign anything. I never paid it back."

"What was it used for?"

"Office deposit, building the website, and the rest was living costs. It was gone in two years."

"Anything else?"

"No. That was the last. He said it at the time. *This is once*, he said. *Only once.* I didn't understand what *once* meant, back then."

Kamiya wrote *five million*, and the date.

"This goes into the calculation of the statutory share."

"Meaning my share goes down?"

"Meaning it goes into the calculation, that's all. In this case your share doesn't fall below the statutory share, so in practice it makes no difference."

"Then why write it down?"

"I write it down."

Ryosuke said, "Very thorough," and stood the screen up again.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The flat in Itabashi was eleven minutes' walk from the station.

One bedroom and a living room. Cardboard boxes stacked in the entrance. Four with the tape still uncut. A label for a collagen drink printed on the sides.

"That's from something I used to do."

In the room were three monitors on a desk. Two were black. Only one had a chart moving on it. There was no bed; a futon lay on the floor, unfolded. The curtains were drawn.

Ryosuke sat on the floor and pushed what was on the low table to one side.

"You want to know how much I owe, don't you."

"That's not the executor's business."

"Then what did you come for?"

Kamiya did not answer.

Ryosuke got up and brought a clear plastic folder. He took out the contents and laid them on the table. He did it smoothly. *He has shown these to people many times*, Kamiya thought.

　　Card loans, three companies　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　 4,200,000
　　Margin trading losses (unpaid to securities firm)　　　　7,600,000
　　Top-up margin for crypto (consumer finance)　　　　　　　 5,800,000
　　Borrowed from relatives and acquaintances　　　　　　　　 2,400,000

Twenty million in all.

"Interest?"

"All in, about two hundred and forty thousand a month."

"Income?"

"Three hundred and fifty thousand when there's a job on. Seventy thousand in the months there isn't."

Kamiya looked at the figures. He had seen hundreds of sheets in this shape. There is a shape that can be repaid and a shape that cannot, and this was the one that could not. Unless, that is, a seventy-two-million-yen basket came his way. So he was waiting.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On one of the monitors a chat window was open.

A column of member names ran down the left. At the top it said *TAKA*. The icon was a photograph of a chair standing in a white room.

"What's this?"

"A community. Thirty thousand a month."

"What does it do?"

"Share a view of the market. TAKA runs a model he built with AI and posts the output every morning. You look at that and decide for yourself."

"Members?"

"Two thousand four hundred."

Kamiya did the sum in his head. Thirty thousand a month times two thousand four hundred was seventy-two million a month.

"How much does he make?"

"On the market?"

"No."

For a moment Ryosuke looked as though he did not understand. Then he looked away.

"…Don't look at it like that."

"I'm sorry."

"I've been a sub-leader since last year," Ryosuke went on. "When somebody new joins, I answer their questions. I get part of the fees. Forty or fifty thousand a month."

"Do you recruit?"

"No. I introduce people. Introducing and recruiting aren't the same thing."

Kamiya knew that way of putting it. He had heard it at branch counters and at head office. Cut it up small enough and none of it is wrong.

At the top of the screen was that day's post.

　　**Stand on the winning side, or stand on the losing side and complain. That's the whole choice.**

"Ryosuke-san."

"Yes."

"When your father's estate comes in, what will you do?"

"Pay it all back. And that's the end of it."

"And after that?"

"After that?"

Ryosuke looked genuinely puzzled.

"After that I'm at zero. I get back to zero. That's good enough, isn't it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Leaving, putting on his shoes in the entrance, Kamiya asked:

"Where were you on February 9?"

Ryosuke's hand stopped halfway through passing him the shoehorn.

"The 9th?"

"A Monday."

"Tokyo. Why?"

"The woman next door to the old house says there was a car parked there on the evening of the 9th. A man in his forties, she said."

Ryosuke stood holding out the shoehorn, not moving, for a while. Then he laughed.

"Somebody else, then."

"I see."

"I was in Tokyo. All that week."

Kamiya put on his shoes.

"She said voices got a little loud at the door."

"No idea."

Ryosuke opened the door. One of the fluorescent tubes in the corridor was going, flickering.

"Kamiya-san. You're the executor. You're not a detective."

"That's right."

"Then cut that out."

The door closed.

Outside, the wind came through the alley. Tokyo was a little warmer than Takasaki.

Waiting for the lift, Kamiya opened his notebook and wrote *February 9*. Then, underneath, he added: *Tokyo (his account)*.

For thirty years he had written things that way. *The person says so* does not mean *it is so*.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The train back was nearly empty.

Kamiya sat by the window, looking at his own face reflected in the dark glass.

He tried to think about the difference between Ryosuke and himself.

Ryosuke was in a thirty-thousand-a-month community, looking at predictions an AI produced, being told to stand on the winning side. He himself had sent twenty-eight million to a firm that said an AI traded automatically in markets around the world, twenty-four hours a day.

The only difference was the amount.

There was one more. Ryosuke had something coming in. His father had died and a cash basket would come to him. Eight million a year, for ten years. His repayments were two hundred and forty thousand a month — two million eight hundred and eighty thousand a year. On paper, he would be saved.

He himself had nothing coming.

Somewhere in Saitama the carriage grew brighter. The face in the window vanished and the lights of a car park outside showed through.

A man with something coming to save him, and a man without. The difference came down to the fact that he was, at this moment, the more wretched of the two. But wretchedness warps judgement. For thirty years he had looked at the paperwork of people whose judgement had warped. Before it warped, every one of them had found one thing to count on.

Kamiya pressed a hand to the inside of his jacket. The executor's seal was there.

The estate account was due to be opened at the start of the following week.


# Chapter Eleven: Three Prices

On the morning of February 20, the ground floor of Ugano City Hall was busier than usual.

Kamiya was at the counter of the property tax section, requesting three certificates of assessed value. An executor must draw up a schedule of the estate and deliver it to the heirs and legatees. As Tachibana had told him, he was collecting the documents one sheet at a time and numbering each one.

The clerk said, "Just a moment, please," and disappeared into the back and did not come out again for some time. There were voices from the direction of the lifts. Craning his neck, he saw three men carrying cameras and several reporters with armbands going up the stairs.

On the noticeboard, a single sheet of A4 was held by a drawing pin.

　　Business Location Promotion Division　Press briefing　11.00　Conference Room, 2nd Floor

When he had his certificates, Kamiya went up to the second floor. He had no reason to. He had earned his living for thirty years by the way the air changed in rooms like that, and the habit had not left him.

The door of the conference room was open. He did not go in. He listened from the corridor.

The operator had withdrawn from the project. Two reasons were given. No spare grid capacity could be expected from fiscal 2028 onward. And consent had not been obtained from the local land improvement district for the volume of groundwater to be drawn for cooling. Talks would be opened on an alternative site in Tochigi.

The mayor said, "This is not a cancellation. It is a pause, to take stock."

*A pause.* Kamiya had heard that phrase for thirty years. At the bank they said, *Let us take it away and consider it.* Very few matters taken away ever came back as they were.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Before noon, he called in at Maeda Realty.

Maeda, who had been quoting thirty thousand a *tsubo* the day before, had not been answering his phone all morning. So Kamiya went in person. Maeda was at the back of the office giving instructions to a clerk. When he saw Kamiya he broke off and offered him a chair.

"Well, that's torn it."

Maeda said it first. Kamiya sat without saying anything.

"The price until yesterday was a rumour's price. Not the land's."

"What is today's price?"

"Five thousand a *tsubo*."

Maeda tapped at his calculator and turned it round to face him. "And that's if there's a buyer."

Nine thousand eight hundred square metres. Two thousand nine hundred and sixty-four *tsubo*. At five thousand, fourteen million eight hundred and twenty thousand. "Call it fifteen million," Maeda said. Until yesterday, on the same sheet of paper, it had been eighty-nine million.

"The woodland?"

"That isn't a mountain, it's tilted scrub. One million. If anyone wants it."

"The three empty houses?"

"Four million. Mind you, that's the land, not the buildings. The buildings are a minus. One and a half million a house to demolish."

*Twenty million in all.* Kamiya wrote it in his notebook.

"The factory site still won't sell," Maeda said, cupping his teacup in both hands. "It isn't the price. They used to do plating there, you know. Any buyer's going to insist on a soil survey. Two million just for the survey. If anything turns up, the clean-up adds a digit."

"It won't necessarily turn anything up."

"It won't necessarily not. So nobody measures."

*Nobody measures, so there is no price.* Kamiya had once written exactly that on a credit memo. In the box for collateral valuation: *Cannot be valued.*

"Two thousand nine hundred *tsubo* is the hardest size there is to sell in this town," Maeda said. "Too big for houses. No company's coming to build a factory. So when one big story comes along, everybody jumps on it. If you don't jump, it'll never move at all."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Three in the afternoon. The Ugano Central Law Office.

There were five people at the table. Tachibana, Ryosuke, Chizuru, Misaki, and Kamiya. It was cloudy outside, and though it was not yet four the lights in the room were on.

"One hundred and two million is now twenty million."

Tachibana said it and turned her calculator face down.

"But for the purposes of the statutory share, it remains one hundred and two million."

Ryosuke looked up. "What does that mean?"

"When the statutory share is calculated, property is valued at its market price on the day of death. February 11. On that day, that land was worth one hundred and two million."

"It went to twenty million yesterday."

"Yesterday does not come into it."

Tachibana was unmoved. *This woman is the only kind who does not lower her voice to give bad news*, Kamiya thought.

"Is the tax worked out on one hundred and two million as well?"

Kamiya asked it. Tachibana brought out another sheet.

"No. For inheritance tax the land is valued on the published roadside valuation, or on the assessed value for property tax times a multiplier. That land is in a multiplier zone. The city only revises its assessed value once every three years. The figure in force now was set before the rumour started."

"How much?"

"Thirty-nine million."

On the same page of his notebook, Kamiya wrote three figures one under another.

　　Market　　　　　　　89,000,000　→　15,000,000
　　Inheritance tax　　 39,000,000
　　Statutory share　　102,000,000

One piece of land, and three prices. None of them wrong. The price only changed with who was measuring, and for what. For thirty years he had used all three without a second thought. For the first time now, having used them, he felt something close to nausea.

While Tachibana went on explaining the tax, Kamiya did a different sum in the corner of his notebook.

Land one hundred and two million, business ninety-eight million, cash seventy-two million. Two hundred and seventy-two million in all. There were two children, so Ryosuke's and Chizuru's statutory shares were a quarter each — sixty-eight million. Ryosuke would receive seventy-two million in cash. If Chizuru took the land, on paper she would receive one hundred and two million. Both above the statutory share. The will could not be broken.

Below it he wrote one more line.

If the land were counted at twenty million, the estate would be one hundred and ninety million. A quarter of that was forty-seven and a half million. Whoever took the land could have claimed twenty-seven and a half million from the other two.

Nine days.

If Soichiro Tono had lived nine more days, whoever received the land could have recovered twenty-seven and a half million. He died nine days too early, so they could not. The law stops the clock at the moment of death, and sets the price there. Whatever happens afterwards, it does not start the clock again.

"That date—"

It was Chizuru. Kamiya looked up.

"The date?" Tachibana asked.

"…The day the price is fixed. February 11."

"It does not move. It stops on the day of death."

"I see."

Chizuru lifted her teacup in both hands, moved it a little as though looking for somewhere to put it, and set it down again in the same place. Hardly any of the tea had gone.

"So who's going to pick land now?"

Ryosuke leaned back in his chair and said it. Nobody answered.

"The company," Ryosuke said after a while, turning to Misaki. "Is the company all right? With the project gone — doesn't that hit it?"

"It doesn't."

Misaki answered without opening the file in front of her.

"Our largest customer is eleven per cent of sales. We're spread across twenty-eight companies."

"Twenty-eight? That's inefficient."

"It is. The chairman made it that way. So that no single customer turning us down could kill us."

Ryosuke started to open his mouth and shut it again. Then he turned to Kamiya.

"Kamiya-san. You were a banker, right."

"Yes."

"What should the old man have done? Split it three ways — if one goes under, what's the difference? Three hundred million, and now it's a hundred and ninety."

Kamiya had the answer. For thirty years he had been paid to give people that answer. *If he had not split it, you might have twenty million left now*, he could have said. *If it had all been in one basket, no one could have chosen which basket that would be.*

He could not say it.

He knew what would come next if he did. *And what about you?*

"…I don't think anyone can know," Kamiya said. "You only know afterward."

"You're no use at all."

"No."

Tachibana squared up her papers and confirmed one last thing. The deadline for the three sealed papers was the forty-ninth day. March 31.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Outside the building, in a corner of the car park, a man was on the phone.

He had a down jacket over his suit and one hand on the bonnet of his car. His voice had risen.

"—No, look, the understanding was by the end of March, wasn't it. … We're in a difficult position too. The land is there. It's there. It's just that who holds it hasn't been decided yet."

When Kamiya came near, the man ended the call and put on a face. The speed of the change was the speed of his work.

"How do you do. I'm Chizuru's husband. Kajiwara."

He produced a card. *Kajiwara Financial Planning. Principal.* On the back, four qualifications were listed.

"You were at a bank, Mr Kamiya, I hear. That's reassuring, at a time like this."

"I left a long time ago."

"Not at all. By the way — your own retirement money, is that invested anywhere?"

The wind crossed the car park. Kamiya put the card in the inside pocket of his jacket.

"No. It's in a fixed-term deposit."

"Is it. Shame. A fixed deposit these days, well."

Kajiwara laughed. Kamiya laughed too.

It was the same lie he told his wife. It was the first time he had told it to anyone but her.

Chizuru came out of the office and got into the passenger seat. The two of them said nothing to each other. Kamiya stood there until the car had gone.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He drove the national route back toward Takasaki.

Eighty million in a day.

He himself had lost twenty-eight million over eight months. *The only difference is the speed of the fall*, Kamiya thought. The fast one might even be the more honest. Eight months of a PDF arriving every month with the numbers going up a little each time was much the nastier.

At a red light, the copy of the schedule on the passenger seat caught his eye. A numbered sheet he had only just finished.

　　1　Land　Ugano City, ——　9,800 m²　and others　¥102,000,000

In the eyes of the law, whoever took that land had been given a hundred million.

What they would actually receive was twenty million.

No one had stolen the eighty million difference.

Nothing had been stolen, and it was gone.


# Chapter Twelve: Four Holes

On the morning of February 24, Chizuru phoned.

"It's cleared. You can go in and look."

"Could you come with me?"

"I'm just off a night shift and going to bed. I'll put the key in the postbox."

And she rang off.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The old house was four kilometres north of the town, in a hamlet among the paddies.

There was no gate. The entrance was a gap in the hedge. The garden was large. It looked as though no one had tended it for some years; the pines had grown wild, and only the nandina was red.

In the east corner of the garden stood a corrugated-iron shed. There was no padlock on the door.

The main house looked single-storeyed, with an upper floor at the back. About sixty years old. None of the roof tiles were missing. A single fine crack ran vertically down the mortar wall.

The key was in the postbox, in an envelope. On the front of the envelope was written *Old house — front door*. The hand was careful.

When he slid the door open, cold air came out.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The earth-floored entry was wide. The beaten earth was chipped here and there.

To the left, the kitchen. To the right, a six-mat sitting room. A storeroom at the back. It was tidy. Three cardboard boxes had been pushed into a corner of the entry, labelled on their sides: *Altar room — small items*, *Sitting room*, *Kitchen*. They were not yet sealed. The hand was the same as on the envelope in the postbox.

On the kitchen wall papers were pinned up. Copies of applications to stop the electricity, gas and water. On all three the stop date was February 28. The applications were dated February 16.

On the fifth day after he died, she had already put in the paperwork to shut the house down. *A quick worker*, Kamiya thought. Fourteen years doing the admin at a care home, and perhaps what had to be done after someone was gone was simply in her body.

Kamiya pressed the light switch. The bare bulb over the entry came on. It was dim. He read the number printed on it: forty watts.

The staircase faced him across the entry.

It rose directly from the raised floor at the edge of the beaten earth. He measured the width: seventy-five centimetres. Each step was twenty-three centimetres high. It was a pitch the building regulations would not allow today. Houses of this age built the upper floor as storage. No one was expected to go up every day.

A wall on one side. On the other side, nothing.

Kamiya stopped moving.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

There was no handrail.

In the wall there were four screw holes. A little above waist height, evenly spaced, in a straight line. Around the holes the wallpaper had lifted slightly.

Kamiya crouched and looked at the lowest hole.

It had been pre-drilled. The rail had been added later. Where the screw had come out, the wood fibres were splintered outward. When he touched it, it was rough.

All four holes had their edges crushed in the same way.

*Taken off before the demolition*, Kamiya thought. Anything that sells gets taken off first. Some firms will pay for metalwork and fittings. But nobody pays for a handrail.

*A rough job, all the same*, he thought.

That was all. At the time, he thought no further.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He went up the stairs.

On the third step he put his hand to the wall. The wall was sand-plaster and left a fine powder on his palm. At the fourth step the stair turned and beyond it was dark. There was no landing. At the turn you set your foot sideways and twisted your body to go on up.

Upstairs there were two six-mat rooms.

The front one was empty; at the back was the altar room. The family altar was empty. Its doors stood open and the gold leaf inside was peeling.

On the tatami was a square of faded straw.

A patch of different colour about thirty centimetres square. Where the box holding the memorial tablet had stood. It was downstairs now, in a cardboard box on the earth floor.

Kamiya felt in his jacket pocket. He had not brought incense. Had he brought it, there was no burner. The ash, the bell, the candlesticks were all in the boxes downstairs.

He stood in front of the altar, doing nothing.

He opened the window. Paddies. In February the paddies were dry, stubble everywhere. Beyond them, two pylons. The same line that ran north of the old factory site.

Soichiro Tono had come up here on the evening of the eleventh of every month.

His wife's monthly memorial day. What he did once he was up here, Kamiya did not know. To put his hands together would take no time at all. But thinking about how long a man of seventy-eight would take to climb these stairs, the round trip would come to fifteen minutes.

He had done that every month since June 2019. Close to eighty times.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Coming down was worse.

He could not see his feet. Even with a hand on the wall, his body leaned forward. At the turn of the fourth step, the wall ran out for a moment. His left hand closed on air.

Kamiya stepped down onto the earth floor and let out a breath.

Then he looked back at the stairs.

He slid the door shut and locked it. He did not return the key to the postbox, but put it, envelope and all, in the inside pocket of his jacket. He would have to come once more before the schedule was finished. He could return it then.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Back in the car, he phoned Ugano police station.

This was the second time he had spoken to the detective named Nagasawa. The first had been when Kamiya took in Soichiro's letter. Nagasawa had read it, said, "We'll hold on to this," and that had been the last Kamiya had heard.

"Could you tell me the circumstances in which he was found?"

"As far as I'm able."

Nagasawa was businesslike.

"The body was at the foot of the stairs. Head injury from striking the beaten-earth floor. Estimated time of death between six and seven p.m. on the 11th. Found at about eight-thirty on the morning of the 13th, by his eldest daughter."

"The front door?"

"Locked."

"The key?"

"In his trouser pocket."

Kamiya wrote it in his notebook.

"Does anyone else have a key?"

"The eldest daughter has one. She let herself in with it."

"Any sign of a struggle?"

"None. No sign of anything searched, no outdoor shoe marks. It's winter, in a house with no heating on. We've treated it as an accident."

"The handrail?"

A short pause.

"…The handrail?"

"There's no handrail on the stairs. There are screw holes left in the wall."

"We were told by the builder that it had been taken off ahead of the demolition."

"I see."

"Is there something?"

"No."

Kamiya thanked him and rang off.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Without starting the engine, he took the letter out of his bag.

It had come with the ledger on February 14. He had read it more than ten times.

　　*If this has reached you, I am already dead.*
　　*If I died falling down the stairs, it was not an accident.*

When he read that line in the library, he had thought: this man had a particular staircase in front of him every day. Which staircase, he had not known.

Now he knew.

Steep, and dark, a wall on one side and nothing on the other. Going up you keep a hand on the wall, and at the turn, for a moment, the hand closes on air.

Kamiya had only just made sure that this house had one staircase.

This man had known in advance where he would fall.


# Chapter Thirteen: February 5

The Tono Construction office was a prefab building on the old road, south of Ugano.

On the morning of February 26, Kamiya parked outside it. Next door was a materials yard, with scaffolding poles stacked high. It was a spot the rain reached, and still they were stacked square.

Inside, a kettle was singing on the stove. There was one woman in the office, about sixty by the look of her. The boss was out on site, she said.

"My name is Kamiya. I'm the executor. I'd like to see the estimate for demolishing the old house, if I may."

"Oh — the Tonos'."

She brought out a file. She knew exactly where it was.

　　ESTIMATE
　　Re: Tono, Ugano City, ——　Demolition of timber-frame dwelling
　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　¥1,180,000 (excl. tax)
　　Date of issue: February 5, 2026

Kamiya looked at the breakdown.

Temporary scaffolding and sheeting, removal of remaining contents, roof demolition, structure demolition, removal of footings, haulage and disposal of waste, levelling. Eight items. Unit prices and quantities for each. A careful estimate.

Nowhere did the word *handrail* appear.

"Who asked for this estimate?"

"Chizuru-san."

"When?"

"She phoned at the end of January, I think. We went out to look on the 3rd, or the 4th."

The woman turned the pages of the desk calendar.

"The 4th. The boss went on his own."

"Did he go inside?"

"I'd think so. You can't get to that figure without going in to see what's left in there."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya asked about something else.

"Before a demolition, do you take out the fittings and metalwork?"

"We do. Anything that sells comes out first. Not that there's anything in that house."

"The handrail?"

"Handrail?"

The woman pushed her reading glasses up.

"Nobody pays for a wooden handrail. It'd cost more to cart it away. It just goes under the machine with everything else."

"So you wouldn't take it off before the demolition."

"No."

She was certain.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the back wall of the office was a corkboard.

A work schedule, safety slogans, a calendar from the local credit union. Under them, a college notebook hung on a string. On the cover it said *KEYS*.

"What's this?"

"The key log. We hold keys to people's houses, you see. You take one, you write it down; you bring it back, you write it down. The boss is strict about it."

Kamiya said, "May I look?" The woman hesitated, then said, "Don't look at the bits with names on, will you." *Don't look* meant *you may look*.

It was ruled into a table. Date, property, taken by, returned, seal.

He looked at February.

　　2/4　　Tono (old house)　　　Katsumi Tono　　　2/4　　　[seal]

The day the boss went out to look. Returned the same day.

Two lines further down there was another entry.

　　2/8　　Tono (old house)　　　Chizuru Tono　　　2/8　　　[seal]

Kamiya looked for some time at the handwriting in the *taken by* column.

It was the same hand as *Old house — front door* on the envelope in the postbox, and *Altar room — small items* on the cardboard box. Rounded, with short tails.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Chizuru-san borrowed the key on February 8."

The woman leaned over to look at the book.

"So she did. Clearing out, I expect. She's borrowed it a few times."

"Which other days?"

The woman turned back a month. Twice in January, once in December. Chizuru's name every time.

"Doesn't Chizuru-san have her own key?"

"She does. But when she comes straight from work she's left her house keys at home, hasn't she. So she stops in here and borrows ours. We've held one for years."

"For years — since when?"

"1998. Since the job when we put the handrail on those stairs."

Kamiya looked up.

"You fitted the handrail?"

"The boss did. Those stairs are steep, aren't they. His wife had bad knees. She couldn't get up, so Mr Tono asked us to put one in."

The woman ran a finger along the back of the calendar, as if tracing something.

"He had it made from wood, specially. Turned by hand, not off the shelf. He said a thin grip would suit his wife's hand better."

"And his wife, afterwards—"

"She died in 2019."

Kamiya thought of the four holes. A little above the waist, evenly spaced, in a straight line.

Someone had taken it off.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Do you have a security camera here?"

The woman pointed above the door. A black half-sphere was mounted there.

"We do, but it records over itself every fourteen days. The boss said a cheap one would do."

February 8 was exactly eighteen days ago.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Just after noon, Katsumi came back to the office.

The knees of his work trousers were white with dust. He saw Kamiya, said, "Ah, the executor," and put his helmet down on the desk. A sunburnt face; when he smiled, the lines at the corners of his eyes went deep.

"Did you get a look at the estimate?"

"I did. It's very thorough."

"Even for a job that's only knocking something down, we give honest figures. My brother was that kind of man."

Kamiya stopped slightly at the way he said it. *My brother*, Katsumi had said.

"One question. On February 8, did anyone from here go to the site?"

Katsumi reached for his teacup and stopped.

"The 8th."

"A Sunday."

"We don't work Sundays."

"Nothing taken off in advance, getting ready for the demolition?"

"No. There's no contract yet. We gave an estimate, that's all. We haven't taken the job."

Katsumi lifted the cup and drank.

"Nobody from here went to that site that day."


# Chapter Fourteen: One Third

He got the registry certificate for the old factory site on the morning of February 27.

He collected it at the issuing counter of the Legal Affairs Bureau and opened it in the car.

In the owner column there were two names.

　　Soichiro Tono　　share: two-thirds
　　Katsumi Tono　　 share: one-third

Cause: inheritance, 1985. When their father died, the brothers had divided it. The factory building had been in Soichiro's name alone; only the land beneath it had stayed jointly owned, for forty-one years.

Kamiya laid the certificate on his knee and thought for a while.

Jointly owned land cannot be sold by one owner. You can sell your share alone, but hardly anyone is fool enough to buy one-third of a plot. Put the other way: for forty years Katsumi had held a third and never once been able to turn it into money.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was past ten when he reached the Tono Construction office.

Katsumi was there today. In the same work clothes as yesterday, he sat on the sofa in the corner of the office and offered Kamiya a chair.

"I've seen the register." Kamiya brought out the certificate. "The factory site is jointly owned with you."

"That's right."

"I'll put only Soichiro's share in the schedule. Two-thirds."

"Understood."

Katsumi glanced at the certificate and handed it straight back. It was the way a man hands back a document whose contents he already knows.

"Forgive me — has there been any talk of selling this share?"

"There has."

He said it plainly.

"Last autumn. A company came up from Tokyo wanting to buy the lot. Said they'd take just my third if that was all they could get."

"At what price?"

"Twenty-eight thousand a *tsubo*. For my part alone, it comes to about twenty-seven million."

Kamiya wrote it down. Close to thirty thousand a *tsubo*.

"You didn't sell?"

"My brother wouldn't."

Katsumi folded his hands on his knees. Thick knuckles; a site man's hands.

"It's joint, you see. If I sell only mine, the buyer's stuck. A third on its own, you can't do anything with it. They said themselves it was no use unless it was three-thirds."

"Why wouldn't Soichiro sell?"

"He didn't give reasons. That's the kind of man he was. He told you what he'd decided, and that was it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Katsumi got up to make tea.

Kamiya's eyes went, without intending to, to the desk.

Bundles of delivery notes, a calculator, reading glasses, a catalogue of seismic isolation brackets. At the edge lay a business card, face down. The back was uppermost.

Something was scrawled on it in black marker.

　　3/31

With a single line drawn under it.

Katsumi came back with two teacups. Kamiya looked away, but Katsumi seemed to have noticed. He picked up the card, turned it face up, and set it in front of Kamiya.

　　Shirasagi Development Co., Ltd.　　Land Development Division　　Kijima

"That's the Tokyo company."

"And March 31?"

"That's their deadline. They said they'd wind the whole thing up at the end of March."

"Even though the data centre's been cancelled?"

"He rang last week. Said if it could all be put together by the end of March, there was still something to be done."

Katsumi put down his cup.

"I've already turned him down."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"May I ask about the stakes?"

Katsumi's hand stopped at the rim of the cup.

"There are stakes along the boundary of the site. Red heads. Dated January 20."

"Not ours."

"Then—"

"Shirasagi, I'd say. They'll have sent a surveyor in."

"Can they survey without the landowner's consent?"

"No."

Katsumi said it, and then said nothing more.

*No* meant someone had consented. If not Soichiro, then the man who held the other third, or somebody acting for one of them.

"Did you consent?"

"I did not."

His voice hardened slightly.

"That's the truth. I wanted to sell that land. I'm not hiding that. But I did not let people onto my brother's land behind his back."

Kamiya believed him. He believed him because the man had said *I wanted to sell* first. A liar does not lead with what counts against him.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

When he had finished his tea, Kamiya asked:

"Did you know Soichiro used to go upstairs in the old house?"

"I knew he went on the anniversary. My sister-in-law's tablet's there."

"On the eleventh of every month."

"Ah, the monthly one. Yes, he did say something like that."

Katsumi set his cup down.

"In the daytime, surely. I thought he went at midday, put his hands together a minute, and came home."

"In the evening."

"The evening?"

Katsumi looked surprised.

"He went up those stairs after dark?"

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

As he was leaving, Kamiya asked once more.

"Let me just confirm what you told me yesterday. On February 8, nobody from here went to the old house. Is that right?"

Katsumi had half risen, and stopped.

For a while there was no answer.

"…They shouldn't have."

"Shouldn't have."

"It was a Sunday. Only—"

Katsumi tugged at the cuff of his work jacket, for no reason.

"The young lads, they'll sometimes go and do something off their own bat. Trying to be helpful."

"Such as?"

"Anything. Put sheeting up in advance. Take off whatever comes off."

Yesterday this man had said flatly: *Nobody from here went to that site that day.*

In a day, the way he said it had changed.

"Katsumi-san."

"Yes."

"Did something happen last night?"

Katsumi did not answer.

"Did someone ask you something?"

"No."

"Did you ask someone something?"

Katsumi looked at him. In the sunburnt face, only the skin around the eyes was pale.

"Kamiya-san. You're the executor."

"Yes."

"Your job is to divide up the property."

"Yes."

"Then do that, and only that."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Back in the car, Kamiya wrote in his notebook.

　　2/26　Katsumi: "Nobody went." (flat)
　　2/27　Katsumi: "Shouldn't have." "The lads sometimes help off their own bat."

When he had written it, he looked at the two lines.

When a man changes how he says something overnight, it is usually because he learned something in between. He learned it last night. After Kamiya had seen the key log.

And this man was not saying what he had learned.

*He's covering for someone*, Kamiya thought. He did not write down who. If he wrote it, the fact that he had thought it would remain. For thirty years he had lived by that kind of caution. More than once or twice, that caution had delayed something.


# Chapter Fifteen: Sagara

On the evening of March 2, only Misaki and Kamiya were left in the Tono Precision office.

The presses stopped at half past five and everyone had gone before six. Yamane was the last; he handed Misaki the key for locking up, said, "I'll be off," and went.

The kettle was singing on the stove. Misaki put a rubber band round a bundle of delivery notes and put it in the cabinet.

"Mr Kamiya. Was there something?"

Kamiya took from his bag a bundle of envelopes held with a rubber band. The copies of the transfer forms Misaki had taken out of her own desk drawer the month before.

"I'm returning these."

Misaki did not take them.

"You looked."

"I looked."

Misaki settled back in her chair. Having settled, she said nothing for a while.

"The chairman asked me not to tell anyone."

"Who asked?"

"Both of them. My mother, and the chairman."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"My mother died in 2016."

Misaki held her teacup in both hands as she spoke.

"Until then, fifty thousand yen came to her every month. She said it was a scholarship. A scholarship the company was paying. I believed that until I was about in middle school."

"And afterwards?"

"After she died it came to me, and it went up to eighty thousand. I refused. I refused three times, and three times it was paid in."

Kamiya put the bundle of copies on the table.

"Your father—"

Misaki put her cup down.

"He died in June 1996."

Outside the window, one of the factory's security lights came on, late.

"His name was Yasuo Sagara. He was senior managing director of this company."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya knew the name.

It was a name he had spent thirty years not letting himself remember. Not in the approver's box on the credit memo, but in the preparer's box on the attached trial balance, that seal had been stamped. A small seal, with one corner chipped.

"In the spring of 1996 the company's cash flow went wrong."

Misaki went on evenly. *She has an order for telling it*, Kamiya thought. It was the way someone speaks who has rearranged it in her head many times.

"After the bubble, two construction firms we supplied went under one after the other, and the receivables went bad. The chairman had the trial balances for the bank reworked. Built up the inventory so it looked as though we were making a profit. My father was the one who did it."

"…Yes."

Misaki looked up.

"I was three. I don't remember any of it."

"There was. The policy was old enough that the exclusion period had passed."

"How much?"

"Fifteen million."

Misaki ran a finger round the rim of her cup.

"Not one yen is left. My father was a joint guarantor on the company's borrowing. He was a director, so he had to be. The insurance money went straight to the bank."

Kamiya could not say anything.

He did not need to ask which bank.

"That year my mother moved into a housing estate in Maebashi. She worked part-time at a supermarket and nights at a boxed-lunch shop, both at once, and brought me up. Without the fifty thousand from the chairman I would not have finished high school."

"Why did Soichiro—"

"I don't know. I never asked."

Misaki said:

"Whether it was to make amends, or to keep us quiet. Perhaps both. My mother took it, and never once said a bad word about the chairman. I think she had decided not to."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Why did you join this company?"

"Because I wanted to know."

She answered at once.

"I was doing accounts in Tokyo. At twenty-six I saw the vacancy here. It wasn't chance. I had been watching for years. I applied, and when I went for the interview, the chairman came out himself. He looked at my name and hired me without saying anything."

"Did you tell him who you were?"

"No. Only the surname, Sagara. I think that was enough for him."

"Seven years, and you never asked?"

"I asked. Once."

Misaki looked toward the cabinet.

"In my third year I asked about my father. The chairman looked at my face and said: *Those were figures I had him make.*"

"That was all?"

"That was all. Then he went back to talking about work."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the way out, Kamiya asked something unconnected. He asked it as a question for checking the schedule.

"Did the chairman go to the old house often?"

"On the anniversary, I think."

"The anniversary."

"His wife's. In June."

Misaki answered while clearing away the cups.

"Did you know he went up there on the eleventh of every month?"

"Every month?"

Her hands stopped.

"I didn't know. He wasn't someone who talked about that sort of thing at work."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The flame in the stove dropped once, and came back.

"Mr Kamiya."

Misaki said it.

"May I ask you one thing?"

"Yes."

"Why do you think the chairman made you his executor?"

Kamiya had no answer ready.

"We had a little to do with each other, thirty years ago."

"What did you have to do with each other?"

*The way she's asking has changed*, Kamiya thought. Until now she had spoken like someone telling things in a fixed order. Now she was waiting for an answer.

"I—"

He began, and set the words down once.

For thirty years he had avoided this moment. He had avoided it, and sitting across from him now was the daughter of the man he had avoided. Soichiro had known that, and called him here.

"I was at the Ugano branch of Joshu Akagi Bank. In lending."

Misaki's finger stopped at the rim of her cup.

"In June 1996, it was I who turned down this company's credit."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Yes."

"I believe it — but the man who made that trial balance died that month. I met him once. In the warehouse, when I counted the stock. He stood behind me the whole time. When I'd finished counting, I said, *It's short*, and he said, *Yes.*"

Misaki's finger left the rim of the cup.

"He didn't make any excuse. He didn't say why it was short. He only said yes. That is all I ever said to your father, and all he ever said to me."

Misaki slowly raised her face.

Her eyes were dry.

"So it was you."


# Chapter Sixteen: The Fourth Time

The estate account was opened on February 24.

At the Ugano branch of Joshu Akagi Bank. The account name was *Estate of the late Soichiro Tono — Executor Shuji Kamiya*. A passbook and a registered seal, no cash card. The registered seal was a new one Kamiya had had made.

On March 4, there was seventy-two million yen in that account.

He had closed the deposits, sold the shares, and put everything together in one place for the time being. The contract with the trust bank was not yet signed. The papers would be ready in mid-March and the trust set up after that. For those two weeks or so, seventy-two million sat in an account with Kamiya's name on it.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

That night Kamiya was upstairs in the six-mat room of the house in Takasaki.

It was past eleven. He laid out on the desk the passbook, the registered seal, and a calculator. He laid them out to reconcile the figures in the schedule. That was what he told himself.

He looked at the balance column.

　　72,043,518

He had looked at other people's money until he was sick of it. So he was used to it. He thought he was used to it.

It was the first time he had seen seventy-two million in a passbook with his own name printed on it.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Not thinking about something is impossible.

Trying not to think is itself thinking. For thirty years Kamiya had seen it in people's faces. The look a borrower gives for an instant, on the other side of the counter.

The look he was wearing now.

He pulled the calculator toward him.

Twenty-eight million.

What had vanished into Arclight Capital. If that amount came back tomorrow, he would not have to tell Kayoko. He could give Mai the eight million for her deposit. He had said the fixed deposit matured the year after next. Fill the hole before then, and the lie would end as a lie, and nothing more.

Seventy-two million minus twenty-eight million was forty-four million.

The trust was being set up in mid-March. If the balance did not match when it was set up, it would show. So it would have to go back before then. Two weeks was impossible.

Kamiya pressed the keys.

Once.

He thought about how to delay the trust. A defect in the paperwork could push the contract with the trust bank back a month. If the executor delayed it, no one would suspect. A month and a half — no, even a month and a half would not be enough to put it back.

Twice.

He worked out what he could put back. Sell the house, eighteen million. What was left of the retirement money, four million. A loan against the pension, two million. Not enough.

Three times.

He thought about the simplest version. Don't put it back. When the trust was set up, report that the cash had been forty-four million. The evidence, he would make himself. The executor draws up the schedule. Tachibana would look at the schedule, but the executor obtains the bank balance certificates. The executor reports the proceeds of the share sales.

Who would notice?

Ryosuke would not. He did not look at figures. Chizuru read documents, but she did not know what her father's assets had been. Misaki only looked at the company's numbers. Tachibana would not suspect.

There was Soichiro's note. *Land one hundred million, business one hundred million, cash one hundred million.* Because of that note, even seventy-two million was short. At forty-four it would be shorter still. But the man had sent twenty-eight million abroad last August. Say he had done the same thing a second time, and it would pass.

It would pass.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya did not press the key a fourth time.

It was not conscience that stopped him.

It was fear. He was afraid of having reached the conclusion *it would pass*. Everything he had learned in thirty years had just moved, all of it, toward taking money out. Where to press so that nothing showed: those thirty years had taught him that.

A man is not honest because he is capable.

Kamiya turned the calculator face down.

Then he went back, in order, over what he had just been thinking.

*Borrow it for now. Put it back in three months. No one is hurt. On paper no one loses anything. Put it back and it never happened.*

He knew those five phrases.

For thirty years he had heard them hundreds of times across the table in branch meeting rooms. The accounts manager who had dipped into the company's money said them. The branch office head who had had his hand in the till said them. Every one of them said them in the same order. *For now, I'll put it back, no one's hurt, on paper, it never happened.*

Not one of them had put it back.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Downstairs, Kayoko turned off the television.

Kamiya put the passbook in an envelope, and put the registered seal in a different drawer. Keeping them apart was a banker's habit. The two in the same place is the most dangerous thing there is.

He kept them apart.

Kept apart, getting at them meant moving his hand twice. Before the second time, he might be able to stop.

The will does not say *divide it in three*. It says: *To anyone who staked it all on one thing, leave not a single yen.*

Kamiya turned off the light.

Last August, someone had moved twenty-eight million out of Soichiro Tono's account. He did not yet know that person's face. But what that person had been thinking, he now knew exactly.

Word for word.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The next morning, Kamiya telephoned Tachibana's office.

"I have a request about the estate account."

"Yes."

"Could withdrawals be made to require your registered seal as well? I'd like an arrangement that any withdrawal over one million can't be made without two seals — the executor's and yours, as agent."

On the other end, he heard Tachibana set down some papers.

"If an agreement with the bank will do, we can exchange documents. For the two weeks until the trust is set up."

"Yes."

"May I ask why?"

Kamiya was quiet for a moment.

"It isn't good for the executor to be able to move it alone."

"I have never had a request like that before."

"I imagine not."

Tachibana said, "I'll prepare it." She asked nothing more.

They exchanged the papers that afternoon and delivered them to the bank. Afterwards, Kamiya thought about what he had done.

If he trusted himself, he would not need this. The man who can say he doesn't need it is the most dangerous.

Soichiro Tono had not given his son the cash in a lump sum. Eight million a year for ten years. That was not because he did not trust his son. It was to keep trust out of it. Make it so you don't have to rely on trust, and when trust gives out, nothing happens.


# Chapter Seventeen: Voluntary

The enquiry from the Gunma Chuo Credit Union came on March 5.

It was addressed to the executor. The deceased, Soichiro Tono, had a liability as joint guarantor, it said.

　　Principal debtor　　Ryo Create Co., Ltd.
　　Amount　　　　　　¥20,000,000
　　Date of guarantee　　February 9, 2026

Kamiya looked at the date twice.

February 9. Two days before Soichiro died.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Ryo Create was the company Ryosuke Tono had set up in 2018.

When Kamiya pulled the registration, the head office was the flat in Itabashi, down to the room number. Business purposes: advertising agency, systems development, consulting. One director: Ryosuke. A company that was all but dormant had borrowed twenty million on February 9.

He went to the credit union. A young officer in lending brought out a copy of the guarantee.

In the guarantor's box were Soichiro Tono's signature and the impression of his registered seal.

Kamiya looked at it for a long time.

The hand was similar. For an amateur copy it was very similar. But it was not Soichiro's.

The last downstroke of *rō* in his given name leaned too far to the right. Soichiro's signature had been on the papers attached to a credit memo thirty years ago, and Kamiya still remembered it. That man's downstrokes came straight down. And the characters were too even in size. In a real signature, the later characters grow smaller.

"The seal certificate?"

"It was attached, yes."

He brought it out. Date of issue, February 6. Ugano City Hall. Requested in person.

"Is there any way to know whether he came to collect it himself?"

"That would be a matter for the city office. We wouldn't know."

On February 6, someone had obtained Soichiro's seal certificate. On February 9, someone had written Soichiro's signature. On February 11, Soichiro died.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya telephoned Nagasawa at Ugano police station.

"There's something I should report, as executor."

Nagasawa listened. He did not interrupt once until Kamiya had finished.

"Have you had the handwriting looked at by an expert?"

"No. That's my own judgement."

"A former banker's judgement."

"Yes."

A short pause.

"Mr Kamiya. If we act on this, it stops being about an accidental death."

"Does it?"

"It does. It gives us a motive."

Kamiya shifted the receiver to his other hand.

"I'm not talking about motive."

"I know you're not. But that's not how it'll be read upstairs."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the morning of March 6, Ryosuke Tono was asked to come in to Ugano police station voluntarily.

Kamiya heard it in a phone call from Tachibana. Tachibana had heard it in a phone call from Chizuru.

In the afternoon, Kamiya was in the ground-floor lobby of the station. No one had asked him to come. As executor he had to decide how to fill in the liabilities column of the schedule, so he was waiting for the outcome. That was the reason he gave himself for waiting.

A little after four, Chizuru came.

Under her coat she was in her care-home uniform. She must have slipped out. When she saw Kamiya she walked straight over.

"Was it you who told the police?"

"I reported the credit union's enquiry."

"They've called my brother in."

"I know."

Chizuru did not sit on the lobby chairs. She stood, looking toward the front desk.

"Is it about a guarantee?"

"You knew?"

"No. The officer just told me."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

A reporter from the local paper came into the lobby before five.

Two of them, one with a camera. They asked something at the desk, were turned away, and did not leave. In the next morning's paper there was a short piece on the prefectural page.

　　Ugano man of means dies — dispute over estate?
　　Police, who treated the death as an accident, now believe there was a family
　　dispute over the inheritance and are questioning those involved.

It stated no facts. Nor did it state anything that was not a fact. For thirty years Kamiya had read articles written that way.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Ryosuke came out at half past six.

It was voluntary, so he came out the front, like anyone. Coat over his arm, head down, walking fast toward the car park. Chizuru went after him and said something. Ryosuke did not stop; he raised one hand.

Chizuru stopped short of the car park, and came back.

"Mr Kamiya."

Her voice was not the same as before.

"My brother is not someone who would do that."

Kamiya did not know what he should say.

"I know he has debts. I know he's hopeless. He's trouble for people. But he wouldn't put Father's seal on something."

"Chizuru-san—"

"To do that to Father—"

Her voice broke off there.

Chizuru shut her mouth and looked down. Both hands gripped the handles of her bag. Her knuckles were white.

"…I'm sorry. That had nothing to do with anything."

Chizuru bowed and walked away toward the car park.

Kamiya watched her back.

It was the first time he had seen Chizuru raise her voice. He had met her many times in three weeks. On the day she was told of her father's death, the day the will was read, the day the land became twenty million, she had spoken in the same voice.

It was when her brother was suspected that her voice changed for the first time.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Just after ten at night, his phone rang. Ryosuke.

"You sold me out."

He was drunk; Kamiya could tell from the first word.

"I reported the credit union's enquiry to the police."

"That's what selling out means."

"The executor verifies the deceased's liabilities. A guarantee affects the estate. It would be a problem not to report it."

"Don't give me that."

In the background a television was on.

"You're saying I killed the old man?"

"I haven't said that."

"Then what?"

Kamiya pressed the phone closer to his ear.

"Ryosuke-san. You went to the old house on February 9."

A silence. Only the television went on.

"…I didn't."

"The seal certificate was issued on February 6. Requested in person. Did your father go to the city office on February 6?"

"How would I know."

"The guarantee is dated February 9. Two days before your father died."

"So what?"

His voice was getting smaller.

"I didn't kill him."

That was all, and the line went dead.

Kamiya put the phone down on the desk.

*I didn't kill him*, Ryosuke had said. When a man answers a question you haven't asked, it is because that is where it hurts.

He had taken calls like that many times. At night, drunk. Afterwards you think about what the caller was trying to protect. There are the ones protecting money, the ones protecting face, and one more.

The ones who want to believe they are still forgiven.


# Chapter Eighteen: Only Once

On the morning of March 9, Ryosuke phoned.

"I need to talk to you."

"Where shall I come?"

"I'll come to you. Takasaki all right?"

Kamiya named the coffee shop by the station. He had known it for forty years and it was still in the same place.

Ryosuke was there first. In a booth at the back, his coffee untouched. He had not shaved.

"I saw a lawyer yesterday."

He said it as soon as Kamiya sat down.

"A lawyer Chizuru got for me. Not Tachibana — a different one. He said I'd better tell the truth."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the evening of February 9, Ryosuke had been at the old house.

"I drove up from Tokyo. Got there about half past five."

Soichiro was not in the altar room but in the sitting room off the entry, with the heater on, reading the paper. The house was half cleared, Ryosuke said; only a cushion had been left out.

"We talked about money. Twenty million."

"What did your father say?"

"*I told you it was only once.*"

Ryosuke turned his saucer round with a finger.

"When he gave me five million in 2018 he said it was once, only once. Eight years ago. He said it as though it was yesterday."

"And then?"

"I shouted. Me. He doesn't shout. He sat there, folded the paper, put it on his knee, and looked at me."

Kamiya remembered what Okada, the neighbour, had said. *It was just that voices got a bit loud at the door, and that's why I looked.*

"What did he say?"

"That there'd be a talk in March."

Ryosuke looked up.

"*There's something I'll be telling the three of you in March. Wait till then.* I didn't know what he meant. Now I suppose it was the will."

The forty-ninth day fell on March 31. While he was still alive, Soichiro had been calling the day the three would gather after his death *March*.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"What time did you leave?"

"Before six. It was already dark when I went out the front."

"Your father?"

"In the sitting room. He was alive."

Ryosuke smiled a little there, for the first time. Then he looked as though he had realised the smile did not belong.

"On the way out I went past the study. The back room he used as a study is at the end of the corridor, and that's where the toilet is."

Kamiya listened without speaking.

"There was an envelope on the desk. From the city office. *Certificate of Registered Seal*, printed on it."

"The date?"

"February 6. There were two in it."

*Two.* Kamiya wrote it down.

To make a notarial will, the testator needs a seal certificate. Soichiro had got it on February 6. Having got it, he had waited four days and gone to the notary on the 10th.

When he got it, he had not yet decided which day he would go.

"I took one."

Ryosuke said it.

"The registered seal was in the drawer. The top one, the one with no lock. He's always kept it there. I've known since I was a kid."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"I took it to the credit union the next day."

February 10. The day Soichiro was at the notary's.

"The signature on the guarantee?"

"I wrote it."

"The seal?"

"I stamped it. I was going to go back the week after and return it."

"Return it."

"The seal. Put it back in the drawer. Nobody was going to notice."

Kamiya heard the way he said it. *Nobody was going to notice.* He had heard those words many times across the table in branch meeting rooms. He had thought them himself, last week, upstairs in the six-mat room.

"Your father died on February 11."

Ryosuke nodded.

"I got the call on the morning of the 13th. From Chizuru."

"When you heard, what was the first thing you thought?"

Ryosuke was silent for a long time. He still had not touched the coffee. A skin had formed on it.

"…That it wouldn't come out."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The sounds in the shop were suddenly loud.

"When I heard he was dead, that was the very first thing. I wouldn't have to put it back. He'd never find out. Not *my father's dead* — *it won't come out*. That came first."

Ryosuke was looking at the table.

"After that I thought, my father's dead. The order was the wrong way round."

"Ryosuke-san."

"Yes."

"Where were you on February 11?"

"Tokyo. From seven in the evening I was on the community's online call. Till eleven. There were about thirty people on it. There'll be a record."

"Did you tell the police?"

"I did. They've checked it."

"One more question," Kamiya said. "Did you know your father went up to the first floor of the old house on the eleventh of every month?"

"Every month?"

Ryosuke looked up. He genuinely did not understand.

"The anniversary. In June. The day Mum died."

"The monthly memorial. The eleventh of every month, in the evening."

"…I didn't know."

Ryosuke thought for a moment, and then said:

"Nobody knew that except Chizuru, I'd have thought."

Kamiya closed his notebook.

This man had not killed his father.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"How did it end up like this?"

Ryosuke said it. To no one in particular.

"I was doing all right at the start, you know. Advertising. Went independent in 2018, I was getting work. It fell apart in the second year. One big client went, and I tried to cover it, and started trading on margin."

"One thing, and it was everything."

"…Yes."

"And then?"

"I tried to get it back. That's all I was ever doing, trying to get it back. I wasn't trying to make more. I just wanted to get back to where I was."

Ryosuke pressed a hand over his eyes.

"One good hit and I'd have got it all back."

His voice gave way.

"One good hit and I'd never have had to tell the old man. Never have had to tell Chizuru. It would all have been as if it never happened. I was nearly there."

Kamiya was looking at the glass of water.

He had spent thirty years watching people say *I was nearly there*. People who were nearly there always say they were nearly there. But not one of them has ever been able to explain where *nearly* was.

Ryosuke put his elbows on the table and stayed like that, not moving.

Kamiya said nothing. He searched thirty years of memory for something he ought to say, and found nothing at all.


# Chapter Nineteen: The List

Kajiwara Financial Planning was on the second floor of a building six minutes' walk from the west exit of Takasaki station.

On the same floor were a judicial scrivener, a labour-and-social-security consultant, and a sign reading *Marketing Research Institute*. The kind of building that is rented out in partitioned units.

At two in the afternoon on March 9, Kamiya was shown into the meeting room.

Four certificates hung framed on the wall. Financial planner, inheritance advisor, estate-clearance advisor, and one written in English. Printed beneath it in Japanese: *International Wealth Succession Advisor*. Kamiya looked at the last one for a long time. He had seen qualifications with names like that many times.

"How good of the executor to come in person."

Kajiwara gave him a card. He had given him one on February 20 as well.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"I'll come straight to the point. On August 6, 2025, Soichiro Tono sent twenty-eight million yen overseas."

Kajiwara's expression did not change.

"I'm aware of that."

"Was it you who introduced it?"

"Introduced. Not sold."

The speed of the correction was the speed of his work.

"I'm registered only as a financial instruments intermediary. I'm not permitted to sell an overseas fund manager's products. So what I gave my father-in-law was information, nothing more. He applied himself."

"At the bank counter, your father-in-law said only that it was *an investment*."

"He would have."

"What state is it in now?"

"The principal is intact."

Something went cold along Kamiya's spine.

"Withdrawals are only temporarily suspended. The regulator over there is conducting a review. I'm told it'll be lifted within the year."

Last October Kamiya had been given the same explanation. *A tax procedure is required. It will be lifted within the year.* After that they had asked him for two million four hundred thousand to cover the cost of the procedure.

"And if it isn't lifted?"

"Then I'm a victim too."

Kajiwara said it, and smiled a little.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"You run seminars."

Kamiya pointed at the flyers stacked at the end of the table.

　　　Is your retirement money just sitting there?
　　　—A workshop for the over-65s: *Protect it while you grow it*
　　　Free · Individual consultations available

The venue was a banqueting room in a Takasaki hotel. Once a month, twelve times a year.

"We're fortunate. About thirty people come each time."

"How do you get them?"

"Ads in the local free paper, and word of mouth."

Kajiwara got up and brought a thick file from the cabinet. On the cover: *Seminar Record*. *He wants to show me*, Kamiya thought. This man liked people to see how big his business was.

"That's the last three years."

Inside, one attendance sheet was filed for each session. Date, venue, number of attendees. Below, handwritten signatures in a column. Signed at the reception desk.

Kamiya looked at October 2024. Then November.

　　　Saturday, November 16, 2024　Takasaki　Attendance: 32

Ninth from the top of the signatures:

　　Soichiro Tono

The same hand as the note. The hand that had written *Land one hundred million*.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"My father-in-law came twice," Kajiwara said from beside him.

"November, and February the following year. In February he booked an individual consultation."

Kamiya turned back to the page. November 16. Attendance thirty-two.

He ran his eyes down the signatures from the top. There was no reason. He had simply never lost the habit of reading a list from the top down.

At the seventeenth, his finger stopped.

　　Shuji Kamiya

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was his own hand.

November 16, 2024. A Saturday. A hotel in Takasaki.

Kamiya remembered the day. About six months after the re-employment scheme ended. He had seen the ad in the free paper tucked inside the newspaper and told Kayoko he was going to the Go club. He had sat near the back, listened for two hours, and gone home without buying anything.

It had been dull. He had spent thirty years on the other side of that talk. Nothing in it he did not already know.

So he had gone home reassured.

"I didn't buy anything there."

His voice came out lower than he meant. Kajiwara said, "No."

"I remember your name. I'd heard you were a banker."

"You keep the names of the ones who don't buy?"

"We do. They're future clients."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Three months after he signed that list, a call came.

February 2025. Arclight Capital's Japanese account manager. Kamiya had not asked where the number came from. He had known that if he asked, he would not be told. In April he sent twenty-eight million.

Now he knew where the call had come from.

"The list has never gone outside this office?"

Kajiwara's hand squared the corner of the file.

"It has not."

"Not once."

"Not once."

Denials come fast or slow. The fast ones have been prepared in advance.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

As Kamiya was leaving, Kajiwara said:

"Last October, my father-in-law came here once."

Kamiya stopped just short of the door.

"About a refund?"

"Yes. I explained, but he wasn't satisfied. So he asked to see who else I'd introduced over the years."

Kajiwara pointed at the file.

"I showed him this. As a record of my clients."

"And Soichiro read it?"

"He did. For about thirty minutes, without a word."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He took the stairs down. He could not face waiting for the lift.

October 2025. Soichiro sitting in that meeting room, turning the pages of that file for thirty minutes.

A man going back over, on paper, how he had lost twenty-eight million, had found in the list a name from thirty years ago.

The banker who stopped his company's credit in 1996 was on the same list. In the same room, listening to the same talk, drawing near to the same man.

The following month, Soichiro asked Tachibana to draft a will.

*—I am asking you.*

The letter had not said that. The letter had said nothing but the stairs. But the question of why it was him now had an answer.

You cannot ask a man who kept his money to watch money.

Only a man who failed to keep it knows where the hand slips.


# Chapter Twenty: It's All Right Now

At 7:40 on the morning of March 10, Kamiya was woken by a call from Nagasawa.

"We're going into Tono Construction."

"Going in?"

"A search. I'm telling you beforehand."

Kamiya sat on the futon and for a while could say nothing.

"What you told us was one of the things that set it off. That the estimate doesn't include removing the handrail. That Katsumi Tono's explanation changed."

"I didn't report that because I suspected Katsumi-san."

"I know."

Nagasawa's voice was flat.

"But a report is a report. We move on the material that comes in."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was on the lunchtime news.

A short slot for the prefecture: the head of a building firm in Ugano City had been arrested on suspicion of murder. The name was given. Katsumi Tono, 72. The victim was his elder brother, Soichiro Tono.

The outline of the case appeared in the evening paper.

That he had been in conflict with his brother over their joint share in the former factory site. That a Tokyo developer had approached him about buying the share. That on February 4 he had gone into the old house alone to prepare a demolition estimate. And that about the removal of the stair handrail he had given the police a false explanation — *it was taken off because the house was going to be demolished*.

Motive, opportunity and a lie.

Anyone who has written loan proposals for thirty years knows that when those three are lined up, they make a shape. A shape gets approved. It is rare for a mistake to be found after approval. When one is found, nobody says so.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the evening he went to the Tono Construction office.

The shutter was half down. He called through the gap and the woman from the office came out. Her eyes were red.

"It's finished for us now."

She said it and pointed inside. Every drawer had been pulled out of the desks, and files were piled on the floor. Nobody had tidied up after the search.

"What time this morning?"

"Eight. The boss had only just got in."

She sat down on an office chair and folded her hands on her lap.

"About ten of them. They came in the front and read out the warrant. The boss heard it all out, then put his helmet down on the desk."

"Did he say anything?"

"He told the young one to look after the site."

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing else."

She sniffed.

"I said, *Boss, tell them it wasn't you.* I said *tell them*. And he said —"

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"*It's all right now.*"

The woman said it.

"That's all he said, and he went out."

Kamiya was looking at the office floor. On top of a pile of files lay a notebook. On the cover was written *Keys*. Its string had snapped; it had come off the corkboard.

He picked it up, brushed off the dust, and put it on the desk.

"He hasn't denied it?"

"He doesn't say anything, apparently. That's what the lawyer said."

No denial. No confession.

Kamiya had seen a few people like that. Usually they had something to protect. People with something to protect do not deny. If they deny, the questions go on.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He went back to the car and sat without starting the engine.

He had not lied. That the estimate did not include removing the handrail was a fact. That Katsumi's explanation had changed in a single day was a fact. Kamiya had told Nagasawa what he saw, as he saw it.

He had done the same in 1996.

The inventory in the trial balance did not add up. He found it and took it to the branch manager. He did not lie. He did not move a single figure. He did the right thing.

Two months later, Yasuo Sagara was dead.

Thirty years on, he was doing the same thing again. Reporting what he saw, as he saw it. And each time, someone stepped off their own life.

A banker who does not report correctly is not a banker. Nor is he the one who answers for what comes of a correct report. For thirty years, Kamiya had been able to go on by keeping those two things apart.

When had he stopped being able to keep them apart?

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

From the car, he phoned Chizuru.

As executor there were things he ought to report. With the arrest of a co-owner of the joint share, the treatment of the inventory and the outlook for disposing of the land would change. That was what the call was for.

That was what the call was for, he told himself, and he made it.

"Hello."

Chizuru answered at once. She seemed to be at the care home; there were voices behind her.

"Have you heard about your uncle?"

"I have."

"Can you talk now?"

"I can."

Kamiya explained about the share. At the important points Chizuru said, "Yes." He could hear her taking notes.

When he had finished, she said nothing.

"Chizuru-san."

"Yes."

"About your uncle —"

There was a short pause.

"I thought: I see."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

*I see*, Chizuru had said.

Kamiya went on thinking about it after he hung up.

For three weeks Chizuru had read documents, stamped her seal, checked the inventory and taken the envelope. On the day her father died, on the day the land became twenty million, she had spoken in the same voice. Only when her brother was called in by the police had her voice changed.

Her uncle had been arrested for murder. A man from the house she had gone to four days a week for six years.

*I see.*

If your uncle were arrested on suspicion of killing your father, what would you say? Surely *it can't be*. Surely *that's not true*.

Or perhaps you would say *I see*. People are different. Some people do not let shock into their voice. Someone who works in care, all the more.

Kamiya thought that, and stopped thinking.

He would remember, later, that he had stopped.


# Chapter Twenty-One: Leaning Against the Wall

On March 13, Kamiya took the key to the old house and went there once more.

As executor he had no reason to go. The inventory of movable property was done. He had Chizuru's seal on it. The key he had forgotten to return was simply still in the inside pocket of his jacket.

He arrived at two in the afternoon.

The grass in the garden was longer than it had been two weeks before. Under the pine, three sparrows had come down.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He stood in the earth-floored entry and looked up the stairs.

The screw holes in the wall were as they had been. A little above waist height. Four.

For three days Kamiya had been thinking the same thing.

Suppose Katsumi Tono killed his brother to turn the share into money. He took off the handrail and waited for him to fall. That much makes a shape. As Nagasawa said, the motive, the opportunity and the lie are all there.

What Kamiya could not see was what came after.

Where had the man put the handrail he took off?

Neither the papers nor Nagasawa had said anything about where it went. The police were dealing only with the fact that it had been removed.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the east corner of the garden there was a corrugated-iron shed.

When he came on February 24 he had seen that there was no padlock on the door. He had not looked inside. In the inventory, under *movables in the shed*, he had written what Chizuru told him. Gardening things, a stepladder, an old bicycle.

He had not opened it himself.

Kamiya stood in front of the shed and put his hand on the handle.

He left it there, not moving it.

If there was nothing inside, that was the end of it. Katsumi Tono was in custody on suspicion of murder, and the police had their motive, opportunity and lie. It was not his place to interfere. An executor's job was to divide the property, not to investigate a case. He had thought so three days ago. He had thought so yesterday.

If there was something inside, it would not be the end.

The wind struck the side of the shed once.

Kamiya pulled the handle.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The bottom of the iron door had rusted and swollen, and it took two pulls to open.

It was dark inside. A stepladder on the left, the bicycle at the back. A shovel with earth on it. Stacked planters. A smell of mothballs.

On the right-hand wall, there it was.

A rounded wooden pole about two metres long. The fittings still on both ends. Leaning against the wall.

A wooden crate had been pushed up beside it so that it would not fall.

Kamiya looked at it for some time.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He put on gloves before he went near the pole.

He took hold of it. Thin. Too thin for Kamiya's hand. His fingers went too far round.

He remembered what someone in this house had said in 1998. *A thinner grip will suit my wife's hand better.*

It was not something bought off the shelf. Someone had shaved wood to make it fit the thickness of one woman's hand.

He looked at the fittings at each end. Two brackets, still attached. The screws had been left in the brackets too. Someone had taken the trouble to put each screw back into its own hole after taking it out.

Kamiya counted them twice.

Four. All there.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He went out of the shed and stood in the garden.

Kamiya had never met Sawako Tono. He had seen a photograph of her only once, the one on the altar. A round face, not smiling. Memorial portraits of that generation mostly do not smile.

She had died in June 2019. Some twenty years before that her knees had gone and she could no longer climb these stairs. So her husband had asked a builder to shave the wood. A thick grip would not have suited her hand.

Kamiya remembered the thickness he had just held through his gloves.

That pole was now standing in the shed. With a crate pushed up so it would not fall.

Someone had taken it down, and put it away.

To put a thing away is not to throw it out. Whoever had not thrown it out knew whose it had been.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He took photographs with his phone.

Close-ups of the screw heads in the brackets. On all four, the cross of the slot was crushed. The edges were rounded, and the centres shone white.

He photographed the four holes in the wall too. When he got the light down into them, the pictures showed fine scratches running in a spiral round the inside of each pilot hole.

When he had taken them, he went back to the car.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

It was just before four when he reached Tono Precision.

Yamane was in the tool room at the back of the works. When Kamiya held out the phone, he wiped the oil from his hands with a rag before he took it.

"What are these pictures?"

"I'd like you to look at them. Where screws have been taken out."

Yamane spread the image with his fingers and brought it close. He moved on to the second, the third.

Partway through, his hand stopped.

"This wasn't an impact driver."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"How can you tell?"

Yamane turned the screen towards him.

"All four heads are stripped. Stripped means the bit rides up out of the slot and spins."

"Yes."

"An impact driver hammers. It turns while it's striking — clack, clack. So it bites and backs the screw straight out. If any strip, it'll be one or two. All four stripping, that hardly ever happens."

Yamane took one down from the tool-room wall to show him. Heavy. A thick grip.

"This is our impact driver. Any professional uses one of these first. A demolition man above all. Those people live on speed."

"Then these?"

"A drill-driver, I'd say. The kind they sell at the home centre for about ten thousand yen. You get this if you turn it slowly without upping the torque."

Yamane went back and looked at another picture.

"And this."

It was the pilot hole.

"The scratches only go one way. Not back and forth. Whoever it was never once ran it in reverse to get it out."

"Meaning —"

"That's how an amateur takes a screw out. Someone who knows what they're doing, when it binds, tightens it a touch and then loosens it. This is somebody leaning on it to hold the bit in while it turned, because it wouldn't come loose."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya left the tool room and stood with his back to the outside wall of the works.

The press was going, at a steady interval.

The handrail that had been taken down had not been thrown away. It had not been broken. The screws had all been put back into the brackets they came from. It had been stood against the shed wall, with a crate pushed up beside it so it would not fall.

People who pull houses down do not do that.

Kamiya had watched demolition for thirty years. Every time he went to look at a building held as security, he saw it. Fittings that come out get thrown. Metalwork gets tied in bundles and put outside. Something like a handrail, which nobody will buy, is crushed where it is by the machine.

This was not the work of someone who pulls things down.

It was the work of someone who puts things away. Someone who writes the date, seals the box and sets it in line.

Kamiya remembered the four cardboard boxes.

*Altar room — small items*. *Sitting room*. *Kitchen*. A careful hand.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He went out to the road in front of the works, and with his hand on the car door, he stopped.

What he had now was a dozen or so photographs and the works manager's opinion.

If he took this to Nagasawa, Katsumi Tono's arrest would waver. If it wavered, someone else would stand in his place. A man who had written loan proposals for thirty years knew what that meant. To turn one case down was to hand the allocation to someone else.

Not taking it was also a choice.

Katsumi had not denied it. The police were building their shape. By the forty-ninth day, the work on the will would be done. As executor he could divide the property in three, take his fee and go back to Takasaki. Nobody would blame Kamiya. Nobody would have any reason to.

He had lived thirty years on that reasoning.

Kamiya opened the door and got in. He started the engine, and with it running, for some time he did not pull away.

Through the window he could hear the press in the works. At a steady interval.


# Chapter Twenty-Two: Three

On the morning of March 16, upstairs in the house in Takasaki, Kamiya opened his notebook.

On a blank page he drew three lines.

　　One — knew of the habit of going up to the first floor of that house on the evening of February 11
　　Two — had a key to the old house
　　Three — could get into the old house on February 8

Unless all three were true, that handrail could not have been taken off.

If even one was missing, it could not. Even if someone had taken it off, it meant nothing unless they knew someone would be coming up on the evening of the 11th. People do not do things that mean nothing.

Kamiya began to write out names.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

**Ryosuke Tono**

One. He had asked at the coffee shop on March 9: did you know your father went up to the first floor on the eleventh of every month? Ryosuke had said, "Every month?" The anniversary, surely — June. It had not looked like acting. And then: *Nobody knew that except Chizuru, I'd have thought.*

Two. He had no key. At the coffee shop on March 9, Kamiya had asked how he had got in on February 9. Ryosuke had said, "I rang the bell." His father had come to the door.

Three. Not checked. With one and two missing, there had been no need.

Crossed out.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

**Misaki Sagara**

One. He had asked her on the evening of March 2: did the chairman go to the old house on the monthly memorial day? Misaki had said, "I believe he went on the anniversary." She had not known it was every month.

She held even the copies of Soichiro's private remittances. She knew everything about the company. Even so, she had not known about the eleventh. Which meant that Soichiro had not told anyone at the company.

Two. She had no key.

Crossed out.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

There were footsteps on the stairs.

"Lunch."

Kayoko slid open the door. Kamiya turned the notebook face down. Only after he had turned it over did he notice he had done it.

"What's that?"

"Work."

"The car park?"

"The executor business."

Kayoko stood there a moment. Then she said, "Oh," and went downstairs.

Kamiya looked at the face-down notebook.

For a year now, in this house, he had been turning things face down. Passbooks, envelopes, screens. Who he was hiding them from had been different each time. Today he had hidden it for a different reason. That alone made it a little easier. He supposed he would feel guilty later for having found it easier.

He opened the notebook again.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

**Katsumi Tono**

One. Kamiya had asked him on February 27: did you know Soichiro-san went up to the first floor of that house? Katsumi had said, "I knew he went on the anniversary." And then: "In the evening? I thought it was midday."

A man who did not know the time could not have set it up for a fall in the evening.

Two. He had one. Tono Construction had held a key since 1998.

Three. February 8 was a Sunday, and in the key log for that day, Katsumi's name did not appear. But he was the boss. He could take his own firm's key without signing for it.

Two and three, yes. One was missing.

Kamiya did not cross out Katsumi's name. He drew a line beside it.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

**Makoto Kajiwara**

One. Unknown. He might have known his father-in-law's habits.

Two. No key. His name was not in the Tono Construction log either. He could borrow Chizuru's key, but if he borrowed it, Chizuru would know.

Three. Unknown.

Under Kajiwara's name Kamiya wrote a question mark. Then he crossed it out.

This was a man who tried to solve things with money. Men of that kind do not use their hands. They ask someone else, or do it on paper. However hard he tried, Kamiya could not picture him going to an empty house on a Sunday to take down a handrail himself.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

At the fifth, Kamiya's pen stopped.

Four names were written in the notebook so far. Ryosuke, Misaki, Katsumi, Kajiwara. Three crossed out, one with a line beside it.

He did not write the fifth name.

On February 27, in the Tono Construction car park, he had done the same thing. He had thought Katsumi was protecting someone, and had not gone so far as to write down who. If he wrote it, the fact that he had thought it would remain.

That caution had brought him this far.

Leaving the name blank, he wrote the three again beneath it.

One.

Kamiya turned back through the notebook to the page for February 15. His first day in Ugano. In a house where the funeral had only just ended, Chizuru had said something as she served tea, and he had written it down that same day.

　　*Father always went to the old house on the eleventh.*

*Always*, it said.

Two. She had one. Nagasawa had said so. *The elder daughter has one. She used it to let herself in.*

Three.

Kamiya brought up the photograph of the Tono Construction log. He had taken it on March 10, when he picked the notebook up off the floor and put it on the desk.

　　2/8　　Tono (main house)　　Chizuru Tono　　2/8　　[seal]

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He set the handwriting side by side.

The envelope that had been in the old house's letterbox. *Main house — front door*.

The side of the cardboard box in the entry. *Altar room — small items*.

The February 8 line in the Tono Construction log. *Chizuru Tono*.

The same hand. Rounded, with short flicks, pausing once at the end of each stroke. Not written in a hurry. The shape that settles into the body of someone who has written the same characters many times.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Then Kamiya set about breaking his own conclusion.

That was how he had done it with loan proposals. The more he wanted a case approved, the harder he looked for the holes himself. If he looked and found none, he approved it. If he found one, he rewrote it. Human judgement goes most wrong after a conclusion has been reached, when one sets about defending it.

He listed three holes.

One. Was Chizuru really the only one who knew the habit? Soichiro himself might have told someone. But he had not told the company, or his brother, or his son. This was a man who said what he had decided and never gave reasons. It was hard to imagine him telling anyone that he climbed a dark staircase on his wife's monthly memorial day.

Two. There might be other keys. Copies can be cut. But to cut one you have to borrow the original. And if you borrow it, the lender knows.

Three. The biggest hole. **Taking down a handrail is not the same as killing someone.**

There might be some other reason it had been taken down. Taken down because it was dangerous. Because it had worked loose. Taken down as part of the clearing before demolition. Each held together. They held together — but whichever it was, it was strange not to say a word to her father afterwards.

*Dad, I've taken that handrail down, so be careful.*

That one sentence had not been said.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya closed the notebook.

Then he opened it again and looked at the three lines.

One, two, three.

And at the empty space above them.

If he did not write it, the fact that he had thought it would not remain. If it did not remain, tomorrow he could think something else. Tomorrow the crushed rims of the four holes and the placing of the crate might look like mere chance. A morning might come when he could think so.

For thirty years he had waited for mornings like that.

While he waited, a man had died.

Kamiya picked up his pen and, in the space he had left, wrote two words.

Only one person in this town met all three.


# Chapter Twenty-Three: I Didn't Do Anything

At three in the afternoon on March 17, Kamiya unlocked the front door of the old house and waited inside.

The electricity had been cut off the month before. The western sun came through the lattice and laid thin stripes across the earthen floor. In the corner of the entry stood four half-packed cardboard boxes. Cushions brought down from the altar room, teacups, and the paulownia box holding Sawako Tono's memorial tablet. Chizuru had set them all in a row. On the side of one, in marker pen: *Altar room — small items*. A careful hand.

The staircase was straight ahead.

Seventy-five centimetres wide. A wall on one side. On the other, nothing. It turned once at the fourth step from the bottom, and beyond that it was too dark to see. In the wall, four screw holes. Evenly spaced, at waist height, in a straight line.

At five past three, he heard a car.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Sorry to keep you. The handover ran on."

Chizuru still had her coat on over her care-home uniform. She took a bundle of papers from her bag and set it on top of a box. He had asked her here on the pretext of getting her seal on the inventory of movables.

"It's cold in here."

"The electricity's off."

"So it is. I had it cut off, didn't I."

Chizuru laughed. After she laughed, she looked up the stairs, once. Then she did not look again.

Kamiya handed her the inventory. She went through it from the top, put a finger on two items and said, "These were my mother's." She worked fast. Fourteen years in the office of a care home had taught her how to read a document. She stamped it and handed it back.

"Anything else?"

"There are three things I'd like to ask you."

Chizuru looked at him, one hand still resting on the pile of cushions.

"The first. How many people knew that your father came up to the first floor here on the evening of the eleventh of every month?"

"…How many? Me, my brother, Mrs Sagara, my uncle, I suppose."

"Ryosuke-san mistook the monthly memorial day for the anniversary. Mrs Sagara didn't know it was the eleventh. Your uncle didn't know he went up in the evening."

Chizuru said nothing.

"The second. Who has a key to this house?"

"I do."

"The third. In Tono Construction's key log there's a line for February 8. It was your handwriting."

The wind rattled the lattice. Chizuru pulled her coat closed across the front.

"I came to clear things. To bring the altar-room things down a little at a time."

"Which of these did you bring down on the 8th?"

Chizuru looked at the boxes. All four had the date they were sealed written on them. February 26, March 2, March 2, March 9. None for the 8th.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya opened the door of the shed.

The handrail was there. The rounded wooden pole, the brackets still on both ends, leaning against the wall. A crate pushed up beside it so it would not fall.

"When I found this, I thought it was a demolition man's work."

Chizuru stood at the entrance of the shed and did not come in.

"No tradesman stands something he's taken off a house that's coming down like this. He leaves it, or ties it in a bundle and puts it outside. Pushing a box up beside it so it won't fall over —"

Kamiya stopped there.

"That's how someone who puts things away does it."

Chizuru did not move. The western light cut a square on the floor of the shed.

"All four screw heads are stripped. With an impact driver, that wouldn't happen. Someone took them out slowly, with a household drill, without raising the torque."

"You know a lot about it."

"When I was at the bank, I looked at hundreds of buildings held as security."

For the first time, Chizuru did not smile.

"It's the handrail of a house that's being pulled down anyway."

Her voice was steady.

"The demolition was decided. The estimate had been done in February. It was attached to a wall that was going to be knocked down, in a house that was going to be knocked down. Is taking it off some sort of crime?"

"Your father came up here on the eleventh."

"My father was still strong on his legs."

"It's a dark staircase."

"If he thought it was dangerous, he didn't have to go up."

Kamiya knew that way of talking. It was how a branch manager talked when he wanted a proposal through. Take each point on its own, and none of it is wrong.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Shirasagi Development's deposit was fifteen million, wasn't it."

Chizuru's shoulders moved, very slightly.

"You're a joint guarantor on your husband's company's loan. Twenty-three million. Both fall due at the end of March."

"My husband's business isn't something I —"

"Your seal is on the guarantee."

Chizuru stepped back from the shed door into the entry. She put a hand on the corner of a box.

"On the evening of February 9, what did your father tell you?"

There was a long pause. The light through the lattice was slowly getting lower.

"…Ten years, he said."

Chizuru said it looking at the box.

"That the land would be put in a form no one could touch for ten years. That he'd make it into something called a trust. That was how my father put things. He never gave reasons. Only what he'd decided."

"And then?"

"And then that was it. Ten years. In ten years I'll be fifty-two. The children will have finished university. My husband's company will have gone under long before. When it does, I'll pay. Over twenty years, or thirty."

"Did you tell your father that?"

"I couldn't."

"Why?"

"If I had, he'd have paid."

It took Kamiya a moment to understand what the answer meant.

"…You didn't want your father to pay?"

"I started it."

Chizuru raised her head. Her eyes were dry.

"I put my seal on the guarantee for my husband's company. I didn't consult anyone. I didn't, because I thought they'd stop me. So I was the one who was supposed to end it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"There's one thing I should tell you."

Kamiya took a copy of the notarial will from the inside pocket of his jacket. He did not open the cover; he showed her only the date.

"It was drawn up on February 10."

Chizuru looked at the copy. For a few seconds.

"…The 10th."

"The handrail came off on the 8th. Your father told you on the evening of the 9th, went to the notary's office on the morning of the 10th, and made this. There are two witnesses. On the evening of the 10th he posted a letter to me."

Chizuru's mouth opened a little.

"So I was too late?"

That was what she said.

Kamiya would remember those words for a long time. The first thing she had checked was not why her father died, nor what was in the will. It was whether she had been in time.

"You were too late," Kamiya said. "And on the 20th, the land became twenty million. The fifteen million can't be paid back."

Chizuru gripped the corner of the box. Her knuckles were white.

"I'm not asking why you did it," Kamiya said. "What I'm asking about is your uncle."

Chizuru's hand went still.

"Your uncle remembers you asking him, early in February, how much it would cost to take the handrail off. So when he saw it was gone, he assumed one of his young men had done it. He told the police it had been taken off because the house was being demolished. For that one sentence, he's in custody now. Today is the eighth day."

"…My uncle hasn't said anything?"

"Not a word."

Chizuru closed her eyes. Then she opened them.

"I never asked him to do anything."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya did up one button of his coat. It was not because of the cold.

"I've lost twenty-eight million."

Chizuru looked up.

"Last year. An overseas firm that claimed an AI would invest it automatically. Most of my retirement money. My wife thinks it's in a fixed deposit. I haven't been able to tell her yet."

"…Why are you telling me this?"

"Last month, after I took this job on, I opened the estate account. Money that moves on the executor's seal, the passbook, and my judgement alone. I thought, if I put it back within three months, nobody would know. I did the sums on the calculator three times."

Chizuru did not move.

"On the fourth, I stopped. The reason I stopped was nothing to be proud of. I was frightened. That's what I want to say to you. I'm not someone who can judge you. I went to the same place. I stopped and you didn't. That's the only difference."

"I did stop."

Chizuru said it.

"I didn't do anything. I only took a handrail off. I didn't push my father down the stairs, I didn't leave anything on the steps. That day, I wasn't here. I was at home with the children. Whether my father went up or not was for my father to decide."

"You knew he'd go up on the eleventh."

"I knew. Does that mean I did something?"

Kamiya did not answer. He had no answer. Tachibana, he supposed, could explain it in three lines of legal language. All Kamiya had was a single line in blue ballpoint. *There is one more — I could not divide it.* He still did not know what it meant.

"It was you who found him, on the 13th," Kamiya said. "You didn't come to check on him the night of the 11th, or on the 12th."

"I phoned on the 12th. He didn't answer."

"And so you waited until the morning of the 13th."

Chizuru did not answer.

"How could you wait two days?"

The silence was long. Outside, somebody's washing pole rattled in the wind.

"…I was frightened."

"Of what?"

Chizuru looked towards the stairs. For the second time that day.

"Of what I'd do if it hadn't happened."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya put the envelope of photographs and the copy of the key log back in his bag.

"Tomorrow I'll take these to Ugano police station."

"I see."

"I wanted to tell you before I did."

"I see."

Chizuru squared the papers on top of the box and put them in her bag. The stamped inventory was neatly folded in two. What she had brought down from the altar room was divided among four boxes, each with its date. *She divides everything*, Kamiya thought. She divides it, sets it in order, and writes her name on it.

At the sliding front door, with her hand on it, Chizuru stopped.

"Kamiya-san."

"Yes."

"Do you think I killed him?"

The stripes on the earthen floor had climbed all the way to the west wall.

"Yes."

Chizuru did not turn round.

"I didn't do anything."

The door slid half open and the light outside came in.

"I just didn't do anything about it."


# Chapter Twenty-Four: February 10

At nine in the morning on March 18, on the second floor of Ugano police station, Kamiya laid four envelopes side by side on the desk.

Twelve photographs of the screw holes. Five of the handrail standing in the shed. A copy of the Tono Construction key log. And the conversation at the old house on the evening of the 17th, which he had written out when he got home. Six sheets of A4.

Nagasawa went through them in order. Partway, he began turning the pages more slowly.

"Chizuru Tono said this herself?"

"She did."

"Recorded?"

"No."

Nagasawa read to the end and put it back in the envelope. Then he tapped the edge of the desk once with a finger.

"This is awkward."

"Awkward?"

"Yes. Awkward."

Nagasawa pushed his chair back and turned to face Kamiya.

"The letter you brought us in the first place. The one Soichiro Tono wrote. *If I died falling down the stairs, it was not an accident.* Doesn't that strike you as odd?"

"…"

"It's written as if he'd already decided where he was going to fall. That house has only one staircase. The handrail was off by the 8th. In other words, Soichiro Tono may have known there was no handrail."

Outside the window, a patrol car could be heard pulling into the garage.

"If he knew and went up anyway," Nagasawa said, "then perhaps he wanted it to happen. And if so, the link between taking the handrail off and the death is broken."

"It isn't broken. If she hadn't taken it off, he wouldn't have fallen."

"In court it isn't that simple."

Nagasawa gave the envelopes a light push back towards Kamiya.

"These papers of yours point at Chizuru Tono, and at the same time they could make the whole case disappear. Do you see? If I take this upstairs now, the line that goes through most easily is Katsumi Tono's release and a review on the basis of suicide."

Kamiya looked at the envelopes. What he had spent a month gathering lay on the desk as four bundles of paper.

"Bring me one answer," Nagasawa said. "Why did Soichiro Tono go up those stairs?"

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Soichiro's own house was in the town centre of Ugano. A small single-storey house he had built in 2005, separate from the old family home.

Misaki unlocked it. She had been four times in the past month to make the inventory of movables. Half the drawers of the study desk were still untouched.

The diary was in the second drawer. A thin shop-bought one that ran by the fiscal year.

The pages up to January were packed. Hospital appointments, memorial services, company visitors, the tax accountant, the barber. Small, precise writing.

In the space for February 9, only *Chizuru*.

In the space for February 10, two lines.

　　Notary　10:00
　　Post office

In the space for February 11, a single character. In pencil: 土 — *earth*.

The pages after that were white. The 12th, the 13th, the 14th, white. As he turned them with a finger, white pages went on and on.

He stopped at March 31.

　　The three

The deadline for the envelopes was the forty-ninth day, Tachibana had said. That fell on March 31.

The will said only *by the forty-ninth day*. When the forty-ninth day would be could not be known until the man himself had died.

And yet the space for March 31 already had *The three* written in it.

Counting from February 11, it was the forty-ninth day.

Kamiya looked at those two words for a long time.

"The chairman," Misaki said, "always wrote down what was coming well ahead of time."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

That afternoon he met Mitsuko Tachibana.

The call record had come from the itemised bill for Soichiro's mobile. February 9, from 21:04, seventeen minutes. Not to the Ugano Central Law Office, but to Tachibana's mobile.

"My private number," Tachibana said. "I'd given it to Mr Tono."

"What did you talk about?"

"He wanted to make the will the next day."

"On the night of the 9th — the next day?"

"Yes. A notary office won't act the same day. But Mr Tono and I had been working on the draft since last autumn. The frame of dividing it in three, the way of choosing, the ten-year trust — all of that was done last year. All it needed was a date."

Tachibana reached for her teacup, and did not drink.

"So the next day was possible. We got an appointment for ten o'clock on the 10th, and I arranged two witnesses."

"Did he give a reason?"

"He didn't. I didn't ask."

Kamiya waited. Tachibana lowered her eyes once.

"But he did ask me one thing."

"What?"

"*Does a will only take effect after the one who wrote it has died?*"

The air in the room seemed to grow a little heavier.

"What did you say?"

"Yes, I said. While you're alive you can rewrite it whenever you like. Only when you die does it become impossible to change."

Tachibana lifted the cup, and this time she drank.

"At the time I thought I was just explaining the procedure."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the evening Kamiya went to the old house.

He set out so as to arrive at 6:40 — the time Soichiro had fallen. Six-forty in March is far lighter than in February. Even so, inside the house it was dark.

He stood in the entry. From the fourth step up, the stairs were in shadow. A wall on one side. On the other, nothing.

He went to climb, put a foot on the step, and stopped.

That was when he remembered.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The dead man's effects had been returned to the family by the police. Kamiya had looked through the bag once, for the inventory.

He phoned Misaki and asked her to get the bag out. Inside were a wallet, reading glasses, the house keys, a mobile phone, and —

A torch.

Black plastic, with a thick grip. The kind of battery torch that costs less than two thousand yen at a home centre. Kamiya pressed the switch. It did not come on. When he took out the batteries, the two D cells were drained almost to the point of leaking.

The switch was still in the ON position.

"Where was this?"

"Next to the body," Misaki said. "Lying there, I was told. Since it was an accident, it's only in the corner of a photograph. There's hardly anything about it in the report."

Kamiya read the stamp on the side of the batteries. The recommended use-by date was five years off. They had been new.

Then he went back to Soichiro's study and took out every bundle of receipts in the drawer. Held with rubber bands, divided by month. The February bundle was thin.

It was the fourth one.

　　Eiwa Home Centre, Ugano Store　February 9, 2026　16:32
　　Torch (LED, takes 2 × D)　¥1,480
　　Alkaline batteries, D, pack of 2　¥698

The 9th.

The day he told Chizuru *ten years*. The day he phoned Tachibana. That afternoon, he had bought a torch.

Kamiya put new batteries in the torch and went back to the old house.

It was past eight. An hour and a half later than the time Soichiro fell. Darker by that much. He stood in the entry and switched it on. A ring of light fell round the third step.

He climbed.

One step. Two. Three. His left hand against the wall, his right lighting his feet. The risers were high. His knees did not come up as far as he expected. If it was like this with sixty-seven-year-old knees, at seventy-eight it would be worse.

At the fourth step, the stairs turned. At the turn the wall ended, and his left hand closed on air.

Then he understood. On stairs with a handrail, at the turn you shift your grip along the rail. Someone climbing with a hand on the wall is, at the turn, for a moment holding on to nothing.

Kamiya put his foot on the fifth step and stood there for about ten seconds. *If you fell, it would be here*, he thought. There was no better place to be recorded as an accident.

Coming down was more frightening. Going down, the light cannot reach ahead of your feet.

Back in the entry, he switched off the torch. His palms were sweating.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

There, the order fell into place.

On the 8th, the handrail comes off. On the 9th, Soichiro notices. He notices, and he does not call the police. He does not tell the builder. He does not have it put back.

Instead, he does three things.

He tells his daughter the land will be put in a form that cannot be touched for ten years.

He phones his lawyer and says he will make the will the next day.

He buys a torch at the home centre.

On the 10th, he makes the will at the notary's office, goes straight on to the post office, and sends the ledger and the letter to Takasaki.

On the 11th, carrying the torch, he climbs those stairs.

Kamiya sat down on the earthen floor. It was cold.

It was not suicide. People who mean to die do not buy lights. He meant to go up, and he thought he could. If there was no handrail, you lit your way. A seventy-eight-year-old man had thought that.

And he fell.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

The next day, Kamiya took the receipt and the photographs of the torch to Ugano police station. Nagasawa looked at them without speaking, listened as far as the state of the batteries, and said, "I'll have forensics pull the photographs from the day."

"And the suicide line?"

"Gone. As far as I'm concerned, at least."

Nagasawa squared the papers.

"But, Kamiya-san. Bringing a charge won't be easy. There's still no bridge between taking the handrail down and intent to kill. She'll keep saying *it belonged to a house that was being pulled down*. To be honest, there's a chance that will stand."

"I know."

"You knew, and you brought it anyway?"

"Yes."

Nagasawa looked at Kamiya for a moment, then put the envelopes away in a drawer.

Outside the station, in the car park, Kamiya got into his car and for some time did not move.

When Soichiro Tono found on the 9th that the handrail was gone, he could have called the police that same day. He could have confronted his daughter. He could have called the builder and had it put back.

He did none of those things.

Instead of accusing his daughter, he wrote a will.


# Chapter Twenty-Five: Next of Kin

On March 19, after handing the copy of the receipt to Nagasawa, Kamiya went down to the ground floor of the same building.

Across the acrylic screen in the visiting room of the police cells, Katsumi Tono's face had changed in less than ten days. It was not that his cheeks had fallen in; the colour had gone out of it. The weathering of a builder who stood on site every day seemed to have simply come off.

"Kamiya-san."

"Yes."

"Are my lads all right?"

"The work's going on. I heard your wife's sister has come in to do the office."

Katsumi said, "I see," and smiled a little.

There were only fifteen minutes. Kamiya skipped the preliminaries.

"Early in February, did Chizuru-san ask you anything?"

The smile went out of Katsumi's face.

"…The 3rd, it was."

"What did she ask?"

"How much it would cost to take the handrail off."

Katsumi folded his hands on his side of the screen.

"She came to the office — it was by the way, when she brought round a photograph of my mother. When the old house comes down, how much should come off beforehand to make it cheaper. She's like that, that girl. Looks into everything ahead of time."

"And what did you say?"

"I said we'd take a handrail off for her, no bother. Wouldn't charge her even ten thousand."

Katsumi stopped there for a moment.

"So when I saw it was gone, I thought one of mine had done it. Because I'd said so — they'd done it off their own bat. So I told the police it had been taken off because of the demolition. I didn't think I was lying."

"When did you find out it wasn't so?"

"End of February. There's only six of us. I asked every one of them, one at a time. None of them had done it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the other side of the screen, Katsumi was silent for a long time.

"You could have corrected it," Kamiya said. "At the end of February you could have told the police. *It wasn't one of my people.* Then you wouldn't be here."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Katsumi raised his head.

"My wife was bedridden for six years."

Kamiya waited.

"She collapsed in 2019 and died last autumn. Our daughter's in Tokyo; she does well to come home twice a year. I was out on site. If I'm not out, no money comes in."

"Chizuru-san —"

"Came four days a week. Six years."

Katsumi's voice was flat. Neither resentment nor pride — the way you would read out a record.

"I didn't do anything. I didn't give her a yen. When that girl came, my wife smiled. She wouldn't smile when I talked to her, but when that girl came, she smiled. That's all."

"And so you kept quiet."

"I thought if I kept quiet, that would be the end of it."

Then, for the first time, Katsumi lowered his eyes.

"I'm sorry."

Time was up. As he stood, Katsumi asked one thing.

"Is she going to take the land?"

"I don't know."

"She will," Katsumi said. "That's the sort of girl she is."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Outside the station, in the car, Kamiya opened his notebook.

For a month, as executor, he had seen every one of Soichiro Tono's papers. That was what making an inventory of an estate meant. Savings, shares, insurance, registrations, contracts, bills. A person's life, measured in paper, came to six cardboard boxes.

In particular spaces on that paper, the same name kept appearing.

Kamiya wrote out as many as he could remember.

　　Soichiro's hospital consent form　　　Family　　　　　　Chizuru Tono
　　Sawako's care facility　　　　　　　 Guarantor of person　Chizuru Tono
　　Katsumi's wife's care facility　　　 Emergency contact　Chizuru Tono
　　Soichiro's family doctor　　　　　　 Contact　　　　　 Chizuru Tono
　　Tono Precision social insurance　　　Correspondence to　Chizuru Tono
　　Cemetery administrator　　　　　　　Registered holder　Chizuru Tono
　　Spare key to the old house　　　　　Held by　　　　　 Chizuru Tono
　　Both children's schools　　　　　　 Emergency contact　Chizuru Tono
　　Kajiwara Financial Planning loan　　Joint guarantor　　Chizuru Tono

There were nine. He must have forgotten some, so in fact there were more.

Of the nine pieces of paper, eight were the kind used when someone grows weak, or is gone. The other one was the kind used when someone borrows money.

Kamiya read the list three times.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

In the afternoon he called in at Tono Precision.

Misaki brought an old file out of the cabinet at the back of the office. On the spine: *Clients — rationalisation*. The date on the cover was August 2019.

"The chairman made this himself."

Inside was a list of clients and how each one's share of sales had changed over time. In the 2019 column, the largest client accounted for 38 per cent of sales. Beneath it, one line in red ballpoint.

　　　Bring this down to 20% in three years.

"It didn't take three years," Misaki said. "In fact it took four. He didn't cut clients, he added them. He took on cheaper work too. The works got busier and the margin went down."

"Why would he do that?"

"I asked him once. He said, *If one goes, everything goes.*"

Misaki closed the file.

"I took it for granted he meant clients."

Kamiya looked again at the date on the cover. August 2019.

Sawako Tono had died on June 11, 2019.

Two months after his wife died, this man had set about changing the fact that his company depended on one client.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the main road home, Kamiya kept thinking the same thing.

He had been misreading the line in the statement of wishes all along.

　　*To anyone who staked it all on one thing, leave not a single yen.*

When he first read it, the one he had pictured was Ryosuke. The eldest son trying to pay off his debts with crypto. Anyone would read it that way. That was why, he had thought, Soichiro had made all three of them read it.

He had been wrong.

What the man had been looking at was not a son who staked his money on one thing.

Her mother's care. Her aunt's care. Her father's hospital visits. The key to her father's house. The children's schools. Her husband's company. Her husband's debt. Her own seal, stamped in place of her husband's. Nine pieces of paper, nine spaces. All filled with the same name. Nobody had decided it should be so. Each time a space was empty, she had filled it, that was all.

The one most at risk is not the person who has put all their assets in one place.

It is the person who has had everyone's *could you just* put in one place. The first chose it. The second does not even remember choosing.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

That night, in the sitting room of his own house, Kamiya took the papers out of the drawer.

Insurance policies. Pension notices. The neighbourhood association list. The medical questionnaire tucked in with his card for the internist. The consent form from when he had a stomach polyp removed three years ago.

He went through them one by one, looking at the space for family and the space for emergency contact.

Kayoko Kamiya. Kayoko Kamiya. Kayoko Kamiya.

Every one. His daughter's name was nowhere. *Because she's in Tokyo*, he had told himself. In fact he had simply never once hesitated when he wrote it. In the empty space he had written the name of the person nearest to him. He had been doing it for decades.

Kayoko came out of the bath and said, "What are you doing?"

"Tidying."

"That's a first."

Kamiya put the papers back. As he did so, for the first time he thought concretely about who would come if she collapsed. The answer was: only himself.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Soichiro Tono must have looked at the same kind of paper. It was he himself who had written his daughter's name in the family space on the hospital consent form, in the guarantor space for the care facility. The one who filled the empty spaces was the one who asked.

That was why he had divided it in three.

If one person holds everything, that one person carries everything. Divide it in three, and three people each carry a third. It is inefficient. It is more trouble. Unless the three of them talk, nothing gets decided.

Diversification is not dividing up assets. It is not letting one person carry everything.


# Chapter Twenty-Six: The Fourth Envelope

On March 31 it rained from the morning.

The forty-ninth-day service began at ten, at the temple in Ugano. Eight people came. Ryosuke; Chizuru and her two children; Misaki; the works manager from Tono Precision; the temple's lay steward; and Kamiya.

Katsumi Tono's seat was empty.

Makoto Kajiwara did not come either. Shirasagi Development was demanding the return of its deposit, and the week before, Kamiya had heard from Tachibana, it had begun preparing a criminal complaint. Chizuru said not a word about it.

During the sutras, Kamiya watched Chizuru's back in the front row. In her mourning clothes, her back was straight. When it was their turn to offer incense, she sent both children up first and went last herself.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

After the service they moved to the reception room of Soichiro's house. Tachibana had not come to the temple; she was waiting there.

From her black document case she took three white envelopes and laid them in a row on the low table. All three had been handed in before the deadline. Ryosuke on March 24, Misaki on the 26th, Chizuru on the 28th.

"I'll open them in the order they were received."

That was what Tachibana said. No one objected. The children were in the next room with the works manager.

The first. Ryosuke Tono.

　　Cash

Tachibana read it out. Ryosuke sat with his hands folded on his knees, looking straight ahead.

The second. Misaki Sagara.

　　The business

Misaki lowered her eyes. It was the answer everyone had expected. Since Soichiro's death, she was the one who had kept the works running.

The third. Chizuru Tono.

Tachibana's finger stopped once, just as it was about to slit the envelope. Then she opened it.

　　The land

The room went quiet. The sound of the rain was suddenly loud.

"All three of you have written something different," Tachibana said. "There is no overlap. Accordingly, no donation to the foundation arises. As the will provides, it is divided in three."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"Wait."

Ryosuke put his hand flat on the table.

"You're seriously saying this? You're giving the land to her?"

Chizuru did not move.

"Ryosuke-san," Kamiya said. "Nothing has been decided yet. It's under investigation."

"Decided or not, you're the one who took it to the police, aren't you? My uncle didn't do anything. She's the one who took the handrail off. Isn't that right?"

He could not answer.

"It's written in Dad's will, isn't it?" Ryosuke pulled the copy of the statement of wishes towards him. "*To anyone who staked it all on one thing, leave not a single yen.* That's it, isn't it? Dad's written, don't leave her a single yen."

Tachibana's hands stopped squaring the papers.

"Ryosuke-san. That line has no legal effect."

Ryosuke looked up.

"It's a statement of wishes. It's written inside the will, but the only parts that have legal force are how the property is divided, the appointment of the executor, and the provisions on the trust. The statement of wishes is where one writes one's feelings."

"Feelings?"

"Yes. Even though it's written there, it has no power to reduce anyone's share."

Ryosuke looked at the copy, then at Tachibana, then at the copy again.

"What has force is Article Five." Tachibana turned to another page. "Where one person acquires two or more, that person's beneficial interest is extinguished. That is a provision. That alone moves anything."

"Then that line —"

Ryosuke's voice dropped a little.

"What did he write it for?"

Kamiya held the rim of his teacup down with a finger.

"For you to read."

They all looked at him.

"That line wasn't written to cut anyone's share. It was written for the three of you to read. He put it in the one place each of you was bound to read, once, before you decided what to write in your envelope."

Kamiya set the copy of the will in the middle of the table.

"A line with no legal effect, written where it would show most. I think that's the kind of man he was."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Tachibana explained the figures on the land side.

For inheritance tax, the land would be valued at thirty-nine million. Land the market said was worth twenty million, the tax office would count as thirty-nine. Chizuru's inheritance tax would be about seven million.

"No cash comes with it," Tachibana said. "For ten years the trust prohibits disposing of the land or pledging it. You can neither sell it to pay nor borrow against it to pay. We'll apply to pay in instalments. If that's granted, it'll be paid yearly."

"And if it isn't?" Chizuru asked. It was the first time she had spoken that day.

"Then we'll think about it together, you and I."

Tachibana went on. Demolishing the three empty houses, about four and a half million in all. A soil survey of the old factory site, two million as a deposit. Without the survey there would be no buyer; with it, if anything was found, the figures would gain a digit.

"You will be holding it for ten years," Tachibana said. "Holding it, and paying all the while."

Chizuru said, "Yes."

That was all.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

"There is one more."

From the bottom of her case, Tachibana took out a fourth envelope.

An envelope of heavy Japanese paper. On the front, in Soichiro's hand, one word.

　　Forecast

No — not only one word. Beneath *Forecast*, small, was written *Do not open*, and through *Do not open* a double line had been drawn afterwards.

"I received it last November," Tachibana said. "I was told to open it after the three had finished choosing."

She slit it open.

Inside, a single sheet of letter paper. At the top, three lines.

　　Ryosuke　　Cash
　　Misaki　　 The business
　　Chizuru　　The land

No one made a sound.

Ryosuke reached for the sheet and stopped halfway.

"…He got it right."

"All three," Tachibana said.

Below, there was a little more. Something like reasons, one line for each.

　　Ryosuke　　Will take the lightest. He only ever holds what he can hold.
　　Misaki　　 Will take the most troublesome. She is someone who chooses the work.

At the third line, Kamiya's finger stopped.

　　Chizuru　　Will try to carry the heaviest. She always has.

The rain had grown heavier. The paper screens on the garden side had warped a little in the damp.

Chizuru did not look at the letter. With a fingertip she turned over the white envelope on the table with her name on it. Then she turned it face up again and put it back in the same place.

Kamiya remembered the law office on February 20. She had done the same thing then, with her teacup.

"Father knew," Misaki said quietly, "that the three of us would choose different things."

"I suppose he did," Tachibana said.

*No*, Kamiya thought.

It was not that he divided it in three because he knew. It was the other way round. Because he knew these were three people who would choose different things, he had judged that dividing it in three would not break. Only people who can divide can be handed something divided. In a family where one person wants everything, this will would have fallen apart on the first day.

But he had also known there was a child who would try to carry the heaviest. Knowing that, he had written that of the three, that child would take this one.

He had written it last November. Three months before the handrail was taken down.

As the letter was going back into its envelope, the sliding door opened a little.

It was Chizuru's younger child. Ten or eleven, by the look of it. Holding the bag of gifts handed out after the service.

"Mum, aren't you finished?"

"Nearly."

When Chizuru answered, the child began to slide the door closed, and looked at Kamiya.

"Are you the man for Grandad's land?"

"…That's my job, yes."

"What's going to happen to it?"

"For ten years, it'll stay as it is."

"Ten years."

The child started counting on fingers, and stopped.

"So it'll still be there when I'm grown up."

The door closed.

Chizuru was looking at her lap. Kamiya worked out in his head the year that child would turn twenty, and there he stopped working it out. In ten years the land might still be unsold. Even if it sold, no one could say whether it would reach twenty million. All that was certain was that until then there would be one person paying the tax, the grass-cutting and the demolition.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

They broke up a little after two.

Ryosuke left first, and Misaki went back to the works with the works manager. Chizuru put the children in the car, then turned to Kamiya at the front door.

"Kamiya-san."

"Yes."

"Father thought I'd write *the land*, didn't he."

"That's what it said."

Chizuru held her umbrella without opening it.

"Why do you think that was?"

Kamiya remembered the third line of the letter. The way it said *She always has.*

He had an answer. He had one, but he did not think he was the one who should say it.

"I don't know," he said.

Chizuru said, "I see," and went out into the rain. She did not open the umbrella until she was in the car.


# Chapter Twenty-Seven: Fixed Deposit

At eleven in the morning on April 3, Katsumi Tono walked out of the Maebashi branch of the detention centre.

The charge had been withdrawn. Whether the prosecutor had explained why, Katsumi did not say. Outside the gate a Tono Construction pickup was waiting, and a young tradesman got down from the driver's seat and bowed.

Katsumi was carrying one thin paper bag. Inside were the things he had handed over when he went in.

"Kamiya-san. You shouldn't have."

"I was nearby."

Katsumi looked up at the sky, then slapped the tailgate of the pickup once with his palm. *A habit*, Kamiya thought. The gesture of a man arriving on site.

"The girl —"

That was all Katsumi said.

"I see."

Katsumi nodded and asked nothing more. Before he got into the passenger seat, he turned towards Kamiya once.

"The forty-ninth day?"

"It's done."

"I see. I couldn't be there."

The pickup pulled away. All the way to the corner, its brake lights did not come on once.

Nagasawa's call came that evening.

"She's been coming in since April 2. Voluntarily. She has a lawyer."

"She is. But on intent, she keeps saying the same thing she said to you on the 17th."

"It will take time. Six months, a year. Where it lands, I can't tell you."

"Even if it doesn't, it stays on the record. Everything she says goes into a statement."

Kamiya thanked him and put the phone down. Whether this was right, he still did not know.

Makoto Kajiwara, asked by Shirasagi Development to return the fifteen-million deposit and unable to, had been reported for fraud. The Kanto Local Finance Bureau had also begun voluntary questioning about his registration as a financial instruments intermediary.

"Twenty-three million of the Kajiwara Financial Planning loan is still outstanding," Tachibana said. "The joint guarantor is Chizuru-san."

"Even with the land —"

"The land can't be sold for ten years. The loan repayments won't wait."

Kamiya moved the phone to his other hand.

"She's asked me about divorce," Tachibana went on. "But divorce doesn't make a joint guarantee disappear. She understands that herself."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

By the middle of April, most of the executor's work was done.

He delivered the inventory of the estate, transferred the titles, concluded the trust agreement and sent in the invoice for his fee. Tachibana numbered every document and bound them for him.

The first payment from the cash was made on April 15. Eight million yen. The two of them sat in the bank's reception room and waited while the transfer to Ryosuke's account went through.

"Ten years, eh."

Ryosuke said it.

"Ten years."

"I owe twenty million. At eight a year, it's paid off in the third year. What about the seven after that?"

"Seven years' worth will be left."

Ryosuke looked up at the ceiling.

"Dad thought I'd blow it all in one go."

"That's what it said."

*Will take the lightest. He only ever holds what he can hold.* Ryosuke had read that line on the forty-ninth day.

"Kamiya-san."

"Yes."

"What am I supposed to do, all that time?"

Kamiya thought a little before he answered.

"The same amount, on the same day, every month."

Ryosuke looked at him.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He took the papers for the transfer of the shares to Tono Precision.

Misaki was in the works office, stamping a bundle of slips. When Kamiya handed her the papers, she stopped, read them through to the end, and then stamped them.

"Is that the end of it?"

"All that's left is my invoice."

Misaki put the papers in an envelope and filed them away in the cabinet. Then she turned to him.

"I don't forgive you."

Kamiya, still standing, nodded.

"In 1996 you turned down the proposal. You found the figures my father made up. The figures my father was *made* to make up. The company survived, and my father died."

"Yes."

"I know you were right. I still don't forgive you."

"Yes."

Misaki stood with her hand on the cabinet handle and was silent for a while.

"But I understand why the chairman asked you."

"Because you can only ask a man who has lost money to watch money?"

"I don't think so."

Misaki shook her head.

"I think the chairman wanted to be forgiven. By asking you."

Out in the works, the press sounded twice.

"It's no joke for the one who gets asked," Misaki said. "That's the kind of man he was."

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He got home to Takasaki just before seven.

Kamiya set his shoes straight in the entrance, went into the living room and took a brown envelope out of his bag. In it were copies of his passbook, the remittance slips, and printouts of the emails from Arclight Capital. Thirty-one sheets in all.

Kayoko was in the kitchen. Kamiya put the envelope on the low table and sat down.

"I need to talk to you."

Kayoko came out wiping her hands and sat down opposite him.

Kamiya took the papers out and spread them on the table. In date order. After two months of making inventories, it was the only way he could lay things out any more.

"I've lost twenty-eight million."

His voice came out lower than he had expected.

"The retirement money. I sent it in April last year, and in October it couldn't be taken out. When I said it was in a fixed deposit, that was a lie. For a year I've been lying."

Kayoko looked at the papers. She did not pick them up one by one. She only moved her eyes, from the top down.

It was long enough to hear the clock.

"That envelope, in February last year."

That was what Kayoko said.

"It was from a securities firm, wasn't it. It wasn't company papers."

Kamiya could not answer.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"I couldn't."

"Nor could I."

Kayoko squared the edges of the papers.

"I didn't ask, either. I thought if I asked, you'd lie. I didn't want to make you lie, so I didn't ask."

Kayoko raised her head. Her eyes were red, but there were no tears.

"I should have asked, shouldn't I."

Kamiya folded his hands on his knees. He noticed that his folded hands were the same shape as Katsumi Tono's.

"Mai's eight million," Kayoko said. "That's out of the question."

"Out of the question."

"Then we'll tell her. She's coming on Sunday."

"I'll tell her."

"We'll tell her together."

Kayoko stood up, started back towards the kitchen, and turned round.

"Will you eat?"

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

What Soichiro Tono left was three hundred million yen.

No — it was three hundred million only while there was supposed to be a hundred million in cash. The cash actually left was seventy-two million; the difference, twenty-eight million, had vanished inside the overseas fund his daughter's husband had sold him.

Then the land went from a hundred and two million to twenty million, and the whole came to a hundred and ninety million.

A hundred and ten million had disappeared. Nobody stole it. Not the police, not the tax office, nobody will go after it. One part vanished inside a lie; the other part was only a rumour vanishing.

Even so, it is still divided in three.

The works are running. The jobs from twenty-eight clients are going round, and next month's wages will be paid to thirty-one people. The cash will go out over ten years, eight million a year. The third is land that will not move for ten years.

The child who tries to carry the heaviest is carrying it.


# Epilogue

On the last day of the May holidays, Kamiya went upstairs to the six-mat room.

He no longer had to invent a reason to go up. Even that much, he was not yet used to.

The ledger stood on end beside the box of tax returns. He had thrown away the newspaper it arrived in and wrapped it again in a furoshiki cloth.

He opened it on his knees.

The 土 page — *earth*. At the very bottom, the line in blue ballpoint.

　　February 10, 2026　I divided it in three　there is one more　I could not divide it

Above it, in ballpoint, Heisei 3.

　　Heisei 3　I held only land　I held nothing but land

Heisei 3 was 1991. Between it and Soichiro's line lay thirty-five years.

The letter had said he received it from someone seven years ago. 2019.

Whoever wrote in Heisei 3 had kept it for twenty-eight years and handed it to Soichiro in 2019. Or there had been one more person in between, who wrote nothing. Kamiya had no way of knowing which.

Nor could he know who it was who, in Heisei 3, had held nothing but land. The hand sloped up to the right and the strokes were thin. It was not Soichiro's.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

*2019*, Kamiya said under his breath.

The year Sawako Tono died. On June 11.

The newspaper it had been wrapped in was from June 2019. It had been kept in that same newspaper for nearly seven years.

In the month his wife died, this man had received it. Received it, wrapped it again, and left it as it was.

In August of the same year, he had set about spreading the company's clients across twenty-eight firms.

Kamiya set the three side by side in his head. Having done so, he thought the order might be the other way round. Had he divided things because he lost his wife, or because he received this book? Or had both been one and the same thing?

There was no longer anyone to ask.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

He turned the pages.

種, seed. 木, tree. 蜜, honey. 鍵, key. 手, hand. 水, water.

On every page were a few lines each, in the hands of different eras. Picking out only the years, Kamiya copied them into his notebook.

　　土　earth　 Showa 16　Showa 23　Showa 48　Heisei 3　2026
　　種　seed　　Showa 28
　　木　tree　　Heisei 20
　　蜜　honey　 Showa 39
　　鍵　key　　 Showa 30　Showa 60
　　手　hand　　Showa 58　Heisei 15
　　水　water　 —

When he had copied them out, he looked at them for some time.

Something did not line up, he felt. What did not line up, he could not tell. At the bank, a few times a year, he had had the same feeling looking down a row of figures, and when he did, it was usually a premise one step further back that was wrong.

On the 水 page there was not a single line. Only the one symbol.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

On the paper of the back endpaper there were pencil marks.

They had been rubbed out. When he tilted the page to the light, they were faintly still there. The seven symbols, written small, in a column. One of them had a circle round it.

　　蜜

*Honey.* It was Soichiro's hand. The same thin pencil line as the 土 in his diary.

Kamiya looked at the circle for a long time.

What he had been given was the story of 土. The story of a man who divided things in three, and of the one thing he could not divide. That was over. Whether it could be called over he did not know, but on paper at least, everything had been numbered and bound.

But the symbol Soichiro had circled was not 土.

Footsteps came along the corridor. Kayoko said "Tea" from outside the sliding door and set down a cup. She set it down and stayed standing there.

"What is that thing?"

She had asked him the same once before, in March, and he had not answered.

"Someone in Gunma sent it. Someone who's died."

"Can you read it?"

"The Japanese parts."

Kayoko knelt and looked at the open page.

"There are no amounts in it."

"Not one."

"And it's a ledger."

"And it's a ledger."

Kayoko laughed a little, said, "Odd sort of person, him too," and went downstairs.

*Him too*, she had said.

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　※

Kamiya took a fountain pen from the desk drawer.

He opened the 土 page and brought the nib close, below Soichiro's line.

He knew what he would write. *Lost twenty-eight million in eight months.* He remembered the date, the amount, the name of the firm. One line would do.

He did not write it.

*Not yet*, he thought. He could not explain why. He felt that if he wrote it, something would be over.

He closed the ledger and wrapped it again in the cloth.

Then he took it out once more and opened the 蜜 page.

The most recent entry was from Showa 39. In the sixty years and more since, nobody had written anything there.

Which meant that the person whose turn it was to write was still alive, somewhere.
